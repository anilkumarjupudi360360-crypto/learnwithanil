# Paper II Reasoning & Mental Ability (Q1-50) and Mathematics (Q51-100), OPSC Assistant Section Officer (Odisha Secretariat) written exam of 27 August 2022, Advt. No. 26 of 2021-22, Series A (OPSC's own booklet); answers: OPSC's answer key published with the result (9 January 2024)

Q 1 | rea | Verbal Reasoning
Select the correct option that indicates the arrangement of the given words in the order in which they appear in a telephone directory :
(I) Krishanmurty
(II) Krishnamurthy
(III) Krishnmurthi
(IV) Krishanmurthy
(V) Krishnamurti
(1) (IV), (I), (II), (V), (III)
(2) (IV), (I), (II), (III), (V)
(3) (IV), (V), (II), (III), (I)
(4) (IV), (III), (V), (III), (II)
= 1
~ The order is (IV), (I), (II), (V), (III). A telephone directory lists names letter by letter, as a dictionary does. All five names begin with 'Krish', so compare the 6th letter: (I) and (IV) have 'a' (Krisha…), while (II), (III) and (V) have 'n' (Krishn…); so (I) and (IV) come first. Between Krishanmurthy (IV) and Krishanmurty (I), the first eleven letters 'Krishanmurt' match; the 12th letter is 'h' in (IV) and 'y' in (I), and h comes before y, so (IV) leads. Among the 'Krishn…' names, look at the 7th letter: (II) and (V) have 'a', but (III) has 'm', so (III) goes last. Between Krishnamurthy (II) and Krishnamurti (V), the 12th letter is 'h' against 'i', so (II) comes before (V). Final order: IV, I, II, V, III. Option B wrongly puts (III) before (V), option C misplaces (V) and (I), and option D even repeats (III). Tip: move from left to right and decide at the first letter that differs; ignore everything after it.

Q 2 | rea | Verbal Reasoning
In a certain code language, ‘VIRTUE’ is coded as ‘201’ and ‘TRAGEDY’ is coded as ‘218’. How will ‘PROFANE’ be coded in that language ?
(1) 570
(2) 342
(3) 432
(4) 456
= 2
~ PROFANE is coded as 342. The rule: add the reverse alphabet positions of the letters (A = 26, B = 25 … Z = 1) and multiply the total by the number of vowels in the word. Check VIRTUE: V 5 + I 18 + R 9 + T 7 + U 6 + E 22 = 67; vowels I, U, E = 3; 67 × 3 = 201. Check TRAGEDY: T 7 + R 9 + A 26 + G 20 + E 22 + D 23 + Y 2 = 109; vowels A, E = 2; 109 × 2 = 218. Now PROFANE: P 11 + R 9 + O 12 + F 21 + A 26 + N 13 + E 22 = 114; vowels O, A, E = 3; 114 × 3 = 342. Options 570 and 456 use the wrong vowel count (114 × 5, 114 × 4), and 432 is not a multiple of 114. Shortcut: reverse position = 27 − normal position, so for PROFANE the reverse total = 27 × 7 − 75 = 114.

Q 3 | rea | Non-verbal Reasoning
Directions (Q. Nos. 3 to 5) :
One hundred and twenty five cubes of the same size are arranged in the form of a cube on a table. Then a column of five cubes is removed from each of the four corners. All the exposed faces of the rest of the solid (except the face touching the table) are coloured red. Now, answer these questions based on the above statement :
How many small cubes are there in the solid after the removal of the columns ?
(1) 120
(2) 110
(3) 105
(4) 100
= 3
~ 105 cubes remain. Step 1: 125 cubes arranged as a cube means 5 × 5 × 5, since 5³ = 125, so each edge has 5 cubes and there are 5 layers. Step 2: a column at a corner is the vertical stack standing on one corner of the base; it is 5 cubes tall, one cube in each layer. Step 3: there are 4 vertical corner columns, so cubes removed = 4 × 5 = 20. Step 4: cubes left = 125 − 20 = 105. Layer check: each layer had 5 × 5 = 25 cubes; taking away its 4 corner cubes leaves 21, and 21 × 5 layers = 105. Why not the others: 120, 110 and 100 would mean 5, 15 or 25 cubes removed, not 20. This solid is the base for the next two questions: every layer is now a 5 × 5 square with its corners cut off, and only the top and the four sides are painted, not the base on the table. Tip: draw one layer as a 5 × 5 grid, cross out the corners, then multiply by the number of layers.

Q 4 | rea | Non-verbal Reasoning
Directions (Q. Nos. 3 to 5) :
One hundred and twenty five cubes of the same size are arranged in the form of a cube on a table. Then a column of five cubes is removed from each of the four corners. All the exposed faces of the rest of the solid (except the face touching the table) are coloured red. Now, answer these questions based on the above statement :
How many cubes do not have any coloured face ?
(1) 12
(2) 24
(3) 36
(4) 48
= 3
~ 36 cubes have no coloured face. A cube stays unpainted only if none of its faces is exposed, apart from a bottom face resting on the table, which is not coloured. Step 1: look at one layer. After the corners go, each 5 × 5 layer has 21 cubes: 12 on the outer border (3 on each side) and an inner 3 × 3 block of 9. Step 2: every border cube shows at least one side face, so all are painted. Removing the corner columns exposes faces only of the border cubes next to the corners; the inner 3 × 3 block stays hidden, because its corner cube touches a removed corner only diagonally, not face to face. Step 3: the top layer is painted on top, so its inner 9 are coloured. Step 4: each of the lower four layers keeps 9 unpainted cubes, including the bottom layer, whose under-faces rest on the table. Total = 9 × 4 = 36. Quick formula when the base is unpainted: (n − 2)² × (n − 1) = 3² × 4 = 36. The options 12, 24 and 48 come from miscounting the layers or the inner block.

Q 5 | rea | Non-verbal Reasoning
Directions (Q. Nos. 3 to 5) :
One hundred and twenty five cubes of the same size are arranged in the form of a cube on a table. Then a column of five cubes is removed from each of the four corners. All the exposed faces of the rest of the solid (except the face touching the table) are coloured red. Now, answer these questions based on the above statement :
How many cubes have only one red face ?
(1) 40
(2) 25
(3) 20
(4) 15
= 2
~ 25 cubes have exactly one red face. Count layer by layer. Top layer: of its 21 cubes, the inner 3 × 3 block (9 cubes) shows only its top face, so these 9 have one red face; the 12 border cubes show the top and at least one side, so they have two or more. Lower four layers: each has 12 border cubes, 3 on each side. On every side the two end cubes stand next to a removed corner column, so they show their outer side face and the newly exposed face, which makes two red faces. Only the middle cube of each side shows a single face, giving 4 per layer. The bottom layer behaves the same way, because its under-faces touch the table and stay unpainted. So 4 × 4 layers = 16. Total = 9 + 16 = 25. The other options (40, 20, 15) come from counting all border cubes as one-face cubes or from leaving out the top block or some layers. Tip: removing a corner adds an exposed face to both cubes beside it, so on a side of 3 remaining cubes only 3 − 2 = 1 stays single-faced.

Q 6 | rea | Verbal Reasoning
David gets on the elevator at the 11th floor of a building and rides up at the rate of 57 floors per minute. At the same time, Albert gets on an elevator at the 51st floor of the same building and rides down at the rate of 63 floors per minute. If they continue travelling at these rates, then at which floor will their paths cross ?
(1) 19
(2) 28
(3) 30
(4) 37
= 3
~ Their paths cross at the 30th floor. Step 1: the gap between them = 51 − 11 = 40 floors. Step 2: they move towards each other, David going up and Albert coming down, so the gap closes at the sum of their speeds: 57 + 63 = 120 floors per minute (relative speed). Step 3: time to meet = gap ÷ relative speed = 40 ÷ 120 = 1/3 minute, that is 20 seconds. Step 4: in 1/3 minute David rises 57 × 1/3 = 19 floors, so he reaches 11 + 19 = 30. Check with Albert: he drops 63 × 1/3 = 21 floors, and 51 − 21 = 30. Both give 30, so the paths cross at the 30th floor. Shortcut: the meeting point splits the 40-floor gap in the ratio of the speeds, 57 : 63 = 19 : 21, so David covers 19 of the 40 floors. Trap: 19 is the number of floors David climbs, not the floor where they meet; 28 and 37 do not pass either check. The same relative-speed idea solves questions on two trains or two people moving towards each other.

Q 7 | rea | Verbal Reasoning
An egg vendor calls on his first customer and sells half his eggs and half an egg. To the second customer, he sells half of what he has left with and half an egg, and to the third customer, he sells half of what he was then left with and half an egg. However, he did not break any egg. If in the end, the vendor was left with three eggs. How many eggs did he have initially ?
(1) 26
(2) 31
(3) 39
(4) None of these
= 2
~ He started with 31 eggs. Step 1: if he has x eggs and sells half of them plus half an egg, he sells x/2 + 1/2 and is left with x − (x/2 + 1/2) = (x − 1)/2. Step 2: so, working backwards, eggs before a sale = 2 × (eggs left) + 1. Step 3: after the third customer he had 3 eggs, so before that sale he had 2 × 3 + 1 = 7. Before the second sale: 2 × 7 + 1 = 15. At the start: 2 × 15 + 1 = 31. Forward check: from 31 he sells 15.5 + 0.5 = 16, leaving 15; then 7.5 + 0.5 = 8, leaving 7; then 3.5 + 0.5 = 4, leaving 3. Every sale is a whole number, so no egg is broken. Why not the others: 26 would need a first sale of 13.5 eggs (a broken egg), and 39 ends with 4, not 3. Shortcut: for n such sales, start = (end + 1) × 2ⁿ − 1 = (3 + 1) × 2³ − 1 = 32 − 1 = 31.

Q 9 | rea | Non-verbal Reasoning
Choose the box that is similar to the box formed from the given sheet of paper :
(1) 1, 2 and 3
(2) 1, 2 and 4
(3) 2 and 3
(4) 2, 3 and 4
= 4
~ Boxes 2, 3 and 4 can be formed. With the centre square as the base, the four flaps rise as the side faces and the four end-tabs fold over to close the open face as a 2 × 2 block. Read from the sheet, that block has the left flap's half-disc at top left, the circle at top right, the cross at bottom left and the right flap's half-disc at bottom right; both half-discs point the same way, as the two tabs are drawn alike. The only marked flap, the right one with the small square, lies along the edge shared by the circle and the bottom-right half-disc. Box 3 shows exactly this: the 2 × 2 face in front, a plain top and the square on the right. Box 2 is the same box given a quarter-turn (cross top left, circle bottom right), so the square face is hidden. Box 4 shows three plain faces, possible because the base and two plain flaps meet at a corner. Box 1 copies box 3's front but puts the square on top, where the plain top flap belongs, and its half-discs point opposite ways. Tip: place the marked face first.

Q 10 | rea | Verbal Reasoning
Select the option in which the numbers are related in the same way as are the numbers of the following set, (24, 10, 392) :
(1) (29, 18, 242)
(2) (27, 15, 480)
(3) (26, 12, 369)
(4) (21, 18, 234)
= 1
~ (29, 18, 242) follows the same rule as (24, 10, 392). Rule: third number = 2 × (first − second)². Check the given set: 24 − 10 = 14; 14² = 196; 2 × 196 = 392. Option A: 29 − 18 = 11; 11² = 121; 2 × 121 = 242, which matches. Option B: 27 − 15 = 12; 12² = 144; 2 × 144 = 288, not 480. Option C: 26 − 12 = 14; 2 × 196 = 392, not 369. Option D: 21 − 18 = 3; 3² = 9; 2 × 9 = 18, not 234. So only A fits. How to find such a rule: when the third number is much larger than the first two, try products, squares or cubes of their sum or difference. Here 392 = 2 × 196, and 196 = 14², where 14 is the difference 24 − 10; that points straight to the rule. Quick tests that fail here: the product 24 × 10 = 240 and the square of the sum (24 + 10)² = 1,156.

Q 11 | rea | Verbal Reasoning
There are deer and peacocks in a zoo. By counting heads they are 80. The number of their legs is 200. How many peacocks are there ?
(1) 20
(2) 30
(3) 50
(4) 60
= 4
~ There are 60 peacocks. Each animal has one head, so deer + peacocks = 80; a deer has 4 legs and a peacock 2. Method 1 (assume all are one kind): if all 80 were peacocks, legs = 80 × 2 = 160. The real count is 200, so 200 − 160 = 40 legs are extra. Each deer adds 4 − 2 = 2 extra legs, so deer = 40 ÷ 2 = 20 and peacocks = 80 − 20 = 60. Method 2 (equations): let deer = d and peacocks = p. Then d + p = 80 and 4d + 2p = 200. Halve the second: 2d + p = 100. Subtract the first: d = 20, so p = 60. Check: 20 × 4 + 60 × 2 = 80 + 120 = 200 legs, and 20 + 60 = 80 heads. Why not the others: 20 is the number of deer, a common trap; 30 peacocks would give 260 legs and 50 peacocks 220 legs. Shortcut: four-legged animals = (legs − 2 × heads) ÷ 2 = (200 − 160) ÷ 2 = 20. The same method solves the classic hens-and-cows puzzles.

Q 12 | rea | Verbal Reasoning
What day of the week was 31st January, 2007 ?
(1) Tuesday
(2) Monday
(3) Thursday
(4) Wednesday
= 4
~ 31 January 2007 was a Wednesday. Method 1, from a known date: 1 January 2007 was a Monday. 31 January is 30 days later, and 30 = 4 weeks + 2 days, so the day moves 2 steps forward: Monday → Tuesday → Wednesday. Method 2, odd days: 2000 years give 0 odd days, because every 400 years make a whole number of weeks. The years 2001 to 2006 have 5 ordinary years (1 odd day each) and 1 leap year, 2004 (2 odd days), so 5 + 2 = 7 odd days = 0. January 2007 has 31 days = 4 weeks + 3, so 3 odd days. Total = 0 + 0 + 3 = 3, and odd-day number 3 means Wednesday (0 = Sunday, 1 = Monday, 2 = Tuesday, 3 = Wednesday). Why not the others: Monday is 1 January itself, Tuesday would need 2 odd days in all, and Thursday 4. Tip: 1 January 2001 and 1 January 2007 were both Mondays, a handy check. Also, 2007 is not a leap year, so its calendar repeats in 2018.

Q 13 | rea | Verbal Reasoning
Arrange the following words in the order in which they appear in an English dictionary :
(I) Meticulous
(II) Metric
(III) Method
(IV) Mettle
(V) Meter
(1) (V), (III), (I), (IV), (II)
(2) (V), (III), (I), (II), (IV)
(3) (III), (IV), (V), (I), (II)
(4) (V), (I), (III), (II), (IV)
= 2
~ The dictionary order is Meter, Method, Meticulous, Metric, Mettle, that is (V), (III), (I), (II), (IV). All five words begin with 'Met', so the first three letters decide nothing; compare the 4th letter instead. Meter has 'e', Method has 'h', Meticulous has 'i', Metric has 'r' and Mettle has 't'. In the alphabet e comes before h, h before i, i before r and r before t, so the order follows directly. Why not the others: option A puts Mettle (t) before Metric (r); option C starts with Method, but Meter (e) must come first; and option D puts Meticulous (i) before Method (h). Method of solving: write the words one below the other, find the first column where the letters differ, and arrange by that letter; if two words still tie, move to the next column. A quick memory line for this set, e-h-i-r-t: 'Every Hen Is Really Tiny'. The same steps apply to telephone directory questions, where names are arranged letter by letter in the same way.

Q 14 | rea | Verbal Reasoning
Rasik walked 20m towards north. Then he turned right and walks 30m. Then he turns right and walks 35m. Then he turns left and walks 15m. Finally he turns left and walks 15m. In which direction and how many metres is he from the starting position ?
(1) 15m West
(2) 30m East
(3) 30m West
(4) 45m East
= 4
~ Rasik ends 45 m East of his starting point. Trace each move. He walks 20 m North. Turning right from North means facing East: 30 m East. Turning right from East means facing South: 35 m South. Turning left from South means facing East: 15 m East. Turning left from East means facing North: 15 m North. Now add the movements by direction. North–South: 20 m North − 35 m South + 15 m North = 0, so he is back on his starting east–west line. East–West: 30 m + 15 m = 45 m East, with no westward move. So he is 45 m due East. Why not the others: 15 m West and 30 m West are impossible because he never walks West at all, and 30 m East forgets the second eastward stretch of 15 m. Tip: when facing North, right = East and left = West; when facing South, right = West and left = East. Draw a quick sketch, mark every turn, and add north–south and east–west distances separately; if both are non-zero, use Pythagoras for the straight-line distance.

Q 15 | rea | Verbal Reasoning
How is ‘sure’ written in a code language ?
I. ‘he is sure’ written as ‘ja ha main in that code language
II. ‘is she sure’ written as ‘Ka ja main in that code language
(1) Ja
(2) Ja or ma
(3) Ma
(4) Ha
= 2
~ 'Sure' is coded either 'ja' or 'ma'; the two statements cannot separate them. Read the codes carefully: 'he is sure' = 'ja ha ma' and 'is she sure' = 'ka ja ma' (the printed 'main' is just 'ma' followed by the word 'in'). Step 1: find the words common to both sentences: 'is' and 'sure'. Step 2: find the codes common to both: 'ja' and 'ma'. So the pair {is, sure} matches the pair {ja, ma}, but nothing tells us which code goes with which word. Step 3: the leftover words take the leftover codes: 'he' = 'ha' and 'she' = 'ka'. Why not the others: choosing only 'ja' or only 'ma' assumes a link the data do not give, and 'ha' is the code for 'he'. A third sentence containing 'sure' without 'is' would settle it. Tip: in word-code questions, common words map to common codes; when two words share two common codes, the answer is 'either of the two'. Codes are usually jumbled, so never match words to codes by their position in the sentence.

Q 16 | rea | Verbal Reasoning
Pointing to a woman, Nirmal said, “She is the daughter of my wife’s grandmother’s only child”. How is the woman related to Nirmal if she is not the wife of Nirmal ?
(1) Wife
(2) Sister-in-law
(3) Sister
(4) None of these
= 2
~ The woman is Nirmal's sister-in-law. Work from the wife's side, one step at a time. Step 1: the wife's grandmother's only child must be one of the wife's parents (her mother or her father), because the grandmother has just one child and the wife descends from her. Step 2: a daughter of that parent is either the wife herself or the wife's sister. Step 3: the question says the woman is not Nirmal's wife, so she must be his wife's sister. A wife's sister is a sister-in-law. Why not the others: 'Wife' is ruled out by the question itself; 'Sister' would need the woman to be a child of Nirmal's own parents, but this chain runs entirely through his wife's family; and 'None of these' is wrong because sister-in-law fits exactly. Tip: in blood-relation puzzles, start from the far end of the sentence (here, 'wife's grandmother') and walk back towards the speaker, writing each link down. 'Only child' is the key clue: it pins that person down as the wife's parent, with no uncles or aunts to consider.

Q 17 | rea | Verbal Reasoning
IF ZIP = 198 and ZAP = 246, then how will you code VIP ?
(1) 174
(2) 222
(3) 888
(4) 990
= 1
~ VIP is coded 174 as per OPSC's final key. Rule used by the key: code = 6 × (first letter + last letter − middle letter), with A = 1, B = 2 … Z = 26. Check ZIP: Z 26 + P 16 − I 9 = 33, and 6 × 33 = 198. Check ZAP: Z 26 + P 16 − A 1 = 41, and 6 × 41 = 246. Now VIP: V 22 + P 16 − I 9 = 29, and 6 × 29 = 174. The first clue is that both given codes are multiples of 6. Another rule also fits the same two examples: 6 × (sum of reverse positions + 3). ZIP: 1 + 18 + 11 + 3 = 33; ZAP: 1 + 26 + 11 + 3 = 41; VIP: 5 + 18 + 11 + 3 = 37, and 6 × 37 = 222. That is why many books print 222 (option B), but the site follows OPSC's key. Options 888 and 990 fit neither rule. Tip: with only two examples, more than one rule can fit, so test every option quickly. (Official key; some sources differ.)

Q 18 | rea | Verbal Reasoning
Directions (Q. Nos. 18 to 20) : Read the information given below to answer these questions :
Rani and Shreshtha are a married couple having two daughters, Medha and Deepti. Deepti is married to Anurag who is the son of Garima and Tarun. Nidhi is the daughter of Anurag. Komal, who is Anurag’s sister, is married to Harshit and has two sons, Aman and Prem. Prem is the grandson of Garima and Tarun.
What is the relationship between Aman and Nidhi ?
(1) Cousins
(2) Husband-Wife
(3) Father-Daughter
(4) Uncle-Niece
= 1
~ Aman and Nidhi are cousins. Build the family tree first. Generation 1: Rani and Shreshtha (parents of Medha and Deepti), and Garima and Tarun (parents of Anurag and Komal). Generation 2: Deepti marries Anurag; Komal, Anurag's sister, marries Harshit. Generation 3: Nidhi is the daughter of Anurag and Deepti; Aman and Prem are the sons of Komal and Harshit. Now link the two: Aman's mother Komal and Nidhi's father Anurag are sister and brother, so their children are first cousins. The line 'Prem is the grandson of Garima and Tarun' confirms that Komal is Garima and Tarun's own daughter. Why not the others: Aman and Nidhi are in the same generation, so they cannot be father and daughter or uncle and niece; and nothing says they are married, so 'husband and wife' is wrong. Tip: in long family puzzles, draw three rows for three generations, use '=' for a married couple and a vertical line for children, and mark males and females; then each question is a quick look at the diagram.

Q 19 | rea | Verbal Reasoning
Directions (Q. Nos. 18 to 20) : Read the information given below to answer these questions :
Rani and Shreshtha are a married couple having two daughters, Medha and Deepti. Deepti is married to Anurag who is the son of Garima and Tarun. Nidhi is the daughter of Anurag. Komal, who is Anurag’s sister, is married to Harshit and has two sons, Aman and Prem. Prem is the grandson of Garima and Tarun.
How is Komal related to Deepti ?
(1) Aunt
(2) Sister-in-law
(3) Sister
(4) None of these
= 2
~ Komal is Deepti's sister-in-law. Deepti is married to Anurag, and Komal is Anurag's sister, so Komal is Deepti's husband's sister. A husband's sister is a sister-in-law (called 'nanad' in Hindi). Steps: (1) Deepti and Anurag are a married couple. (2) Anurag and Komal are brother and sister, both children of Garima and Tarun. (3) So Komal is related to Deepti through marriage, not by blood. Why not the others: 'Aunt' would need Komal to be a sister of one of Deepti's parents (Rani or Shreshtha), which she is not; Komal is the aunt of Nidhi, not of Deepti. 'Sister' is Medha's relation to Deepti, since both are daughters of Rani and Shreshtha. 'None of these' is wrong because sister-in-law fits exactly. Note the reverse relation too: Deepti is Komal's sister-in-law as well, being her brother's wife. Tip: the English word sister-in-law covers both a spouse's sister and a brother's wife, so read the chain to see which case applies; the answer word is the same either way.

Q 20 | rea | Verbal Reasoning
Directions (Q. Nos. 18 to 20) : Read the information given below to answer these questions :
Rani and Shreshtha are a married couple having two daughters, Medha and Deepti. Deepti is married to Anurag who is the son of Garima and Tarun. Nidhi is the daughter of Anurag. Komal, who is Anurag’s sister, is married to Harshit and has two sons, Aman and Prem. Prem is the grandson of Garima and Tarun.
Which of the following is true ?
(1) Tarun is Deepti’s maternal uncle
(2) Aman is the son of Medha
(3) Garima is Harshit’s mother-in-law
(4) Nidhi is cousin of Komal
= 3
~ 'Garima is Harshit's mother-in-law' is the true statement. Steps: Garima and Tarun are Anurag's parents; Komal is Anurag's sister, and her son Prem is called the grandson of Garima and Tarun, which confirms that Komal is their daughter. Komal is married to Harshit, so Komal's mother Garima is Harshit's mother-in-law, and Tarun his father-in-law. Now test the other statements. A: Tarun is Anurag's father and Deepti is Anurag's wife, so Tarun is Deepti's father-in-law, not her maternal uncle (a mother's brother). B: Aman is the son of Komal and Harshit, not of Medha; Medha is Deepti's sister, and the passage gives her no children. D: Nidhi is the daughter of Komal's brother Anurag, so Nidhi is Komal's niece, not her cousin; Nidhi's cousins are Aman and Prem. Tip: in 'which is true' questions, test each option against the family tree instead of trying to prove one directly; the wrong ones usually fail at the first link. Mother-in-law means the mother of one's husband or wife.

Q 21 | rea | Verbal Reasoning
In a cetain code, MOTHER is written as ONHURF. How will ANSWER be written in that code ?
(1) NBXSSE
(2) NBWRRF
(3) MAVSPE
(4) NBWTRF
= 4
~ ANSWER is written as NBWTRF. Step 1: find the rule from MOTHER → ONHURF. Split the word into pairs: MO, TH, ER. Swap the two letters of each pair: OM, HT, RE. Then move the second letter of each swapped pair one step forward in the alphabet: OM → ON (M + 1 = N), HT → HU (T + 1 = U), RE → RF (E + 1 = F). Result: ON HU RF = ONHURF, which matches. Step 2: apply it to ANSWER: pairs AN, SW, ER; swap to NA, WS, RE; move each second letter one step forward: NA → NB, WS → WT, RE → RF. So the code is NBWTRF. Why not the others: NBWRRF has R in the 4th place instead of T (it moves S back, not forward), and NBXSSE and MAVSPE change letters in ways that do not follow the rule. Tip: when the code looks like the word's own letters jumbled, check pairs first and compare position by position to see which letters shift by +1 or −1. Here ER → RF appears in both words, which confirms the rule.

Q 22 | rea | Verbal Reasoning
Find the odd one out :
(1) Platform
(2) Dock
(3) Bus-stand
(4) Park
= 4
~ Park is the odd one out. A platform, a dock and a bus-stand are all places where a vehicle stops so that passengers or goods can get on and off: trains at a railway platform, ships at a dock, and buses at a bus-stand. A park is an open green space for recreation; no vehicle halts there to load or unload people. So the common link of the other three is 'stopping place for transport', and the park does not share it. Watch the trap: 'park' can also mean a car park, or the verb to park a vehicle, but in a list like this the plain noun means a garden or public ground, not a halt for public transport. Related words exams like: hangar (shelter for aircraft), harbour or port (ships), depot (buses and goods) and terminus (last stop). Odisha example: Paradip Port has docks for ships, Bhubaneswar railway station has platforms for trains, and Baramunda is the city's main bus stand. Tip: in odd-one-out questions, name the common link in one phrase and then test each word against it.

Q 23 | rea | Verbal Reasoning
Find the odd one out :
(1) Lion
(2) Tiger
(3) Fox
(4) Deer
= 4
~ Deer is the odd one out. Lion, tiger and fox are carnivores: they hunt other animals and eat flesh, and all three belong to the mammal order Carnivora (the lion and tiger to the cat family Felidae, the fox to the dog family Canidae). The deer is a herbivore that grazes on grass and browses on leaves; it belongs to the hoofed mammals, family Cervidae, and is in fact a common prey of tigers and lions. So the common link is 'flesh-eating predator', and the deer breaks it. Trap: don't pick the fox just because it is smaller or because it also eats fruit and insects; it is still a predator and a member of Carnivora. Indian examples: in Similipal Tiger Reserve in Odisha, sambar, spotted deer and barking deer form a large part of the tiger's prey; the Gir forests and nearby Saurashtra in Gujarat are the only wild home of the Asiatic lion; and the Indian fox lives in dry grasslands. Tip: in animal odd-one-out questions, look at food habit, family, habitat or number of legs, and choose the link that fits three words exactly.

(2) 13
(3) 14
(4) 17
= 3

Q 29 | rea | Verbal Reasoning
Nurture : Neglect :: Denigrate : ?
(1) Reveal
(2) Extol
(3) Recognise
(4) CalumInate
= 2
~ Extol completes the pair. Nurture means to care for something and help it grow, and neglect means to fail to care for it, so the first pair are opposites (antonyms). Denigrate means to criticise unfairly or belittle someone, to run them down. Its opposite is extol, which means to praise highly and with enthusiasm. So Nurture : Neglect :: Denigrate : Extol. Why not the others: reveal (to make known) is the opposite of conceal, not of denigrate; recognise means to identify or acknowledge and is not a direct opposite; and calumniate (misspelt in the option) means to make false and damaging statements about someone, so it is a synonym of denigrate and the strongest trap. Example sentences: 'The critic denigrated the film'; 'The teacher extolled the student's honesty'. Other antonyms of denigrate: praise, laud, commend, acclaim. Other synonyms: belittle, disparage, defame, vilify. Tip: in analogy questions, first name the relation in the first pair (here, opposites), then test each option with it; when the pair are antonyms, a synonym in the options is the usual trap.

(3) 25

Q 33 | rea | Verbal Reasoning

(2) Wool
(3) Jute
(4) Cotton
= 1
~ Linen completes the analogy. Latex is the milky sap tapped from the rubber tree (Hevea brasiliensis), and rubber is made from it, so the pair is 'raw material : product made from it'. (2) North-East
(3) South-West
(4) North-West
= 4

Q 37 | rea | Verbal Reasoning

(1) West
(2) North-West
(3) North
(4) South-West
= 4
~ He ends up facing South-West. Method 1, step by step: he starts facing West. Turning 45° clockwise from West takes him to North-West (clockwise runs North → East → South → West → North, so from West it moves towards North). Turning a further 180° clockwise from North-West makes him face the exact opposite, South-East. Turning 270° anticlockwise from South-East is the same as turning 90° clockwise, which takes South-East to South-West. (2) 9 : 15

Q 52 | math | Arithmetic

(1) 96
(2) 84
(3) 108
(4) 120
= 3
~ The answer is 108. A number divisible by both 6 and 12 must be a multiple of their LCM. Since 12 is itself a multiple of 6 (12 = 6 × 2), LCM(6, 12) = 12, so we need the smallest 3-digit multiple of 12. Step 1: the smallest 3-digit number is 100. Step 2: divide 100 by 12: 100 = 12 × 8 + 4, so the remainder is 4. Step 3: add (12 − 4) = 8 to 100 to reach the next multiple: 100 + 8 = 108. Check: 108 ÷ 12 = 9 and 108 ÷ 6 = 18, both exact. (2) 6
(3) 1/35
(4) 5/77

Q 55 | math | Algebra & Geometry

(2) 1066
(3) 2
(4) 4
= 4
~ The value is 4. Use the identity (a + b)² − (a − b)² = 4ab. Proof: (a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b² and (a − b)² = a² − 2ab + b²; on subtracting, a² and b² cancel, and 2ab − (−2ab) = 4ab. (2) 3/4
(3) 1/5
(4) 3/5
= 1

(2) 3
(3) −11
(4) −1
= 3

Q 73 | math | Algebra & Geometry
If a pair of linear equations is given by a₁x + b₁y + c₁ = 0 and a₂x + b₂y + c₂ = 0 where a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂ ≠ c₁/c₂ then :

(2) The pair of linear equation is inconsistent
(3) The pair of linear equation is independent
(4) The pair of linear equation is dependent
= 2
~ The pair is inconsistent. When a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂, the two lines have the same slope, so they are either parallel or the same line. The extra condition ≠ c₁/c₂ means they are not the same line, so they are parallel and never meet. No point satisfies both equations, and a system with no solution is called inconsistent. 'Consistent' is wrong because a consistent pair has at least one solution, which happens when a₁/a₂ ≠ b₁/b₂ (a unique solution, intersecting lines) or when all three ratios are equal (infinitely many solutions). 'Dependent' describes coincident lines, where a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂ = c₁/c₂ and one equation is a multiple of the other. 'Independent' is not the standard name for this case. Example: x + 2y = 3 and 2x + 4y = 10 give 1/2 = 2/4 ≠ 3/10, so the lines are parallel and the pair is inconsistent. Memory tip: intersecting → unique solution → consistent; coincident → infinite solutions → consistent and dependent; parallel → no solution → inconsistent.

(2) 88
(3) 89
(4) 100
= 3

(2) 27th
(3) 30th
(4) None of these
= 2
~ 164 is the 27th term. When the sum of the first n terms is given, the nth term is aₙ = Sₙ − Sₙ₋₁. Here Sₙ = 3n² + 5n, so Sₙ₋₁ = 3(n − 1)² + 5(n − 1) = 3n² − 6n + 3 + 5n − 5 = 3n² − n − 2. Q 83 | math | Algebra & Geometry

(1) 25
(2) 32
(3) 10
(4) None of these
= 2
~ The power set has 32 elements. The power set P(A) is the set of all subsets of A, including the empty set φ and A itself. (2) 4
(3) 6

= 4

(2) 3
(3) 4

Q 99 | math | Data Interpretation
Find out the algebraic sum of deviation of a set of P values from their mean :
(1) P − 1
(2) 0
(3) P
(4) P + 1
= 2
~ The algebraic sum of deviations from the mean is 0, whatever the number of values P. Proof: if the values are x₁, x₂, ..., xₚ with mean x̄, then x̄ = Σx ÷ P, so Σx = P × x̄. The sum of deviations is Σ(x − x̄) = Σx − P × x̄ = P × x̄ − P × x̄ = 0. 'Algebraic' sum means signs are kept: deviations above the mean are positive, those below are negative, and they cancel exactly. The options P − 1, P and P + 1 wrongly tie the answer to the number of values; the result depends neither on P nor on the data. Example: for 2, 5 and 8 the mean is 5 and the deviations are −3, 0 and +3, which add to 0. Related facts often asked: the sum of squared deviations is least about the mean; the sum of absolute deviations is least about the median; and if every value is increased by a constant c, the mean rises by c but the deviations do not change.

The first cumulative frequency to reach 50 is 60, so the median class is 20–30. Step 3: L = 20 (lower limit of the median class), cf = 24 (cumulative frequency of the class before it), f = 36 (frequency of the median class) and h = 10 (class width). Median = 20 + [(50 − 24) ÷ 36] × 10 = 20 + 260/36 = 20 + 7.22 = 27.22. The other options are traps: 24 is the cumulative frequency before the median class, 50 is N/2 (a position, not a value), and 36 is the frequency of the median class. Trap: use the cumulative frequency of the class BEFORE the median class, not of the median class itself. Quick sense check: the answer must lie inside the median class, between 20 and 30, and only 27.22 does.
