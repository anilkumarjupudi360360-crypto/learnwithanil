Q 1 | apt | Series & Puzzles
Find the missing number :
Triangle 1: sides 18, 15 | base 5 | inside 54
Triangle 2: sides 12, 10 | base 4 | inside 30
Triangle 3: sides 8, 9 | base 6 | inside ?
(1) 12
(2) 32
(3) 26
(4) 18
= 1
~ In each triangle, multiply the two numbers written on the slanting sides and divide the product by the number written below the base; the result is the number inside. First triangle: 18 × 15 = 270, and 270 ÷ 5 = 54. Second triangle: 12 × 10 = 120, and 120 ÷ 4 = 30. Both complete figures fit, so the rule is confirmed. Third triangle: 8 × 9 = 72, and 72 ÷ 6 = 12. Hence the missing number is 12, option 1. The other options, 32, 26 and 18, cannot be produced from 8, 9 and 6 by a rule that also gives 54 and 30 in the first two triangles; for example, plain addition gives 18 + 15 + 5 = 38, not 54, so it fails at the very first figure. Exam tip: in number-figure questions, always test a rule on the first two complete figures before applying it to the one with the question mark. The usual rules are (a × b) ÷ c, a × b − c, (a + b) × c and sums of squares, so try these first when time is short.

Q 2 | apt | Reasoning
June 29, 2026 is Monday. Then, which day is June 29, 2030 ?
(1) Sunday
(2) Saturday
(3) Thursday
(4) Monday
= 2
~ Count the odd days (days left over after complete weeks) between 29 June 2026 and 29 June 2030. A one-year gap of 365 days gives 1 odd day; a gap that contains 29 February gives 2 odd days. 2026 to 2027: 1 odd day. 2027 to 2028: this period contains 29 February 2028, so 2 odd days. 2028 to 2029: 1. 2029 to 2030: 1. Total = 1 + 2 + 1 + 1 = 5 odd days. Monday + 5 days = Saturday (Tuesday 1, Wednesday 2, Thursday 3, Friday 4, Saturday 5). So 29 June 2030 is a Saturday, option 2. Sunday would need 6 odd days and Thursday 3, while Monday would need 0 or 7, so options 1, 3 and 4 are wrong. A common slip is to forget the leap day and get 4 odd days (Friday). Tip: when the date falls after February, check whether 29 February of a leap year lies inside the period; for June dates, the extra day of 2028 is counted in the 2027–2028 step, not in the 2028–2029 step.

Q 3 | apt | Arithmetic
In a cricket match, the run rate was 4.5 in first 10 overs. To reach the target 325, what should be the run rate in remaining 40 overs ?
(1) 6
(2) 4.5
(3) 8
(4) 7
= 4
~ Runs scored in the first 10 overs = run rate × overs = 4.5 × 10 = 45. Runs still needed for the target = 325 − 45 = 280. Overs remaining = 40. Required run rate = 280 ÷ 40 = 7 runs per over, option 4. Check: 45 + 7 × 40 = 45 + 280 = 325. Option 2 (4.5) simply repeats the earlier run rate and would give only 45 + 180 = 225 runs. Option 1 (6) gives 45 + 240 = 285, which is 40 runs short. Option 3 (8) gives 45 + 320 = 365, more than needed. Dividing 325 by all 50 overs (6.5) is also wrong, because it ignores the runs already scored and the overs already used. Memory tip: run rate = runs ÷ overs, so runs = run rate × overs, and the required run rate is always (target − runs scored) ÷ overs left. The same 'remaining amount ÷ remaining time' idea solves average-speed and average-marks problems that often appear in police recruitment papers.

Q 4 | apt | Arithmetic
If √10 = 3.162, then the value of 1/√10 is
(1) 3.162
(2) 0.03162
(3) 0.3162
(4) 31.62
= 3
~ Simplify 1/√10 by rationalising the denominator, that is, multiply the numerator and the denominator by √10. 1/√10 = √10/(√10 × √10) = √10/10. Since √10 = 3.162, √10/10 = 3.162 ÷ 10 = 0.3162. So option 3 is correct. Option 1 (3.162) is √10 itself, not its reciprocal. Option 2 (0.03162) equals √10/100, one decimal place too small. Option 4 (31.62) equals 10√10 = √1000, which is far too large; the reciprocal of any number greater than 1 must be less than 1, so it can be rejected at a glance. Quick check: 3.162 × 0.3162 ≈ 1.000, which confirms that 0.3162 is the reciprocal of 3.162. Tip: dividing by 10 moves the decimal point one place to the left, and rationalising the denominator is the standard trick for every 1/√n question. Useful values to remember: 1/√2 = √2/2 ≈ 0.707 and 1/√3 = √3/3 ≈ 0.577.

Q 5 | apt | Arithmetic
The rate of sugar is increased by 25%. If a family wants to keep its expenditure the same as before, then by what percent sugar consumption has to be decreased ?
(1) 20%
(2) 75%
(3) 80%
(4) 25%
= 1
~ Expenditure = price × consumption. If the price rises by 25%, the new price is 125% of the old, that is 1.25 times. To keep expenditure unchanged, consumption must become 1/1.25 = 0.8 of the old, that is 80%. So consumption must fall by 100% − 80% = 20%, option 1. Formula: required reduction = r/(100 + r) × 100 = 25/125 × 100 = 20%. Example: if sugar cost ₹40 per kg and the family bought 10 kg (₹400), the new price is ₹50 per kg, and ₹400 now buys only 8 kg, a cut of 2 kg out of 10, i.e. 20%. Option 4 (25%) is the common trap: cutting consumption by the same percentage as the price rise lowers expenditure, because 1.25 × 0.75 = 0.9375, a 6.25% fall. Option 3 (80%) is the new consumption level, not the reduction, and 75% has no basis. Tip: when a price falls by r%, consumption can rise by r/(100 − r) × 100 percent to keep expenditure the same.

Q 6 | apt | Series & Puzzles
In the following question, a letter series is given. Some of the letters of the series are missing. Select the correct answer from the given options.
abc __ abc __ da __ cd __ d
(1) ddbd
(2) dddb
(3) bdbd
(4) bbdd
= 1
~ Fill the blanks with d, d, b, d and the series becomes abc d abc d d a b cd d d, which reads abcd | abcdd | abcddd. Every block starts with a, b, c and then carries one more d than the block before it: one d, two d's, three d's. So option 1 completes the series logically. Check the others: with dddb the string becomes abcd abcd dad cdb d, which breaks the a-b-c order; with bdbd it becomes abcb abcd dab cdd d, where the first block has no d at all; with bbdd it becomes abcb abcb dad cdd d, again breaking the pattern. Only ddbd makes every block begin with abc and grow by one d. Method: count the given letters plus the blanks (here 15 in all) and try to split them into repeating or growing groups such as 4 + 5 + 6. Tip: write each option into the blanks one by one instead of guessing; the correct answer makes the whole string fall into neat blocks, while a wrong one leaves an odd letter somewhere.

Q 7 | apt | Arithmetic
4 + 0.44 + 4.4 + 0.04 + 44 = ?
(1) 52.88
(2) 52.08
(3) 48.08
(4) 44.88
= 1
~ Add the whole numbers and the decimals separately. Whole numbers: 4 + 44 = 48. Decimals: 0.44 + 4.4 + 0.04 = 4.88; write them one below the other with the decimal points in a column (0.44, 4.40, 0.04): hundredths 4 + 0 + 4 = 8, tenths 4 + 4 + 0 = 8, units 0 + 4 + 0 = 4. Total = 48 + 4.88 = 52.88, option 1. Option 2 (52.08) is 0.8 short, the typical result of a slip in the tenths column. Option 3 (48.08) is 4.8 short of the correct sum, and option 4 (44.88) is exactly 8 short, as if the 4 and the whole-number part of 4.4 were forgotten. Tip: in decimal addition, pad every number with zeros to the same number of decimal places (4.00, 0.44, 4.40, 0.04, 44.00) and line up the decimal points before adding. This simple habit prevents most careless slips in the arithmetic section, where each wrong answer costs a quarter mark in KSP constable papers.

Q 8 | apt | Reasoning
Four different positions of the same dice are shown below. Find the number on the face opposite the face showing 4.
(1) 5
(2) 1
(3) 2
(4) 6
= 2
~ List the faces seen next to each number. Face 3 appears with 4 and 2 in (i), with 4 and 5 in (ii), with 5 and 1 in (iii) and with 2 and 1 in (iv). So 3 touches 1, 2, 4 and 5, and the only number left, 6, must be opposite 3. Now look at 4: it is seen beside 3 and 2 in (i) and beside 3 and 5 in (ii). Every face touches all faces except its own opposite; since 6 is opposite 3, 6 is also a neighbour of 4. So the neighbours of 4 are 2, 3, 5 and 6, and the remaining number, 1, is opposite 4: option 2. Options 1 (5) and 3 (2) are wrong because they appear next to 4 in positions (ii) and (i), and option 4 (6) is wrong because 6 is opposite 3. Complete pairs: 3–6, 4–1 and 2–5; no drawing shows an opposite pair together. Tip: start with the number that appears most often (here 3, in all four positions). Do not assume opposite faces add up to 7 unless the question says it is a standard dice; here 4 and 1 are opposite.

Q 9 | sci | Biology
Select the incorrect statement from the following :
(1) Female produce only one type of ovum with X-chromosome.
(2) Out of 23 pairs of chromosomes present in human, 22 pairs are exactly same in both male and female.
(3) The egg of the mother determines the sex of the child.
(4) 50% of total sperm produced carry X-chromosome and rest 50% have Y-chromosomes.
= 3
~ Statement 3 is incorrect, so option 3 is the answer: the sex of a child is decided by the father's sperm, not by the mother's egg. A woman has two X chromosomes (XX), so every ovum she produces carries an X; statement 1 is correct, and females are called homogametic. A man has XY, so half of his sperms carry X and half carry Y; statement 4 is correct, and males are heterogametic. If an X-bearing sperm fertilises the ovum, the zygote is XX and develops into a girl; if a Y-bearing sperm fertilises it, the zygote is XY and develops into a boy. Statement 2 is also correct: of the 23 pairs of human chromosomes, 22 pairs are autosomes, alike in males and females, while the 23rd pair is the sex chromosomes (XX in females, XY in males). Exam fact: this is why blaming a mother for the birth of a girl child is scientifically wrong, a message central to Beti Bachao Beti Padhao and to the PCPNDT Act (1994), which bans sex determination of the foetus.

Q 10 | apt | Reasoning
In certain coding language :
‘123’ means ‘Radha bright girl’
‘145’ means ‘Dark big girl’
‘637’ means ‘Ravi bright boy’
Then, ‘bright girl’ is coded as
(1) 31
(2) 14
(3) 23
(4) 13
= 1
~ Compare the coded sentences to find common words. 'Radha bright girl' (123) and 'Dark big girl' (145) share only the word 'girl' and only the digit 1, so girl = 1. 'Radha bright girl' (123) and 'Ravi bright boy' (637) share only 'bright' and only the digit 3, so bright = 3. Hence 'bright girl' is made of the digits 3 and 1; writing them in the order of the words, bright (3) then girl (1), gives 31, option 1, which is KEA's answer. Option 2 (14) is wrong because 4 stands for 'Dark' or 'big', and option 3 (23) is wrong because 2 stands for 'Radha'. The digits in these codes do not follow word order (in 123, 'girl' is the last word but its code 1 comes first), so option 4 (13), made of the same two digits, can also be argued; KEA's key accepts only 31, which follows the order of the words asked. Tip: decode by the common word – common digit method, and when two options contain the same digits, prefer the one in word order. (Official key; some sources differ.)

Q 11 | apt | Reasoning
In 12 hours format, if the time is 7:00 now, what time will it be in next 54 hours ?
(1) 3:00
(2) 6:00
(3) 5:00
(4) 1:00
= 4
~ A clock face repeats every 12 hours, so only the remainder after removing complete 12-hour cycles matters. 54 hours = 4 × 12 + 6, that is 48 hours (two full days, which bring the clock back to 7:00) plus 6 hours. 7:00 + 6 hours = 13:00 on the 24-hour clock, which is shown as 1:00 in the 12-hour format. So the answer is 1:00, option 4. If it is 7:00 a.m. now, it will be 1:00 p.m. two days later; if it is 7:00 p.m., it will be 1:00 a.m. three days later, but either way the clock reads 1:00. Option 2 (6:00) is just the leftover 6 hours, not the time; option 1 (3:00) and option 3 (5:00) come from wrong remainders (8 or 10 hours added). Shortcut: divide the hours by 12, keep the remainder, add it to the present time and subtract 12 if the total passes 12. The same remainder idea is used for days of the week (divide by 7) in calendar questions.

Q 12 | apt | Reasoning
If BY = 50, then GO + OD and GO – OD are given by
(1) 45 and 165 respectively
(2) 60 and 105 respectively
(3) 105 and 60 respectively
(4) 165 and 45 respectively
= 4
~ Find the rule from BY = 50. In the alphabet B is the 2nd letter and Y the 25th; 2 × 25 = 50, so a two-letter word is coded as the product of the position numbers of its letters (the sum 2 + 25 = 27 does not fit). Now G = 7, O = 15 and D = 4. GO = 7 × 15 = 105 and OD = 15 × 4 = 60. Therefore GO + OD = 105 + 60 = 165 and GO − OD = 105 − 60 = 45, so the answer is '165 and 45 respectively', option 4. Option 1 gives the same two numbers in the reverse order, so it fails the word 'respectively'. Option 3 (105 and 60) gives the values of GO and OD themselves, not their sum and difference, and option 2 reverses those as well. Memory tip for letter positions: EJOTY = 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, so O = 15 and Y = 25 come instantly; count forward or backward from the nearest of these. Always test whether the rule is sum, product or difference using the example before applying it.

Q 13 | apt | Data Interpretation
Directions : Consider the following Venn diagram and answer the questions 13 and 14.
In a class, students have opted different subjects as given in the Venn diagram :
The number of students who opted Geography but not Economics is
(1) 5
(2) 12
(3) 7
(4) 8
= 4
~ Read the seven regions of the Venn diagram: Economics only 6, Economics and History only 4, History only 4, Economics and Geography only 2, all three subjects 5, History and Geography only 3, and Geography only 5. 'Geography but not Economics' means every part of the Geography circle that lies outside the Economics circle. Those parts are Geography only (5) and Geography with History but without Economics (3). So the number of students = 5 + 3 = 8, option 4. Option 1 (5) counts only the students who took Geography alone and misses the 3 who also took History. Option 3 (7) is 2 + 5, the students who took Geography together with Economics, exactly the group to be excluded. Option 2 (12) wrongly mixes regions lying inside the Economics circle (5 + 2 + 5). Check: the whole Geography circle is 2 + 5 + 3 + 5 = 15, and removing the 7 who also took Economics leaves 15 − 7 = 8. Tip: for 'A but not B', shade circle A and erase everything that overlaps B, then add what remains.

Q 14 | apt | Data Interpretation
Directions : Consider the following Venn diagram and answer the questions 13 and 14.
In a class, students have opted different subjects as given in the Venn diagram :
How many of the students opted Economics and Geography but not History ?
(1) 7
(2) 3
(3) 2
(4) 5
= 3
~ We need the students who are inside both the Economics circle and the Geography circle but outside the History circle. In the Venn diagram, the Economics and Geography circles overlap in two parts: the small region shared by Economics and Geography alone, marked 2, and the central region shared by all three subjects, marked 5. The central 5 students also took History, so they must be left out. Hence the answer is 2, option 3. Option 1 (7 = 2 + 5) counts everyone who took both Economics and Geography, including those who also took History. Option 4 (5) is the centre region, students of all three subjects, exactly the group to be excluded. Option 2 (3) is the region of History and Geography without Economics, a different pair altogether. Useful structure: in a three-circle Venn diagram there are 3 'only one' regions, 3 'exactly two' regions and 1 'all three' region. 'X and Y but not Z' always means one of the three 'exactly two' regions, so read just that single number from the figure.

Q 15 | sci | Science & Technology
Which one of the following is not a constellation ?
(1) Callisto
(2) Leo major
(3) Orion
(4) Great bear
= 1
~ Callisto is not a constellation; it is a natural satellite (moon) of Jupiter, so option 1 is correct. It is one of the four Galilean moons discovered by Galileo Galilei in 1610 (Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto) and is the second-largest moon of Jupiter after Ganymede, with a heavily cratered surface. A constellation is a group of stars that forms a recognisable pattern in the night sky; the International Astronomical Union recognises 88 of them. Leo (the Lion, here printed as 'Leo major' to set it apart from the small Leo Minor) is a zodiac constellation whose brightest star is Regulus. Orion, the Hunter, is easy to spot in winter by the three stars of Orion's Belt, with the red supergiant Betelgeuse and the blue-white Rigel. The Great Bear (Ursa Major) contains the seven stars called Saptarishi in India (the Big Dipper), whose two pointer stars lead to the Pole Star (Dhruva Nakshatra). Memory tip: Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto are Jupiter's family of moons, not star patterns.

Q 16 | sci | Physics
Match the following things under List – I with those of List – II :
List – I / List – II
(a) Electromagnets – (i) Cobalt
(b) Fuse – (ii) Tungsten
(c) Electric bulb – (iii) Lead
(d) Permanent magnet – (iv) Soft iron
(1) (a) – (ii), (b) – (i), (c) – (iii), (d) – (iv)
(2) (a) – (iii), (b) – (ii), (c) – (iv), (d) – (i)
(3) (a) – (iii), (b) – (ii), (c) – (i), (d) – (iv)
(4) (a) – (iv), (b) – (iii), (c) – (ii), (d) – (i)
= 4
~ Electromagnets use soft iron (iv) as the core, because soft iron becomes strongly magnetised when current flows and loses almost all its magnetism when the current stops. A fuse wire is made of lead (iii), usually a tin–lead alloy, because its low melting point lets it melt and break the circuit when excess current flows. The filament of an electric bulb is made of tungsten (ii), which has the highest melting point of all metals (about 3,422 °C), so it glows white-hot without melting. Permanent magnets need materials that keep their magnetism for a long time, such as steel, cobalt (i) and Alnico (an alloy of aluminium, nickel and cobalt). So the matching is (a)–(iv), (b)–(iii), (c)–(ii), (d)–(i), option 4. Option 1 gives tungsten to electromagnets and cobalt to the fuse; option 2 gives soft iron to the bulb; option 3 gives cobalt to the bulb, so all are wrong. Memory tip: soft for temporary, hard for permanent; soft iron goes into electromagnets, transformer cores and electric bells, while steel and cobalt alloys make permanent magnets.

Q 17 | sci | Science & Technology
First telephone and television satellite launched by NASA is
(1) Syncom 3
(2) Sputnik 1
(3) TIROS 1
(4) Telstar 1
= 4
~ Telstar 1 was the first satellite to relay telephone calls and live television pictures across the Atlantic. It was built by Bell Telephone Laboratories of AT&T and launched by NASA from Cape Canaveral on 10 July 1962, so option 4 is correct. It was an active satellite: it received signals, amplified them and sent them back, unlike the earlier balloon satellite Echo 1, which only reflected signals passively. Syncom 3 (1964) was the first geostationary communication satellite and carried television of the 1964 Tokyo Olympics to the United States, but it came after Telstar. Sputnik 1 (4 October 1957) was the world's first artificial satellite, launched by the Soviet Union, not by NASA. TIROS 1 (1 April 1960) was NASA's first successful weather satellite, which sent back pictures of clouds. India link: India's first satellite television experiment, SITE (1975–76), used NASA's ATS-6 satellite to beam educational programmes to villages, and India's own first experimental communication satellite was APPLE (1981). Memory tip: TELstar for TELephone and TELevision.

Q 18 | sci | Physics
Expand ‘LED’
(1) Light Emitting Display
(2) Light Emitting Detector
(3) Light Emitting Diode
(4) Low Energy Display
= 3
~ LED stands for Light Emitting Diode, option 3. It is a semiconductor p–n junction diode that gives out light when it is forward biased: electrons and holes recombine at the junction and release energy as photons, a process called electroluminescence. The colour of the light depends on the semiconductor material used, such as gallium arsenide, gallium phosphide or gallium nitride. The other expansions are wrong. An LED display is only one application of LEDs, not the meaning of the letters (option 1). Devices that detect light are photodiodes or photodetectors, not LEDs (option 2). 'Low Energy Display' (option 4) is an invented expansion, even though LEDs do use little energy. LEDs consume far less power than filament bulbs and last much longer, which is why the Government of India's UJALA scheme (launched in 2015) distributes LED bulbs at low prices. Exam fact: the 2014 Nobel Prize in Physics went to Isamu Akasaki, Hiroshi Amano and Shuji Nakamura for inventing efficient blue LEDs, which made bright white LED lighting possible.

Q 19 | sci | Physics
Which of the following option given is correct regarding the frictional forces between two objects in the decreasing order of their magnitude ?
(1) Sliding, static, rolling
(2) Rolling, sliding, static
(3) Static, sliding, rolling
(4) Rolling, static, sliding
= 3
~ Friction between two surfaces is greatest just before motion starts and least when one body rolls over the other, so the decreasing order is static > sliding > rolling, option 3. Static friction acts when a force is applied but the object has not yet moved; its maximum value, called limiting friction, is high because the tiny irregularities of the two surfaces lock firmly into each other. Once the object starts sliding, the irregularities get less time to interlock, so sliding (kinetic) friction is smaller than limiting static friction; this is why it is harder to start pushing a heavy box than to keep it moving. Rolling friction is the smallest, because a rolling body touches the surface over a very small area; that is why wheels, ball bearings and rollers are used to move heavy loads. Options 1, 2 and 4 all place sliding or rolling friction above static friction, which reverses the true order. NCERT memory line: rolling friction is less than sliding friction, and sliding friction is less than static friction.

Q 20 | sci | Physics
Hydraulic lift is based on
(1) Pascal’s law
(2) Stokes’ law
(3) Bernoulli’s principle
(4) Newton’s law
= 1
~ A hydraulic lift works on Pascal's law, option 1. Pascal's law states that pressure applied to an enclosed fluid is transmitted equally and undiminished to every part of the fluid and to the walls of its container. In a hydraulic lift, a small force F1 on a narrow piston of area A1 creates a pressure P = F1/A1; the same pressure acts on a wide piston of area A2 and produces a much larger force F2 = P × A2 = F1 × (A2/A1). That is how a small push lifts a car at a service station. The same principle works in hydraulic jacks, hydraulic presses and the hydraulic brakes of vehicles. Stokes' law (option 2) gives the viscous drag on a small sphere moving through a fluid, F = 6πηrv, and explains the terminal velocity of raindrops. Bernoulli's principle (option 3) says that pressure falls where a fluid flows faster, which explains the lift on aeroplane wings and the working of atomisers. Newton's laws (option 4) describe motion in general and are not the specific basis of the lift. Memory tip: hydraulic means Pascal.

Q 21 | geo | Environment & Ecology
What causes Minamata disease ?
(1) Mercury poison
(2) Arsenic poison
(3) Lead poison
(4) Cadmium poison
= 1
~ Minamata disease is caused by mercury poisoning, option 1. It was first identified in 1956 in the town of Minamata in Kumamoto Prefecture, Japan. The Chisso Corporation's chemical factory released wastewater containing methylmercury into Minamata Bay; the mercury built up in fish and shellfish (bioaccumulation, then biomagnification along the food chain), and people who ate them suffered damage to the nervous system: numbness, loss of muscle coordination, impaired speech and vision and, in severe cases, death; babies were born with defects. The other options cause different problems. Arsenic in groundwater causes arsenicosis (skin lesions, black foot disease), as seen in West Bengal and Bangladesh. Lead poisoning (plumbism) harms the brain and blood, especially in children. Cadmium poisoning caused Itai-itai ('ouch-ouch') disease along the Jinzu river in Toyama, Japan. Memory pairs: Minamata–Mercury (both start with M) and Itai-itai–Cadmium. The Minamata Convention on Mercury (2013), to which India is a party, aims to cut mercury use and emissions worldwide.

Q 22 | geo | Environment & Ecology

(2) National Endangered & Endemic Research Institute
(3) Natural Environmental Ecosystem Research Institute
(4) National Environmental Engineering Research Institute

It works in environmental science and engineering: monitoring air and water quality, treating water and wastewater, managing solid waste and assessing environmental impact, and courts and governments often ask it to study pollution problems. Q 23 | pol | Laws, Police & Society
Name the domain of ethics which establishes the moral standards and guidelines that dictate how people ought to behave :
(1) Normative Ethics
(2) Positive Ethics
(3) Applied Ethics
(4) Meta Ethics
= 1
~ Normative ethics, option 1, is the branch of ethics that sets moral standards and guidelines telling people how they ought to behave, that is, what makes an action right or wrong, good or bad. Its main theories are deontology (duty-based ethics, as in Kant), consequentialism or utilitarianism (judging actions by their results, 'the greatest good of the greatest number') and virtue ethics (building good character, as in Aristotle). Meta-ethics (option 4) does not prescribe conduct; it studies the nature and meaning of moral terms, such as what 'good' means and whether moral truths are objective. Applied ethics (option 3) uses normative principles on specific practical issues such as euthanasia, capital punishment, business ethics or the police use of force. 'Positive ethics' (option 2) is not a standard domain; the word positive comes from economics, where positive statements describe what is, while normative statements say what ought to be. Memory tip: normative = norms = 'ought'; meta = 'about' ethics; applied = real cases. A police code of conduct is a practical example built on normative principles.

Q 24 | pol | Laws, Police & Society
Which of the following is/are not the key principles of code of conduct ?
(a) Integrity and honesty
(b) Political neutrality
(c) Courtesy and good behaviour
(d) Non-responsive to the public
(1) (b) and (d)
(2) Only (b)
(3) Only (d)
(4) (a) and (d)
= 3
~ Only item (d) is not a principle of a code of conduct, so option 3 is correct. A code of conduct lays down how public servants, including police personnel, should behave. Integrity and honesty (a) are its core: an officer must never misuse the office for personal gain. Political neutrality (b) means serving every government and every citizen impartially, without taking the side of any party. Courtesy and good behaviour (c) with the public are expected in every interaction, from the police station counter to a traffic check. 'Non-responsive to the public' (d) is the opposite of a principle; codes demand responsiveness, especially towards the weaker sections. For example, the All India Services (Conduct) Rules, as amended in 2014, list integrity and honesty, political neutrality, responsiveness to the public, and courtesy and good behaviour among the values expected of officers. Options 1 and 2 wrongly treat political neutrality as not a principle, and option 4 wrongly includes integrity and honesty. Q 25 | sci | Physics

= 3

Q 41 | geo | World Geography
Rift valley is formed due to
(1) Folds
(2) Compression
(3) Faults
(4) Joints
= 3
~ A rift valley is a long, narrow, steep-sided trough formed when a block of the earth's crust sinks between two roughly parallel faults, so it is a product of faulting, mainly by tensional forces that pull the crust apart, option (3). The sunken block is called a graben, and the raised blocks between faults are horsts or block mountains. Folds (option 1) form when compressional forces bend rock layers into up-folds (anticlines) and down-folds (synclines), producing fold mountains such as the Himalayas and the Alps. Compression (option 2) is the force behind folding and thrust faults, whereas a rift valley results mainly from tension. Joints (option 4) are cracks in rocks along which no displacement has taken place, so they cannot create such a valley. Examples: the East African Rift Valley and the Jordan valley with the Dead Sea; in India, the Narmada and Tapi flow westwards through rift valleys and form estuaries instead of deltas. Memory tip: 'Rift = Fault'; the Narmada flows west between the Vindhya and Satpura ranges in a rift valley.

The Second Council at Vaishali (c. 383 BCE), under Kalashoka and presided over by Sabakami, was called to settle ten disputed points of monastic discipline practised by the Vajji monks, which matches 'corrections of 10 elements'. The Third Council at Pataliputra (c. 250 BCE), under Ashoka and presided over by Moggaliputta Tissa, purified the Sangha and tried to restore unity in the Buddha's teaching; the Abhidhamma Pitaka (Kathavatthu) took shape here. The Fourth Council at Kundalavana in Kashmir, under Kanishka, with Vasumitra as president and Ashvaghosha as vice-president, saw Buddhism divide into Hinayana and Mahayana. Note: many books also say the first schism (Sthaviravadins and Mahasanghikas) followed the Second Council; the key links the split to the Fourth Council, the more commonly taught fact.

Consider the following statements regarding Shahanama :

Q 90 | eco | Indian Economy
In India ‘Minimum Support Price’ (MSP) for selected agricultural crops is recommended by :
(1) Reserve Bank of India
(2) Commission for Agricultural Cost and Prices
(3) Agriculture Ministry of Indian Government
(4) Food Corporation of India
= 2
~ The Minimum Support Price (MSP) is recommended by the Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices (CACP), so option 2 is correct. CACP was set up in 1965 as the Agricultural Prices Commission and renamed in 1985; it is an attached office of the Ministry of Agriculture and Farmers Welfare. It recommends MSPs for 22 mandated crops (14 kharif crops, 6 rabi crops and 2 commercial crops, jute and copra) and the Fair and Remunerative Price (FRP) for sugarcane, taking into account cost of production (A2, A2+FL and C2), demand and supply, market price trends and inter-crop price parity. The final decision is taken by the Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs (CCEA) chaired by the Prime Minister, and since 2018–19 MSP has been fixed at least 1.5 times the A2+FL cost. The RBI is the central bank and handles monetary policy, not crop prices. The Agriculture Ministry announces and implements MSP, but the recommending body is CACP. The Food Corporation of India (1965) procures grain at MSP and maintains buffer stocks for the public distribution system.

Q 91 | his | Modern India & Freedom Struggle
Gandhiji’s Harijan Newspaper belongs to which of the following form ?
(1) Weekly
(2) Monthly
(3) Fortnightly
(4) Daily

~ Harijan was a weekly newspaper started by Mahatma Gandhi in February 1933 (first issue 11 February 1933), as part of his campaign against untouchability after the Poona Pact (1932). Q 92 | sci | Science & Technology
The fuels used in Flex Fuel Vehicles (FFV) engine are

(2) Petrol + Ethanol in any proportion
(3) 75% Petrol + 25% Ethanol
(4) 80% Petrol + 20% Ethanol

Q 93 | sci | Chemistry
Bacteria present in mouth converts sugar and remaining food items after eating into acids. This causes tooth decay. This can be prevented by using toothpaste which are generally
(1) Basic
(2) Antiseptic
(3) Neutral
(4) Acidic
= 1
~ Bacteria in the mouth break down sugars and food particles left after eating and produce acids. When the pH in the mouth falls below about 5.5, these acids begin to dissolve tooth enamel, which is made of calcium hydroxyapatite (a calcium phosphate compound) and is the hardest substance in the human body; this leads to tooth decay. Toothpastes are generally basic (alkaline), so they neutralise the excess acid and help prevent decay; option 1 is correct. This is a standard NCERT Class 10 example of neutralisation in daily life, along with antacids such as milk of magnesia (magnesium hydroxide) for acidity and baking soda for a bee sting. 'Antiseptic' means able to kill germs, which some toothpastes may also be, but the property that counters the acid is basicity. A neutral paste could not neutralise the acid, and an acidic paste would make decay worse. Memory tip: acid + base gives salt + water, so the alkaline toothpaste cancels mouth acid; brushing after meals removes food particles before bacteria act, and fluoride in toothpaste further strengthens enamel.

Q 94 | sci | Chemistry
The common name used for chlorofluoro methane and chlorofluoro ethane which is responsible for ozone depletion is
(1) Freons
(2) Spiranes
(3) Crown ethers
(4) Chloroform
= 1
~ The chlorofluorocarbon (CFC) compounds of methane and ethane are collectively known as freons; Freon was originally a trade name. Freon-12 (CCl2F2, dichlorodifluoromethane) is one of the most common. Freons are stable, non-toxic, non-flammable and easily liquefied, so they were widely used as refrigerants in fridges and air-conditioners, as aerosol propellants and as foaming agents. In the stratosphere, ultraviolet radiation breaks them down to release chlorine radicals, and a single chlorine atom can destroy thousands of ozone molecules through a chain reaction; this produced the Antarctic 'ozone hole'. So option 1 is correct, as stated in NCERT Class 12 Chemistry (Haloalkanes and Haloarenes). Spiranes are organic compounds with two rings joined through one common atom. Crown ethers are cyclic polyethers that bind metal ions. Chloroform (CHCl3) is trichloromethane, once used as an anaesthetic, and it contains no fluorine. Exam facts: the Montreal Protocol (1987) phased out CFCs, the Kigali Amendment (2016) targets HFCs, and 16 September is observed as World Ozone Day.

Q 95 | sci | Chemistry
The cell used in devices like hearing aids and watches is
(1) Mercury cell
(2) Nickel-Cadmium cell
(3) Lead-storage cell
(4) Dry cell
= 1
~ The mercury cell is the small button-type primary cell suited to low-current devices such as hearing aids and watches, so option 1 is correct. In it, zinc–mercury amalgam is the anode, a paste of mercuric oxide (HgO) and carbon is the cathode, and a paste of KOH and ZnO is the electrolyte. Its main advantage is a nearly constant voltage of about 1.35 V throughout its life, because the overall cell reaction involves no ion whose concentration changes during use. The nickel–cadmium cell is a rechargeable (secondary) cell with a long life, used in cordless tools and emergency lights. The lead storage battery is the secondary cell used in automobiles and inverters, with lead as anode, lead dioxide as cathode and sulphuric acid as electrolyte. The dry cell (Leclanché cell, about 1.5 V) is used in torches, remote controls and wall clocks, but its voltage falls with use. This is an NCERT Class 12 Electrochemistry fact. Note: because mercury is toxic, silver-oxide and zinc–air button cells have now largely replaced mercury cells in watches and hearing aids.

Q 97 | sci | Biology
A novel strategy was adopted to prevent the infection of roots of tobacco plants by nematode is
(1) RNA interference
(2) Polymerase chain reaction
(3) Gene Therapy
(4) Incorporation of Bt. toxins gene
= 1
~ The nematode Meloidogyne incognita infects the roots of tobacco plants and causes a great reduction in yield. NCERT Class 12 Biology (Biotechnology and its Applications) describes the novel strategy used against it: RNA interference (RNAi). Using Agrobacterium vectors, nematode-specific genes were introduced into the host plant so that it produced both sense and anti-sense RNA. These complementary strands form double-stranded RNA (dsRNA), which starts RNAi and silences the specific mRNA of the nematode; the parasite cannot survive in the transgenic host, so the plant is protected. RNAi takes place naturally in all eukaryotes as a method of cellular defence. Hence option 1 is correct. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) is a laboratory technique for amplifying DNA, used in diagnosis and DNA fingerprinting, not for protecting plants. Gene therapy corrects a defective gene in a human patient; the first case (1990) was a 4-year-old girl with ADA deficiency. Bt toxin (cry) genes from Bacillus thuringiensis protect crops such as Bt cotton from insect pests like bollworms, not from nematodes.

Q 98 | geo | Environment & Ecology

Q 100 | sci | Chemistry
When the food is being cooked in the utensil, black colour formed underneath the utensil indicate
(1) Incomplete combustion of fuel
(2) Lack of fuel
(3) Charring of food
(4) Complete combustion of fuel
= 1
~ When a fuel burns in a sufficient supply of air (oxygen), combustion is complete: the carbon is fully converted into carbon dioxide and water, and the flame is clean and blue. When the air supply is insufficient, combustion is incomplete; part of the carbon remains unburnt and is released as soot (fine carbon particles), along with poisonous carbon monoxide, and the flame becomes yellow and sooty. This soot settles on the outer bottom of the vessel and makes it black. NCERT Class 10 Science (Carbon and its Compounds) notes that if the bottoms of cooking vessels are getting blackened, the air holes of the stove are blocked and fuel is being wasted. So option 1, incomplete combustion of fuel, is correct. Lack of fuel would only give a small flame, not soot. Charring of food happens inside the vessel, not on its outer bottom. Complete combustion gives a blue flame and leaves no black deposit, the opposite of what is observed. Safety tip: incomplete combustion in closed rooms produces carbon monoxide, which can cause suffocation, so places with stoves and heaters need good ventilation.
