# KSP PSI (Civil) & PSI (State Intelligence)-2017, Paper-2 General Studies, held 17 September 2017, KSP booklet series B; answers: KSP's provisional key

Q 1 | apt | Reasoning
Kajol goes 20 meters towards West from her house, then to her left she goes 10 meters. After this turning to her left she goes 20 meters. How far and in what direction is she from her house ?
(1) 20 m, North
(2) 30 m, South
(3) 10 m, South
(4) 10 m, North
= 3
~ Draw the path step by step from the house H. Kajol first walks 20 m West. When a person faces West, her left hand points South, so she turns and walks 10 m South. Now facing South, her left points East, so she walks 20 m East. The two 20 m legs (West and East) cancel each other, and she ends up directly below her starting point by the 10 m she walked South. So she is 10 m away from her house in the South direction, option (3). Option (4) gives the right distance but the wrong direction (it would be correct only if her turns were to the right). Options (1) and (2) mix up distances that cancel out with the net displacement. Exam tip: for direction questions, remember that left of West is South and left of South is East; always draw a quick sketch, and cancel equal opposite movements before measuring the final distance.

Q 2 | apt | Reasoning
Which one is different from the other three ?
(1) Exoneration
(2) Dismissal
(3) Retribution
(4) Acquittal
= 2
~ The official key gives option (2), Dismissal, as the odd word. Exoneration means officially clearing a person of blame, acquittal is a court verdict that the accused is not guilty, and retribution means punishment given in return for a wrong. The key's reasoning appears to be that exoneration, retribution and acquittal are all final judgements on a person's guilt (cleared or punished), while dismissal is a procedural or administrative act, such as dismissing a case or removing an employee from service, which says nothing about guilt. However, many candidates would pick Retribution as the odd one, because exoneration, acquittal and dismissal (of charges) all lead to the person being set free, while retribution alone is a punishment. Since the question is open to two readings, follow the key's answer, Dismissal. Exam tip: in odd-one-out word questions, first find the common meaning shared by three words (here, outcomes in a legal case) and look for the word whose meaning or category differs.

Q 3 | apt | Series & Puzzles
7 | 21 | 8
17 | ? | 5
12 | 1 | 23
Which of the following is the missing number ?
(1) 14
(2) 18
(3) 3
(4) 1
= 1
~ Look at the totals of the complete rows and columns. Row 1: 7 + 21 + 8 = 36. Row 3: 12 + 1 + 23 = 36. Column 1: 7 + 17 + 12 = 36. Column 3: 8 + 5 + 23 = 36. So every row and every column adds up to 36. For row 2: 17 + ? + 5 = 36, so ? = 36 – 22 = 14. Check with column 2: 21 + 14 + 1 = 36, which confirms the answer. Hence the missing number is 14, option (1). The other options fail the check: 18 gives a row total of 40, 3 gives 25 and 1 gives 23, none of which equals 36. Exam tip: in number-grid puzzles, first test the simplest rule, equal row or column sums, using a row or column that is fully known; then confirm the missing number in the other direction before marking the answer.

Q 4 | apt | Arithmetic
A train 110 m long passes a platform 90 m long in 10 sec. The speed of the train is
(1) 54 km/hr.
(2) 70 km/hr.
(3) 72 km/hr.
(4) 90 km/hr.
= 3
~ To pass a platform completely, the train must cover its own length plus the length of the platform. Distance = 110 m + 90 m = 200 m. Time = 10 seconds. Speed = 200 ÷ 10 = 20 m/s. To convert metres per second into kilometres per hour, multiply by 18/5: 20 × 18/5 = 72 km/hr. So the speed of the train is 72 km/hr, option (3). Common mistakes lead to the other options: using only the train's length gives 11 m/s = 39.6 km/hr, and using only the platform gives 9 m/s = 32.4 km/hr; 54 km/hr (15 m/s) and 90 km/hr (25 m/s) do not fit the data at all. Exam tip: when a train crosses a pole or a person, distance = train length; when it crosses a platform, bridge or another train, add both lengths. Remember: m/s × 18/5 = km/hr and km/hr × 5/18 = m/s.

Q 5 | geo | Environment & Ecology
Which is true for DDT ? It is
(1) not a pollutant
(2) an antibiotic
(3) an antiseptic agent
(4) a non-degradable pollutant
= 4
~ DDT (dichloro-diphenyl-trichloroethane) is a synthetic organochlorine insecticide, widely used from the 1940s to control mosquitoes (malaria) and crop pests. It is a non-biodegradable (persistent) pollutant: microorganisms cannot break it down easily into simple harmless compounds, so it stays in soil and water for years. It is fat-soluble, so it accumulates in the bodies of organisms (bioaccumulation) and its concentration rises at each level of the food chain (biomagnification). High DDT levels in birds of prey caused thin eggshells and population crashes, as described in Rachel Carson's book 'Silent Spring' (1962). It is now banned for agriculture under the Stockholm Convention on Persistent Organic Pollutants (2001), with limited use allowed for vector control. Hence option (4). It is clearly a pollutant, so (1) is wrong; it is not an antibiotic (which kills bacteria) or an antiseptic (used on living tissue). Tip: DDT is the standard example of biomagnification in exam questions.

Q 6 | sci | Biology
Apiculture is the branch of biology that deals with
(1) Study of cultivation of flower bearing plants
(2) Study of rearing honey-bees
(3) Study of rearing of silkworms
(4) Study of rearing of fishes
= 2
~ Apiculture (from Latin 'apis', bee) is the scientific rearing and management of honey-bees for honey, beeswax, royal jelly, propolis and pollination services. Common honey-bee species used in India are Apis cerana indica (Indian bee) and Apis mellifera (European bee); bee-keeping is promoted under the Khadi and Village Industries Commission and the National Bee-keeping and Honey Mission. So option (2) is correct. The other options refer to different branches: cultivation of flowering plants is floriculture, rearing of silkworms is sericulture (Karnataka is India's leading mulberry silk producer, with Ramanagara known as 'Silk City'), and rearing of fishes is pisciculture. Exam tip: learn these '-culture' terms together, as they are frequently asked: apiculture – bees, sericulture – silkworms, pisciculture – fish, aquaculture – aquatic organisms in general, horticulture – fruits and vegetables, floriculture – flowers, viticulture – grapes, olericulture – vegetables, and vermiculture – earthworms.

Q 7 | sci | Biology
Which is termed as 'Power House' of the cell ?
(1) Mitochondria
(2) Centriole
(3) Lysosome
(4) Golgi apparatus
= 1
~ Mitochondria are called the 'power house' of the cell because they carry out aerobic respiration (the Krebs cycle and the electron transport chain) and produce most of the cell's energy in the form of ATP (adenosine triphosphate). They are double-membraned organelles whose inner membrane is folded into cristae, and they contain their own circular DNA and ribosomes, so they can multiply on their own; this supports the endosymbiotic theory of their origin. So option (1) is correct. The other organelles have different roles: the centriole helps form spindle fibres during cell division in animal cells; lysosomes contain digestive enzymes and are called the 'suicide bags' of the cell; the Golgi apparatus modifies, packs and dispatches proteins and lipids and is called the cell's 'post office' or packaging centre. Exam tip: remember the nicknames together: mitochondria – power house, lysosome – suicide bag, ribosome – protein factory, chloroplast – kitchen of the plant cell.

Q 8 | sci | Biology
Retinol is another name of
(1) Vitamin C
(2) Vitamin A
(3) Vitamin D
(4) Vitamin K
= 2
~ Retinol is the chemical name of vitamin A, a fat-soluble vitamin. It is needed to form rhodopsin (visual purple) in the rod cells of the retina, which allows vision in dim light, and it also keeps the skin and mucous membranes healthy. Its deficiency causes night blindness, xerophthalmia (dry eyes) and Bitot's spots. Good sources are milk, eggs, liver, fish-liver oil, and carrots and green leafy vegetables, which supply beta-carotene that the body converts to vitamin A. So option (2) is correct. The other vitamins have different chemical names: vitamin C is ascorbic acid (deficiency – scurvy), vitamin D is calciferol (deficiency – rickets in children, osteomalacia in adults), and vitamin K is phylloquinone (needed for blood clotting). Exam tip: memorise the chemical name and deficiency disease of each vitamin as a pair – A retinol, B1 thiamine (beri-beri), B12 cobalamin (pernicious anaemia), C ascorbic acid, D calciferol, E tocopherol, K phylloquinone.

Q 9 | sci | Biology
Metabolic seat in human is
(1) Heart
(2) Liver
(3) Kidney
(4) Cardio-pulmonary system
= 2
~ The liver, the largest gland in the human body (about 1.5 kg), is called the 'metabolic seat' or chemical factory of the body because most metabolic reactions take place there. It regulates carbohydrate metabolism by storing glucose as glycogen and releasing it when needed (glycogenesis, glycogenolysis, gluconeogenesis); it converts excess amino acids into urea (deamination and the urea cycle); it synthesises plasma proteins such as albumin and fibrinogen; it produces bile for fat digestion; it stores vitamins A, D and B12 and iron; and it detoxifies drugs and alcohol. So option (2) is correct. The heart only pumps blood, the kidney filters blood and forms urine (excretion and osmoregulation), and the cardio-pulmonary system handles circulation and gas exchange; none of them is the main centre of metabolism. Exam tip: the liver is also the only internal organ that can regenerate significantly, and in the foetus it is a site of red blood cell formation.

Q 10 | his | Ancient India
Which of the following was the largest city of the Indus Valley Civilization ?
(1) Kalibangan
(2) Lothal
(3) Dholavira
(4) Mohenjodaro
= 4
~ Among the given options, Mohenjodaro (in Larkana district of Sindh, Pakistan, on the Indus) was the largest city of the Harappan or Indus Valley Civilization, covering more than 200 hectares. Excavated by R. D. Banerji in 1922 and later by John Marshall, it is famous for the Great Bath, the Great Granary, the assembly hall, a planned grid of streets with covered drains, the bronze 'dancing girl' and the steatite 'priest-king'. So option (4) is correct. The other sites are smaller: Kalibangan (Rajasthan, on the Ghaggar) is known for a ploughed field and fire altars; Lothal (Gujarat) was a port town with a dockyard; and Dholavira (Kachchh, Gujarat), though large, is known for its water reservoirs and a signboard inscription and is the largest Harappan site in India. Note that later excavations at Rakhigarhi (Haryana) suggest it may be equally large, but it is not among the options. Exam tip: link each Harappan site with its unique find.

Q 11 | his | Ancient India
Who was the first Tirthankara of Jainism ?
(1) Mahavira
(2) Parshvanath
(3) Ajinath
(4) Rishaba
= 4
~ According to Jain tradition there were 24 Tirthankaras (ford-makers who show the way across the cycle of rebirth). The first was Rishabhanatha, also called Rishabha or Adinatha, whose symbol is the bull; he is regarded in tradition as the founder of Jainism, and he also appears in the Bhagavata Purana. So option (4) is correct. Parshvanatha was the 23rd Tirthankara, a historical figure who preached four vows (non-violence, truth, non-stealing and non-possession). Vardhamana Mahavira was the 24th and last Tirthankara; he added the fifth vow, brahmacharya, and is treated as the historical organiser of Jainism. Ajitanatha (printed as 'Ajinath') was the 2nd Tirthankara; the 22nd was Neminatha. Exam tip: for Jainism, remember the first (Rishabha – bull), the 22nd (Neminatha – conch), the 23rd (Parshvanatha – serpent) and the 24th (Mahavira – lion), together with their symbols; Karnataka's Shravanabelagola is linked with Bahubali, the son of Rishabhanatha.

Q 12 | his | Ancient India
Which Ashokan edict describes his victory over Kalinga ?
(1) Eleventh
(2) Twelfth
(3) Thirteenth
(4) Fourteenth
= 4
~ The official key gives option (4), the Fourteenth. Historically, however, Ashoka's conquest of Kalinga (about 261 BCE, in the eighth year after his coronation) is described in Major Rock Edict XIII. In it Ashoka records that about 1,50,000 people were deported, 1,00,000 were killed and many more died, expresses deep remorse, and announces his shift from conquest by war (bherighosha) to conquest by dhamma (dhammaghosha). Rock Edict XIII also mentions Greek kings such as Antiochus II. Interestingly, this edict is absent at Dhauli and Jaugada in Kalinga itself, where separate Kalinga edicts were inscribed instead. Rock Edict XII deals with religious tolerance among sects, Rock Edict XI explains dhamma, and Rock Edict XIV is a short epilogue about the engraving of the edicts. So the factually correct answer is the Thirteenth, option (3), although the key marks the Fourteenth. Exam tip: memorise 'XIII – Kalinga war', 'XII – tolerance', 'II – medical care for men and animals'.

Q 13 | his | Art & Culture
Which of the following statements are true ?
(1) Shankaracharya propounded the theory of Advaitavad
(2) Madhvacharya propounded the theory of Vishishtadvaitavad
(3) Both
(4) None
= 1
~ Only statement (a) is correct. Adi Shankaracharya (8th century CE, born at Kaladi in Kerala) propounded Advaita Vedanta, the non-dualism which holds that Brahman alone is real, the world is maya, and the individual soul (atman) is identical with Brahman. He established four mathas, including the Sringeri Sharada Peetham in Karnataka. Statement (b) is wrong: Vishishtadvaita (qualified non-dualism) was propounded by Ramanujacharya (11th–12th century), who stayed at Melukote in Karnataka under Hoysala patronage. Madhvacharya (13th century, born near Udupi) propounded Dvaita, or dualism, which holds that God (Vishnu) and the individual souls are eternally distinct; he founded the Udupi Krishna Matha and the eight Ashta mathas. Since only (a) is true, option (1) is correct, and 'Both' and 'None' are wrong. Exam tip: remember the trio with their Karnataka links – Shankara: Advaita (Sringeri), Ramanuja: Vishishtadvaita (Melukote), Madhva: Dvaita (Udupi).

Q 14 | his | Karnataka History
Who among the following did not travelled to Vijayanagar Kingdom ?
(1) Ibn Batuta
(2) Abd-ur-Razzaq
(3) Nicolo Conte
(4) Marco Polo
= 4
~ The Vijayanagara empire was founded in 1336 by Harihara I and Bukka I of the Sangama dynasty. Marco Polo, the Venetian traveller, visited South India around 1292–93, mainly the Kakatiya port of Motupalli and the Pandya kingdom, more than four decades before Vijayanagara existed, so he never visited it; option (4) is correct. The others did travel in the region: the Italian Nicolo de Conti came in about 1420 during the reign of Devaraya I and described the city and its festivals; the Persian envoy Abdur Razzaq visited the court of Devaraya II in 1443 and praised the capital's seven fortifications; and the Moroccan Ibn Battuta, who was in India from 1333 to the 1340s, recorded meeting Harihara I (whom he called 'Haraib') on the west coast in the early years of the kingdom. Later visitors included Duarte Barbosa, Domingo Paes and Fernao Nuniz. Exam tip: check dates – a traveller before 1336 cannot have seen Vijayanagara.

Q 15 | his | Modern India & Freedom Struggle
Partition of Bengal was annulled in
(1) 1911
(2) 1935
(3) 1947
(4) 1956
= 1
~ The Partition of Bengal was announced by Viceroy Lord Curzon in July 1905 and came into effect on 16 October 1905. Officially justified on administrative grounds, it was seen as an attempt to divide Hindus and Muslims and weaken Bengali nationalism. It triggered the Swadeshi and Boycott movement, with bonfires of foreign cloth, national schools, and Rabindranath Tagore's call to observe Raksha Bandhan as a symbol of unity. Because of the sustained agitation, King George V announced the annulment of the partition at the Delhi Durbar on 12 December 1911, during the viceroyalty of Lord Hardinge II; at the same time the capital of British India was shifted from Calcutta to Delhi. So option (1) is correct. 1935 is the year of the Government of India Act, 1947 is Independence (when Bengal was partitioned again), and 1956 is the States Reorganisation Act. Exam tip: pair 1905 (partition, Curzon) with 1911 (annulment, Hardinge II, capital to Delhi).

Q 16 | his | Modern India & Freedom Struggle
Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev and Rajguru were hanged for
(1) Meerut Conspiracy
(2) Alipore Conspiracy
(3) Lahore Conspiracy
(4) None
= 3
~ Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev and Rajguru, members of the Hindustan Socialist Republican Association (HSRA), were tried in the Lahore Conspiracy Case (the second Lahore Conspiracy Case, 1929–30) for the killing of British police officer J. P. Saunders in Lahore in December 1928, an act meant to avenge the death of Lala Lajpat Rai after a lathi charge during the protest against the Simon Commission. A special tribunal sentenced them to death, and they were hanged in Lahore Central Jail on 23 March 1931, a day now observed as Shaheed Diwas (Martyrs' Day). So option (3) is correct. The Meerut Conspiracy Case (1929) was against communist and trade union leaders, and the Alipore Bomb Case (1908) involved Aurobindo Ghosh, Barindra Kumar Ghosh and others after the Muzaffarpur bombing by Khudiram Bose. Exam tip: Bhagat Singh and B. K. Dutt also threw bombs in the Central Legislative Assembly on 8 April 1929, for which they were given life sentences.

Q 17 | his | Modern India & Freedom Struggle
Which of the following organization boycotted the Simon Commission ?
1. Congress
2. Muslim League
3. Hindu Mahasabha
(1) 1, 2, 3
(2) 1, 2
(3) 1, 3
(4) 2, 3
= 1
~ The Indian Statutory Commission, headed by Sir John Simon, was appointed in November 1927 to review the working of the 1919 reforms. Because all its seven members were British and no Indian was included, it was widely seen as an insult. When it arrived in Bombay on 3 February 1928, it was greeted with hartals and black flags and the slogan 'Simon Go Back'. The Indian National Congress decided at its Madras session (1927) to boycott it 'at every stage and in every form'. The Muslim League faction led by Mohammad Ali Jinnah and the Hindu Mahasabha also joined the boycott; only a section of the League under Muhammad Shafi and some others cooperated. Hence all three organisations are counted as having boycotted it, option (1). During a protest against the commission at Lahore, Lala Lajpat Rai was injured in a lathi charge and died in November 1928. Exam tip: the Nehru Report (1928) was prepared as the Indian answer to the Simon Commission.

Q 18 | his | Modern India & Freedom Struggle
The only annual session of the Congress presided over by Gandhi was
(1) Kakinada, 1923
(2) Belgaum, 1924
(3) Kanpur, 1925
(4) Guwahati, 1926
= 2
~ Mahatma Gandhi presided over only one session of the Indian National Congress: the 39th annual session held at Belgaum (now Belagavi, Karnataka) in December 1924. The venue was named Vijayanagar, and the session is remembered for songs by Gangubai Hangal and for the efforts of local leaders such as Gangadhar Rao Deshpande; it also helped bring the Swarajists and the no-changers together. So option (2) is correct. The other sessions had different presidents: Kakinada 1923 – Maulana Mohammad Ali; Kanpur 1925 – Sarojini Naidu, the first Indian woman to preside over the Congress; Guwahati 1926 – S. Srinivasa Iyengar. In 2024, the centenary of the Belagavi session was commemorated by the Karnataka government. Exam tip: Belgaum 1924 is a favourite question in Karnataka exams because it is the only Congress session Gandhi ever presided over, and it was held in present-day Karnataka.

Q 19 | his | Art & Culture
Who is the father of Carnatic Music ?
(1) Kanakadasa
(2) Purandaradasa
(3) Raghavanka
(4) Sarvajna
= 2
~ Purandaradasa (1484–1564), a Haridasa saint-composer of the Vijayanagara period, is known as the 'Karnataka Sangeeta Pitamaha', the father of Carnatic music. Born Srinivasa Nayaka, a wealthy merchant, he renounced his wealth and became a disciple of Vyasatirtha. He systematised music teaching by introducing graded lessons such as sarali varisai, janti varisai, alankaras and geethas, set in the raga Mayamalavagowla, which students still learn first today. He is said to have composed about 4.75 lakh songs (devaranamas) in Kannada with the signature 'Purandara Vittala'. So option (2) is correct. Kanakadasa was another Haridasa saint, famous for 'Mohana Tarangini' and the Kanakana Kindi at Udupi; Raghavanka was a 13th-century Kannada poet who wrote 'Harishchandra Kavya'; and Sarvajna was a 16th-century poet known for his three-line vachanas (tripadis). Exam tip: the Haridasa movement gave Kannada devotional music; Purandaradasa is the music teacher, Kanakadasa the social reformer.

Q 20 | cur | National
Which Committee recommended changes in Indian Cricket Board Structure ?
(1) Lodha Committee
(2) Debroy Committee
(3) Parekh Committee
(4) Sri Krishna Committee
= 1
~ The Justice R. M. Lodha Committee was appointed by the Supreme Court in January 2015, after the IPL betting and spot-fixing scandal of 2013, to recommend reforms in the Board of Control for Cricket in India (BCCI). Its report of January 2016 proposed sweeping changes in the board's structure: one state, one vote; an age cap of 70 years and term limits for office-bearers, with cooling-off periods; a ban on ministers and government servants holding posts; a players' association; and a separate governing council for the IPL. The Supreme Court accepted most of these recommendations in July 2016, and in January 2017 it removed BCCI president Anurag Thakur and appointed a Committee of Administrators headed by Vinod Rai. So option (1) is correct. The Bibek Debroy Committee dealt with restructuring Indian Railways, the Deepak Parekh Committee with infrastructure financing, and the Justice B. N. Srikrishna Committee with Telangana and later financial sector laws. Exam tip: link each committee name with its subject.

Q 21 | eco | Money, Banking & Budget
How many banks were nationalised in 1969 ?
(1) 16
(2) 14
(3) 15
(4) 20
= 2
~ On 19 July 1969, the Government of India under Prime Minister Indira Gandhi nationalised 14 major commercial banks, each with deposits of over Rs 50 crore, through an ordinance later replaced by the Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Act, 1970. The aim was to extend banking to rural areas, direct credit to priority sectors such as agriculture and small industry, and reduce the concentration of economic power. The banks included Central Bank of India, Bank of India, Punjab National Bank, Bank of Baroda, United Commercial Bank, Canara Bank, Syndicate Bank, Union Bank, Indian Bank, Dena Bank, Allahabad Bank, Indian Overseas Bank, United Bank of India and Bank of Maharashtra. So option (2) is correct. A second round of nationalisation on 15 April 1980 took over 6 more banks (deposits over Rs 200 crore), making the total 20, which explains option (4). Exam tip: remember '1969 – 14 banks (Rs 50 crore); 1980 – 6 banks (Rs 200 crore)'; note that Canara and Syndicate Banks were from Karnataka.

Q 22 | cur | International & Sports
World Yoga Day is celebrated on
(1) 20 June
(2) 22 June
(3) 21 June
(4) 30 June
= 3
~ The International Day of Yoga is observed every year on 21 June. Prime Minister Narendra Modi proposed it in his address to the UN General Assembly in September 2014, and the UNGA adopted the resolution on 11 December 2014 with a record 177 co-sponsoring countries. 21 June was chosen because it is the summer solstice, the longest day of the year in the Northern Hemisphere, which has special significance in many cultures. The first International Day of Yoga was celebrated on 21 June 2015, with a mass yoga session at Rajpath, New Delhi, which set Guinness world records. In 2017, the year of this exam, the main national event was held at Lucknow, with the theme 'Yoga for Health'. So option (3) is correct; the other dates have no link with Yoga Day. Exam tip: the Ministry of AYUSH is the nodal ministry, and each year has a different theme and host city, which are often asked in current affairs.

Q 23 | cur | National
Shri Ramnath Kovind is the
(1) 15th President of India
(2) 13th President of India
(3) 12th President of India
(4) 14th President of India
= 4
~ Ram Nath Kovind was elected as the 14th President of India in July 2017, defeating Meira Kumar in the presidential election, and was sworn in on 25 July 2017 by Chief Justice J. S. Khehar. A lawyer from Kanpur Dehat in Uttar Pradesh, he had earlier served as a Rajya Sabha member (1994–2006) and as Governor of Bihar (2015–2017). He succeeded Pranab Mukherjee, the 13th President. Giri in 1969 before his election, and M. Hidayatullah) are not counted. Exam tip: Kovind was the second Dalit President of India after K. R. Narayanan.

Q 24 | cur | International & Sports
Which country is not a part of SAARC ?
(1) Nepal
(2) Myanmar
(3) Afghanistan
(4) Bangladesh
= 2
~ The South Asian Association for Regional Cooperation (SAARC) was founded on 8 December 1985 at Dhaka with seven members: India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Nepal, Bhutan, Sri Lanka and the Maldives. Afghanistan joined as the eighth member in April 2007 at the 14th summit in New Delhi. Its secretariat is at Kathmandu, Nepal. Myanmar is not a member; it is only one of the nine observers of SAARC (along with China, Japan, the USA, the EU, Iran, South Korea, Australia and Mauritius). So option (2) is correct, and Nepal, Afghanistan and Bangladesh are all members. Myanmar is, however, a member of ASEAN and of BIMSTEC, which links India with its eastern neighbours. In 2016 the 19th SAARC summit planned for Islamabad was cancelled after India and others pulled out following the Uri attack. Exam tip: remember SAARC as 'eight members, secretariat Kathmandu, founded Dhaka 1985'; Myanmar and China are common distractors.

Q 25 | his | Art & Culture
Kathakali is dance of which state ?
(1) Kerala
(2) Karnataka
(3) Andhra Pradesh
(4) Manipur
= 1
~ Kathakali is the classical dance-drama of Kerala, which developed in the 17th century from Ramanattam and Krishnanattam. It is known for its elaborate make-up and costumes, with face colours indicating character types (green 'pacha' for noble heroes, red-and-black 'kathi' for proud villains, 'thadi' beards and so on), large headgear, and stories from the Ramayana, Mahabharata and Puranas told through hand gestures (mudras) and facial expressions, traditionally by male performers. The poet Vallathol Narayana Menon revived it by founding the Kerala Kalamandalam in 1930. So option (1) is correct. Karnataka's famous dance-drama is Yakshagana, Andhra Pradesh's classical dance is Kuchipudi, and Manipur's is Manipuri. Kerala also has Mohiniyattam as a classical dance. Exam tip: India's Sangeet Natak Akademi recognises eight classical dances: Bharatanatyam (Tamil Nadu), Kathak (North India), Kathakali and Mohiniyattam (Kerala), Kuchipudi (Andhra Pradesh), Odissi (Odisha), Manipuri (Manipur) and Sattriya (Assam).

Q 26 | comp | Computer Fundamentals
In computer, 'MPG' refers to which file ?
(1) Movie file
(2) Image file
(3) MS Office Word document
(4) Power Point
= 1
~ MPG (also written MPEG) is a file extension named after the Moving Picture Experts Group, the body that sets standards for compressing digital video and audio. A .mpg/.mpeg file therefore holds video, that is, a movie file, so option (1) is correct. Image files use extensions such as .jpg/.jpeg, .png, .gif and .bmp, so option (2) is wrong. MS Word documents are saved as .doc or .docx, and PowerPoint presentations as .ppt or .pptx, so options (3) and (4) are also wrong. The same MPEG group created the MP3 audio format (MPEG-1 Audio Layer III) and the MP4 video container. Other common video extensions are .avi, .mkv, .mov and .wmv. Exam tip: learn file extensions in groups (text, image, audio, video, office files); questions matching an extension to its file type are frequent in police and other state-level exams.

Q 27 | geo | Environment & Ecology
Largest number of Tiger Reserve is located in
(1) Karnataka
(2) Andhra Pradesh

Q 42 | geo | Environment & Ecology
Which of the following is not a Green House Gas ?
(1) Methane
(2) Nitrous Oxide
(3) Hydrogen
(4) Carbon Dioxide
= 3
~ Greenhouse gases absorb the infrared (heat) radiation emitted by the Earth's surface and re-emit it, warming the lower atmosphere. Carbon dioxide (CO2), methane (CH4) and nitrous oxide (N2O) are major greenhouse gases and are listed among the six gases covered by the Kyoto Protocol (with HFCs, PFCs and sulphur hexafluoride). Hydrogen (H2) is a diatomic molecule of identical atoms with no permanent dipole, so it does not absorb infrared radiation significantly and is not counted as a greenhouse gas; option (3) is therefore correct. Methane, released from paddy fields, livestock and landfills, is far more potent than CO2 per molecule; nitrous oxide comes mainly from nitrogen fertilisers; CO2 is the largest contributor because of fossil fuel burning. Water vapour and ozone are also natural greenhouse gases. Exam tip: noble gases, nitrogen, oxygen and hydrogen are not greenhouse gases; gases with three or more atoms usually are.

Q 51 | apt | Reasoning
In certain language, CALCUTTA is written as ACCLTUAT, then how will you write KARNATAK in the language ?
(1) AKRNTAAK
(2) AKNRTAKA
(3) AKNRATAK
(4) NATKAKAR
= 2
~ Split CALCUTTA into pairs of letters and reverse each pair: CA → AC, LC → CL, UT → TU, TA → AT, which gives AC CL TU AT = ACCLTUAT, exactly the code given. Apply the same rule to KARNATAK: KA → AK, RN → NR, AT → TA, AK → KA, giving AK NR TA KA = AKNRTAKA, option (2). Check the others: AKRNTAAK (AK RN TA AK) reverses only the first and third pairs and leaves RN and AK unchanged; AKNRATAK (AK NR AT AK) reverses the first two pairs but not the last two; NATKAKAR is just a rearrangement of the letters that does not follow the pair-swap rule. Only option (2) applies the pair-swap rule to all four pairs. Exam tip: when a coded word has the same letters as the original word (only the order changes), first test simple patterns such as reversing pairs, reversing halves or reversing the whole word, before trying letter shifts.

Q 52 | apt | Reasoning

= 1
~ The speaker says that the son of the man in the photograph is the speaker's only brother. (2) 21 years
(3) 17 years
(4) 15 years
= 3
~ Step 1: Vinod will be 20 years old after 5 years, so his present age is 20 – 5 = 15 years. Step 2: Arun : Vinod = 4 : 3, so Arun's present age = 15 × 4/3 = 20 years. Q 54 | apt | Arithmetic
If each side of a square is increased by 25%, its area is increased by
(1) 25%
(2) 50%
(3) 40.5%
(4) 56.25%
= 4
~ Let the side be 100, so the area is 100 × 100 = 10,000. After a 25% increase the side becomes 125, and the new area is 125 × 125 = 15,625. Increase = 15,625 – 10,000 = 5,625, which is 5,625/10,000 × 100 = 56.25%, option (4). The same result comes from the successive-percentage formula x + y + xy/100 with x = y = 25: 25 + 25 + 625/100 = 56.25%. ~ Average = total quantity ÷ total number of months. Petrol used in the first 5 months = 5 × 96 = 480 litres. Petrol used in the next 7 months = 7 × 120 = 840 litres. Total for 12 months = 480 + 840 = 1,320 litres. Monthly average = 1,320 ÷ 12 = 110 litres, option (3). A common mistake is to take the simple mean (96 + 120) ÷ 2 = 108, which ignores that the two periods have different numbers of months; a weighted average is needed. Options 105, 100 and 115 litres do not fit the total of 1,320 litres. Exam tip: when averages of groups of different sizes are combined, always use the weighted average: (n1 × a1 + n2 × a2) ÷ (n1 + n2); the answer must lie between the two averages and closer to the larger group's value, here 120.

Q 56 | apt | Arithmetic
A student got twice as many sums wrong as he got right. If he attempted 48 sums in all, how many did he solve correctly ?
(1) 12
(2) 16
(3) 18
(4) 24
= 2
~ Let the number of sums solved correctly be x. The wrong sums are twice that, 2x. Together they make all 48 attempted sums: x + 2x = 48, so 3x = 48 and x = 16. So he solved 16 sums correctly (and 32 wrongly), option (2). Check: 32 is exactly twice 16, and 16 + 32 = 48. Option (4), 24, would be correct only if right and wrong sums were equal (48 ÷ 2); 12 would mean a ratio of 1 : 3; 18 does not divide 48 into a 1 : 2 ratio at all. Exam tip: in ratio-of-parts problems, add the ratio terms (1 + 2 = 3 parts), divide the total by the sum of parts (48 ÷ 3 = 16), and then multiply by each part's share. This method solves most "twice as many" and "thrice as many" questions in a few seconds.

Q 79 | pol | Constitution & Rights
Which of the following writs deals with unlawful detentions ?
(1) Mandamus
(2) Certiorari
(3) Habeas Corpus
(4) Quo Warranto
= 3
~ Habeas Corpus, Latin for "you may have the body", is the writ against unlawful detention, so option (3) is correct. Through it a court orders the person or authority holding someone in custody to produce the detained person before the court and justify the detention; if the detention is illegal, the person is set free. It can be issued against both public authorities and private individuals. The Supreme Court issues writs under Article 32 and High Courts under Article 226. Q 82 | comp | Computer Fundamentals
Smallest unit in digital system is
(1) byte
(2) character
(3) bit
(4) word
= 3
~ The bit (binary digit) is the smallest unit of data in a digital system, so option (3) is correct. A bit can hold only one of two values, 0 or 1, corresponding to the off and on states of an electronic switch. All larger units are built from bits: 4 bits make a nibble, 8 bits make a byte, and a byte is usually enough to store one character (letter, digit or symbol) in ASCII coding. A word is the group of bits a processor handles at one time, commonly 16, 32 or 64 bits. So byte, character and word are all larger than a bit, making options (1), (2) and (4) wrong. Memory sizes then go up as kilobyte (1024 bytes), megabyte, gigabyte, terabyte and so on. Exam tip: the order to remember is bit < nibble < byte < KB < MB < GB < TB; the term "bit" was popularised by Claude Shannon.

(1) P. T. Usha
(2) Karnam Malleswari
(3) Mary Kom
(4) Chanu
= 2
~ Karnam Malleswari, a weightlifter from Andhra Pradesh, became the first Indian woman to win an Olympic medal when she won bronze in the 69 kg category at the Sydney Olympics in 2000, so option (2) is correct. Q 85 | his | Modern India & Freedom Struggle
August 9, 1925 is known in Indian history for
(1) Chauri-Chaura Incident
(2) Jallianwala Bagh Incident
(3) Bengal Partition
(4) Kakori Train Robbery
= 4
~ On 9 August 1925, revolutionaries of the Hindustan Republican Association (HRA) stopped a train at Kakori near Lucknow and looted government treasury money it was carrying, to fund their armed struggle. This is the Kakori Train Robbery (Kakori conspiracy), so option (4) is correct. It was led by Ram Prasad Bismil with Ashfaqulla Khan, Rajendra Lahiri, Chandrashekhar Azad and others. After the trial, Bismil, Ashfaqulla Khan, Rajendra Lahiri and Roshan Singh were hanged in December 1927. The other events have different dates: Chauri Chaura (Gorakhpur), where a police station was burnt, on 5 February 1922, leading Gandhi to withdraw the Non-Cooperation Movement; the Jallianwala Bagh massacre at Amritsar on 13 April 1919; and the Partition of Bengal by Lord Curzon on 16 October 1905. Exam tip: 9 August is also the date of the Quit India Movement (1942), so do not confuse the two.

Q 86 | eco | Money, Banking & Budget
Currency Sign for Indian Rupee was introduced in
(1) 2012
(2) 2010
(3) 2011
(4) 2000
= 1
~ The official key gives 2012. The symbol ₹, designed by D. Udaya Kumar (then a postgraduate at IIT Bombay), was approved by the Union Cabinet on 15 July 2010, which made India the fifth country with a distinctive currency symbol after the US dollar, British pound, Japanese yen and euro. Coins carrying the new symbol were released in July 2011, and the Reserve Bank of India announced its first banknotes bearing ₹ (the ₹10 note) in September 2011, with other denominations following; the key's 2012 can only be read as the year such notes became common in circulation, which is a weak reading. Which of these are forms of social media ?

Q 88 | his | Karnataka History
Who wrote the book Kavirajamarga ?
(1) Pampa
(2) Amoghavarsha I
(3) Nagavarma
(4) Chavundaraya
= 2
~ Kavirajamarga, the earliest available literary work in Kannada (around 850 CE), is attributed to the Rashtrakuta king Amoghavarsha I (Nripatunga), so option (2) is correct; some scholars credit his court poet Srivijaya. It is a work on poetics (rhetoric) based on Dandin's Kavyadarsha and is famous for describing the Kannada land as extending "from the Kaveri to the Godavari" and praising the Kannada people. Pampa (10th century) wrote Vikramarjuna Vijaya (Pampa Bharata) and Adipurana and is called Adikavi; Nagavarma I wrote Chhandombudhi, and Nagavarma II wrote Kavyavalokana and Karnataka Bhashabhushana; Chavundaraya wrote Chavundaraya Purana (978 CE) and built the Gommateshwara statue at Shravanabelagola. Amoghavarsha I also wrote Prashnottara Ratnamalika in Sanskrit. Exam tip: Kavirajamarga – Amoghavarsha I – Rashtrakutas – capital Manyakheta (Malkhed); it is the first Kannada book available to us.

Q 89 | his | Karnataka History
Karnataka was formed in 1956 on the basis of which report ?
(1) JVP Committee
(2) Dhar Committee
(3) Fazal Ali Committee
(4) Wanchoo Committee
= 3
~ The unified Mysore State (renamed Karnataka in 1973) was formed on 1 November 1956 on the basis of the report of the States Reorganisation Commission headed by Justice Fazal Ali, so option (3) is correct. The commission was set up in 1953 with K. M. Panikkar and H. N. Kunzru as members and submitted its report in 1955, recommending reorganisation largely on linguistic lines; it was implemented by the States Reorganisation Act, 1956. The other committees came earlier and did not create Karnataka: the Linguistic Provinces Commission under S. K. Dhar (1948) and the JVP Committee of Jawaharlal Nehru, Vallabhbhai Patel and Pattabhi Sitaramayya (1948–49) both advised against immediate linguistic reorganisation; the Wanchoo Committee (1953) dealt with the financial aspects of forming Andhra State. Exam tip: Mysore was renamed Karnataka on 1 November 1973 under Chief Minister D. Devaraj Urs; 1 November is celebrated as Kannada Rajyotsava.

Q 90 | geo | Indian Geography
Mullaperiyar Dam is a matter of dispute between
(1) Tamil Nadu and Kerala
(2) Tamil Nadu and Karnataka
(3) Tamil Nadu and Andhra Pradesh
(4) None of the above
= 1
~ The Mullaperiyar Dam is the subject of a long-running dispute between Tamil Nadu and Kerala, so option (1) is correct. The masonry gravity dam stands on the Periyar river in Idukki district of Kerala but is owned and operated by Tamil Nadu under a 999-year lease signed in 1886 between the Maharaja of Travancore and the British Madras Presidency. It was built under Colonel John Pennycuick and completed in 1895 to divert water to the drought-prone Vaigai basin in Tamil Nadu. Kerala raises safety concerns about the old dam and the downstream population, while Tamil Nadu wants to raise the water level for irrigation; the Supreme Court in 2014 allowed the level to be kept at 142 feet. Tamil Nadu and Karnataka dispute the Cauvery waters, not Mullaperiyar. Exam tip: Mullaperiyar – Periyar river – Kerala location, Tamil Nadu control; Cauvery – Karnataka and Tamil Nadu.

Q 91 | sci | Physics
The irregular motion of particles arising from the thermal motion of the particles is known as
(1) Velocity
(2) Drift velocity
(3) Brownian motion
(4) Diffusion
= 3
~ Brownian motion is the random, zig-zag movement of tiny particles suspended in a liquid or gas, caused by uneven collisions with the fast-moving molecules of the fluid, which are in constant thermal motion. So option (3) is correct. It was first observed in 1827 by the botanist Robert Brown, who saw pollen grains jiggling in water, and it was explained mathematically by Albert Einstein in 1905; its study gave strong evidence for the existence of atoms and molecules. The motion increases with temperature and decreases with the size of the particle and the viscosity of the medium. Velocity is simply the rate of change of displacement; drift velocity is the small average velocity of free electrons in a conductor under an electric field; diffusion is the net spreading of particles from higher to lower concentration, which is a result of such random motion, not the motion itself. Exam tip: Brownian motion – Robert Brown 1827 – Einstein 1905.

Q 92 | sci | Physics
The phenomenon of sound propagation in air is
(1) Isothermic process
(2) Adiabatic process
(3) Isochoric process
(4) Isobaric process
= 2
~ Sound travels through air as rapid compressions and rarefactions. These changes happen so quickly that there is no time for heat to flow in or out of the compressed or rarefied layers, so the process is adiabatic, option (2). Newton assumed the process was isothermal (constant temperature) and his formula v = √(P/ρ) gave the speed of sound in air as about 280 m/s, well below the measured value of about 332 m/s at 0 °C. Laplace corrected this by treating it as adiabatic, giving v = √(γP/ρ), where γ (about 1.4 for air) is the ratio of specific heats; this matches experiment (Laplace's correction). An isochoric process occurs at constant volume and an isobaric process at constant pressure; neither applies, because both pressure and volume change in a sound wave. Exam tip: Newton's formula – isothermal; Laplace's correction – adiabatic.

Q 93 | sci | Physics
Tesla is the unit of
(1) Electric field
(2) Electric flux
(3) Magnetic field
(4) Magnetic flux
= 3
~ The tesla (T) is the SI unit of magnetic field, more precisely magnetic flux density (B), so option (3) is correct. One tesla equals one weber per square metre (Wb/m²) or one newton per ampere-metre (N/A·m). The unit is named after the Serbian-American scientist Nikola Tesla. The smaller CGS unit is the gauss: 1 tesla = 10,000 gauss; the Earth's magnetic field is only about 0.3–0.6 gauss. The other quantities have different units: electric field is measured in newton per coulomb (N/C) or volt per metre (V/m); electric flux in volt-metre (N·m²/C); and magnetic flux in the weber (Wb), which equals tesla × square metre. So options (1), (2) and (4) are wrong. Exam tip: Weber is for magnetic flux, tesla for magnetic flux density (field); MRI machines typically use fields of 1.5 to 3 tesla.

Q 94 | sci | Chemistry
Which of the following is a chemical change ?
(1) Twinkling of stars
(2) Boiling of water
(3) Cutting of fruits
(4) Cooking of vegetables
= 4
~ Cooking vegetables is a chemical change, option (4). Heat breaks down cell walls, starches and proteins and forms new substances with new taste, colour and texture; the change is permanent and the raw vegetable cannot be recovered. A chemical change produces new substances and is usually irreversible. The other options are not chemical changes: twinkling of stars is an optical effect caused by atmospheric refraction of starlight through layers of air of varying density, not a change in any substance; boiling of water is a physical change of state from liquid to vapour, and the steam can be condensed back into water with the same composition (H2O); cutting fruit only changes its size and shape. Other examples of chemical change: rusting of iron, burning of paper, curdling of milk, ripening of fruit and digestion of food. Exam tip: ask whether a new substance is formed; if yes, it is a chemical change.

Q 95 | sci | Chemistry
Chemical formula for baking soda is
(1) NaHCO3
(2) NaOH
(3) NaCl
(4) NaBr
= 1
~ Baking soda is sodium hydrogen carbonate (sodium bicarbonate), NaHCO3, so option (1) is correct. It is made by the Solvay (ammonia-soda) process. When heated, it decomposes: 2NaHCO3 → Na2CO3 + H2O + CO2; the carbon dioxide released makes cakes and bread soft and spongy. It is mildly alkaline, so it is also used as an antacid to relieve acidity and in soda-acid fire extinguishers. The other formulas belong to different compounds: NaOH is sodium hydroxide (caustic soda), a strong base used in soap making; NaCl is sodium chloride (common salt); NaBr is sodium bromide, once used as a sedative. Related compounds often asked: washing soda is Na2CO3·10H2O, baking powder is baking soda mixed with a mild acid such as tartaric acid, and bleaching powder is CaOCl2. Exam tip: baking soda – NaHCO3; washing soda – Na2CO3·10H2O; caustic soda – NaOH.

Q 96 | sci | Science & Technology
Sagar Samrat is
(1) Oil platform
(2) A research vessel
(3) Dam on Southern river
(4) None of these
= 1
~ Sagar Samrat is the Oil and Natural Gas Corporation's (ONGC) first offshore drilling rig, a jack-up oil platform, so option (1) is correct. Built in Japan and acquired by ONGC in 1973, it began drilling in the Arabian Sea off Mumbai and struck oil at Bombay High in February 1974, which opened up India's largest offshore oil field. Bombay High lies about 160 km west of Mumbai and remains a major source of India's domestic crude oil production. Sagar Samrat is not a research vessel; India's oceanographic research ships carry names such as Sagar Kanya and Sagar Nidhi, which may confuse candidates. It is not a dam either, and since option (1) is correct, "None of these" is wrong. Exam tip: Bombay High – offshore oil field discovered by Sagar Samrat in 1974; Digboi (Assam) is India's oldest oil field and refinery.

Q 97 | geo | Indian Geography
The largest river basin in India is
(1) Ganga
(2) Brahmaputra
(3) Godawari
(4) Indus
= 1
~ The Ganga basin is the largest river basin in India, covering about 8.6 lakh sq km, roughly a quarter (about 26%) of the country's area, so option (1) is correct. It spreads over Uttarakhand, Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, Haryana, Himachal Pradesh, Delhi, Bihar, Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh and West Bengal, and supports a very large share of India's population. The Indus basin is larger overall, but most of it lies in Pakistan; its Indian part is only about 3.2 lakh sq km. The Godavari basin, about 3.1 lakh sq km, is the largest basin in peninsular India, and Godavari is called the Dakshina Ganga. The Brahmaputra basin within India is smaller, about 1.9 lakh sq km, though the river carries a huge volume of water. Exam tip: largest basin – Ganga; largest peninsular basin – Godavari; longest peninsular river – Godavari (about 1,465 km).

Q 98 | geo | Indian Geography
Which state has Jute Industry ?
(1) Bihar
(2) Gujarat
(3) Maharashtra
(4) None of these
= 1
~ Among the options, only Bihar has a jute industry, so option (1) is correct. Jute is a fibre crop of the hot and humid Ganga–Brahmaputra delta region and needs fertile alluvial soil, high temperature and heavy rainfall. West Bengal is by far the leading state in jute cultivation and jute mills (mostly along the Hooghly river), followed by Bihar, Assam and Odisha. In Bihar, jute is grown in the Purnia, Katihar and Kishanganj region, and jute mills have operated at Katihar and Samastipur. Gujarat and Maharashtra are known for the cotton textile industry (Ahmedabad and Mumbai), not jute, so options (2) and (3) are wrong, and since Bihar fits, "None of these" is wrong. Jute is called the "golden fibre" and is used to make gunny bags, sacking, carpets and ropes. Exam tip: jute – West Bengal (Hooghly belt) first, then Bihar and Assam; cotton textiles – Maharashtra and Gujarat.

Q 99 | geo | Indian Geography
National Highway 2 connects the following cities
(1) Delhi to Kolkata
(2) Delhi to Mumbai
(3) Chennai to Bengaluru
(4) Agra to Chennai
= 1
~ Under the old numbering system, National Highway 2 ran from Delhi to Kolkata, so option (1) is correct. It passed through Faridabad, Mathura, Agra, Kanpur, Allahabad, Varanasi, Mohania, Aurangabad (Bihar), Dhanbad and Asansol, roughly following the historic Grand Trunk Road; about 1,465 km long, it formed part of the Golden Quadrilateral. After the highways were renumbered in 2010, the Delhi–Agra–Kolkata route became mainly NH 19, and the number NH 2 now belongs to a highway in north-east India. The other routes were different highways under the old system: Delhi–Mumbai was NH 8 (via Jaipur and Ahmedabad), Chennai–Bengaluru was NH 4 (now NH 48), and there is no single Agra–Chennai national highway; Agra–Mumbai was NH 3. Exam tip: the Grand Trunk Road was rebuilt by Sher Shah Suri; the Golden Quadrilateral links Delhi, Mumbai, Chennai and Kolkata.

Q 100 | geo | Indian Geography
Name the state where density of population is the lowest.
(1) Meghalaya
(2) Mizoram
(3) Arunachal Pradesh
(4) Sikkim
= 3
~ According to the 2011 Census, Arunachal Pradesh has the lowest population density among Indian states, only 17 persons per sq km, so option (3) is correct. It is the largest state in north-east India by area (about 83,743 sq km) but has a small population (about 13.8 lakh) spread over rugged, forested mountain terrain. The other options have higher densities: Mizoram about 52, Sikkim about 86 and Meghalaya about 132 persons per sq km. The national average in 2011 was 382 persons per sq km. At the other end, Bihar has the highest density among states (1,106 per sq km), and among union territories the National Capital Territory of Delhi has the highest (11,320 per sq km), while the Andaman and Nicobar Islands have the lowest (46). Exam tip: lowest density – Arunachal Pradesh (state), Andaman and Nicobar Islands (UT); highest – Bihar (state), Delhi (UT); India – 382.
