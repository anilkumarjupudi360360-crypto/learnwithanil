Q 1 | bio | Bacteria & Viruses
Bacterial cell walls contain which of the following types of structural polysaccharides?
(1) Chitin
(2) Hyaluronic acid
(3) Peptidoglycan
(4) Cellulose
= 3
~ Peptidoglycan (murein), made of alternating N-acetylglucosamine and N-acetylmuramic acid units cross-linked by short peptides, is the structural polysaccharide of bacterial cell walls. Chitin occurs in fungal walls and arthropod exoskeletons, cellulose in plant cell walls, and hyaluronic acid in animal connective tissue.

Q 2 | bio | Bacteria & Viruses
Select the option that is correct regarding the following two statements labelled Assertion (A) and Reason (R).
Assertion (A): Some bacteria demonstrate chemolithotrophy, which allows them to oxidise inorganic substances for energy production.
Reason (R): Bacteria lack mitochondria; hence they completely rely on glycolysis for ATP synthesis.
(1) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
(2) Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A .
(3) A is true, but R is false.
(4) A is false, but R is true.
= 3
~ The assertion is true: chemolithotrophs such as Nitrosomonas, Nitrobacter and Thiobacillus oxidise inorganic substances (NH3, NO2-, H2S, Fe2+) to obtain energy. The reason is false: bacteria do lack mitochondria, but they make ATP by respiration on the plasma membrane, by photophosphorylation or by fermentation, so they do not depend on glycolysis alone.

Q 4 | bio | Bacteria & Viruses
Which of the following Eubacteria is an example of a species that can survive in extreme environments through the formation of endospores?
(1) Bacillus subtilis
(2) Escherichia coli
(3) Staphylococcus aureus
(4) Pseudomonas aeruginosa
= 1
~ Bacillus subtilis (like other Bacillus and Clostridium species) forms endospores that resist heat, drying, radiation and chemicals, so it survives extreme conditions. E. coli, Staphylococcus aureus and Pseudomonas aeruginosa do not form endospores.

Q 5 | bio | Bacteria & Viruses
What is the term for the classification of Archaea based on their membrane property?
(1) Glycerol ether lipid classification
(2) Sugar classification
(3) Glycolipid classification
(4) Phospholipid classification
= 1
~ Archaeal membranes contain glycerol ether lipids, in which isoprenoid chains are joined to glycerol by ether bonds (diethers and tetraethers). Bacteria and eukaryotes instead have ester-linked fatty-acid phospholipids, so phospholipid is not the archaeal feature.

Q 8 | bio | Bacteria & Viruses
Which of the following types of inclusions is typically found in Eubacterial cells and serves as a storage site for carbon and energy?
(1) Glycogen granules
(2) Lipid inclusions
(3) Polyphosphate granules
(4) Carboxysomes
= 1
~ Glycogen granules are the carbon and energy reserve most commonly found in eubacteria such as E. coli and Bacillus. Polyphosphate (volutin) granules store phosphate and carboxysomes hold RuBisCO for CO2 fixation. Poly-beta-hydroxybutyrate (lipid) inclusions also store carbon and energy in some bacteria, which makes option 2 the tempting alternative. (Official key; some sources differ.)

Q 9 | bio | Bacteria & Viruses
Read the following statement and select the correct conclusion about the mode of nutrition of the bacterium without making assumptions.
Statement: A bacterium is only able to grow in a medium containing CO2, H2O and inorganic salts.
(1) It is a heterotroph.
(2) It is an autotroph.
(3) It is a mixotroph.
(4) It is a saprotroph.
= 2
~ A bacterium that grows on CO2, water and inorganic salts alone builds all its organic compounds from CO2, which is the definition of an autotroph (photo- or chemoautotroph). Heterotrophs and saprotrophs need ready-made organic carbon, and mixotrophs use both organic and inorganic sources, which the statement does not say.

Q 10 | bio | Bacteria & Viruses
Which of the following is a distinct group within the domain Archaea?
(1) Methanogens
(2) Cyanobacteria
(3) Chloroxybacteria
(4) Green sulphur bacteria
= 1
~ Methanogens (Methanobacterium, Methanococcus) form a distinct group of Archaea, along with extreme halophiles and thermoacidophiles. Cyanobacteria, chloroxybacteria (prochlorophytes) and green sulphur bacteria are all members of domain Bacteria.

Q 14 | bio | Bacteria & Viruses
What is the term for the process by which bacteria become temporarily dormant to resist the effect of antibiotics?
(1) Sporulation
(2) Stationary phase
(3) Persister cell formation
(4) Quorum sensing
= 3
~ Persister cells are a small fraction of a bacterial population that enter a temporary, non-growing dormant state and tolerate antibiotics without being genetically resistant; they resume growth once the drug is removed. Sporulation is a different, specialised survival process, stationary phase is a growth stage, and quorum sensing is cell-density signalling.

Q 15 | bio | Bacteria & Viruses
Which of the following is a nitrogen-fixing heterotrophic soil bacteria?
(1) Clostridium butyricum
(2) Pseudomonas aeruginosa
(3) Nitrosococcus nitrosus
(4) Nostoc commune
= 1
~ Clostridium butyricum is a free-living, anaerobic, heterotrophic soil bacterium that fixes atmospheric nitrogen. Nitrosococcus is a chemolithotrophic ammonia oxidiser, Nostoc is a photosynthetic nitrogen-fixing cyanobacterium (an autotroph), and Pseudomonas aeruginosa is not a significant nitrogen fixer.

Q 16 | bio | Bacteria & Viruses
Which of the following pairs of bacteria are used in the production of yogurt?
(1) Streptococcus thermophilus and Lactobacillus delbrueckii subsp. bulgaricus
(2) Lactobacillus acidophilus and Streptococcus thermophilus
(3) Bifidobacterium bifidum and Streptococcus thermophilus
(4) Escherichia coli and Streptococcus thermophilus
= 1,2
~ Yogurt is traditionally made with a starter of Streptococcus thermophilus and Lactobacillus delbrueckii subsp. bulgaricus (option 1). Lactobacillus acidophilus is also used with S. thermophilus in acidophilus-type yogurt, so APPSC treated the question as ambiguous and accepted options 1 and 2. E. coli is not a food starter, and Bifidobacterium is mainly added as a probiotic.

Q 18 | bio | Bacteria & Viruses
What type of virus infects bacteria?
(1) Virion
(2) Prion
(3) Bacteriophage
(4) Viroid
= 3
~ A bacteriophage is a virus that infects bacteria, for example T4 or lambda phage. A virion is any complete virus particle, a prion is an infectious protein, and a viroid is a naked RNA that infects plants.

Q 20 | bio | Bacteria & Viruses
Which of the following types of viral replication involves the synthesis of a complementary RNA strand from a DNA template?
(1) Positive-sense RNA replication
(2) Negative-sense RNA replication
(3) Reverse transcription
(4) Transcriptional activation
= 3
~ Reverse transcription is the distinctive replication step of retroviruses such as HIV, in which a DNA copy is made from the viral RNA, and it is the only listed option that involves both DNA and RNA in this way. Strictly, reverse transcriptase makes DNA from an RNA template, while making RNA from a DNA template is ordinary transcription, so the wording of the question is flawed. (Official key; some sources differ.)

Q 21 | bio | Bacteria & Viruses
What is the primary characteristic that distinguishes viruses from other microorganisms?
(1) Host-mediated replication
(2) DNA as genetic material
(3) RNA signalling
(4) Diverse speciation
= 1
~ Viruses are acellular and have no ribosomes or metabolism of their own, so they can multiply only inside a living host cell using the host's machinery. DNA as genetic material is not unique, since many viruses have RNA and all cellular microbes have DNA.

Q 24 | bio | Bacteria & Viruses
What is the term used to describe the viral genome that has been integrated into the host cell's genome?
(1) Prophage
(2) Lysogen
(3) Retrovirus
(4) Virusoid
= 1
~ A prophage is the genome of a bacteriophage integrated into the host bacterial chromosome during the lysogenic cycle and replicated along with it. A lysogen is the bacterium carrying the prophage, a retrovirus is a type of virus, and a virusoid is a small satellite RNA.

Q 25 | bio | Bacteria & Viruses
Which of the following terms indicates the state of specificity of a virus to a particular cell type, species or host?
(1) Antigenecity
(2) Viral tropism
(3) Retro-transcription
(4) Virulence
= 2
~ Viral tropism is the specificity of a virus for a particular host species, tissue or cell type, determined mainly by host receptors and cellular factors. Virulence is the degree of disease caused, and antigenicity is the ability to provoke an immune response.

Q 26 | bio | Bacteria & Viruses
What is the name of the viral enzyme produced by the Influenza A Virus that is responsible for the cleavage of the host cell's haemagglutinin protein?
(1) Neuraminidase
(2) Peptidase
(3) Lipase
(4) Serine dehydrogenase
= 1
~ Neuraminidase is the second major surface glycoprotein of influenza A (besides haemagglutinin); it cleaves sialic acid from host-cell glycoproteins, including haemagglutinin, so new virions can be released, and it is the target of oseltamivir and zanamivir. Peptidase, lipase and serine dehydrogenase are not influenza A enzymes. Strictly, cleavage of HA into HA1 and HA2 is done by host proteases, so the wording is loose. (Official key; some sources differ.)

Q 28 | bio | Bacteria & Viruses
One distinguishing feature of eukaryotic viruses is that:
(1) they can only replicate inside the host cell nucleus
(2) they are infected by bacteriophage
(3) they do not have capsid
(4) they do not have DNA
= 1
~ The keyed statement reflects the textbook view that many eukaryotic (animal) DNA viruses replicate their genome in the host nucleus. The other options are plainly false: bacteriophages infect bacteria, and viruses have a capsid and a DNA or RNA genome. Note that poxviruses and most RNA viruses replicate in the cytoplasm, so the statement is not universally true. (Official key; some sources differ.)

Q 29 | bio | Bacteria & Viruses
During infection, eukaryotic viruses attach themselves to certain _____ found on the surface of host cells.
(1) enzymes
(2) receptors
(3) DNA
(4) ribosomes
= 2
~ Viruses begin infection by binding their surface proteins to specific receptor molecules on the host-cell surface, which is also what decides host and tissue range. Enzymes, DNA and ribosomes are not the attachment sites.

Q 30 | bio | Bacteria & Viruses
Which of the following is a primary characteristic that defines a virus?
(1) Obligate parasitism
(2) Symbionts
(3) Endoparasitism
(4) Endosymbionts
= 1
~ The defining feature of a virus is obligate intracellular parasitism: it cannot reproduce outside a living host cell. Symbionts and endosymbionts live in mutually beneficial association, and endoparasitism is not unique to viruses because many bacteria, protozoa and worms also live inside hosts.

Q 31 | bio | Bacteria & Viruses
What structural components are found in an icosahedral capsid?
(1) Pentameres and tetrameres
(2) Hexameres and octameres
(3) Pentameres and hexameres
(4) Tetrameres and octameres
= 3
~ An icosahedral capsid has 20 triangular faces, 12 vertices and 30 edges and is built from capsomeres. The 12 vertices are occupied by pentamers (five subunits) while hexamers (six subunits) fill the faces and edges.

Q 32 | bio | Bacteria & Viruses
Which of the following chromatography techniques is commonly used for the purification of viruses?
(1) Ion exchange chromatography
(2) Size exclusion chromatography
(3) Affinity chromatography
(4) Hydrophobic interaction chromatography
= 1,2
~ Ion-exchange and size-exclusion (gel filtration) chromatography are both widely used to purify viruses and viral vectors, usually after ultracentrifugation or filtration. Affinity and hydrophobic-interaction chromatography are also used for some viruses, but APPSC treated the question as ambiguous and gave credit only for option 1 or option 2. (Official key; some sources differ.)

Q 33 | bio | Bacteria & Viruses
What is the mechanism of action of the viral reverse transcriptase?
(1) The viral reverse transcriptase synthesizes double-stranded DNA from a single-stranded RNA template.
(2) The viral reverse transcriptase synthesizes single-stranded RNA from a single-stranded DNA template.
(3) The viral reverse transcriptase synthesizes double-stranded DNA from a single-stranded DNA template.
(4) The viral reverse transcriptase synthesizes double-stranded RNA from a single-stranded RNA template.
= 1
~ Reverse transcriptase (an RNA-dependent DNA polymerase) of retroviruses such as HIV copies the single-stranded RNA genome into complementary DNA, removes the RNA with its RNase H activity and then makes the second DNA strand. The result is double-stranded DNA that can integrate into the host genome; the enzyme does not make RNA.

Q 34 | bio | Cell Biology
Which part of the cell contains ecto-enzymes?
(1) Cell wall
(2) Mitochondria
(3) Centrosome
(4) Plasma membrane
= 4
~ Ecto-enzymes have their active site on the outer face of the plasma membrane, where they act on substances outside the cell (for example 5'-nucleotidase and ecto-ATPases). Mitochondria and the centrosome are intracellular, and the cell wall is not a membrane.

Q 35 | bio | Cell Biology
Which of the following is majorly present as the primary component in the middle lamella?
(1) Calcium pectate
(2) Arabinogalactans
(3) Cellulose micelles
(4) Glucan units
= 1
~ The middle lamella is the cementing layer between neighbouring plant cells and is made chiefly of calcium (and magnesium) pectate. Cellulose micelles, arabinogalactans and glucan units belong to the primary or secondary wall layers, not to the main body of the middle lamella.

Q 36 | bio | Cell Biology
What is the name of the protein complex that mediates the transport of proteins from the endoplasmic reticulum to the Golgi apparatus?
(1) COPII
(2) COPI
(3) SNARE
(4) Clathrin
= 1
~ COPII-coated vesicles bud from ER exit sites and carry cargo forward to the ER-Golgi intermediate compartment and the cis-Golgi. COPI mediates retrograde transport back to the ER, clathrin acts at the trans-Golgi network and plasma membrane, and SNAREs control vesicle fusion rather than forming the coat.

Q 37 | bio | Cell Biology
What is the primary function of the plasmalemma in plant cells, and how does it differ from the tonoplast?
(1) The plasmalemma regulates ion transport and signaling, while the tonoplast regulates vacuolar pH and ion homeostasis.
(2) The plasmalemma regulates vacuolar pH and ion homeostasis, while the tonoplast regulates ion transport and signaling.
(3) The plasmalemma regulates cell wall synthesis and deposition, while the tonoplast regulates vacuolar protein degradation.
(4) The plasmalemma regulates vacuolar protein degradation, while the tonoplast regulates cell wall synthesis and deposition.
= 1
~ The plasmalemma (plasma membrane) controls exchange between the cell and its surroundings, including ion transport and signal reception. The tonoplast bounds the vacuole and, with H+ pumps such as V-ATPase, regulates vacuolar pH and ion homeostasis. The other options swap or misassign these roles.

Q 38 | bio | Cell Biology
Which of the following elements is NOT present in a primary cell wall?
(1) Pectin
(2) Microfibril
(3) Hemicellulose
(4) Lignin
= 4
~ The primary cell wall consists of cellulose microfibrils embedded in a matrix of hemicellulose and pectin. Lignin is laid down later in the secondary wall of xylem and sclerenchyma cells to give rigidity and waterproofing.

Q 39 | bio | Cell Biology
Which cell organelle is responsible for lysosome formation?
(1) Endoplasmic reticulum
(2) Plasma membrane
(3) Golgi apparatus
(4) Cytoplasmic vacuoles
= 3
~ Lysosomal hydrolases are made on the rough ER, tagged with mannose-6-phosphate in the Golgi apparatus, and packaged into vesicles that bud from the trans-Golgi network to become lysosomes. The ER makes the enzymes but does not form the lysosomes.

Q 40 | bio | Cell Biology
Which of the following routes of cellular transport in plant cells involve the function of plasmodesmata?
(1) Symplastic route
(2) Apoplastic route
(3) Transmembrane route
(4) Extracellular route
= 1
~ Plasmodesmata are membrane-lined cytoplasmic channels that pass through the walls of adjacent plant cells, so they provide the symplastic route, from cytoplasm to cytoplasm. The apoplastic route runs through cell walls and intercellular spaces without crossing membranes.

Q 41 | bio | Cell Biology
What is the primary function of the tonoplast in plant cells?
(1) To regulate the transport of ions and metabolites across the vacuolar membrane
(2) To regulate protein folding in the ER
(3) To provide structural support and maintain cell shape
(4) To induce cytosolic trafficking
= 1
~ The tonoplast is the single membrane around the central vacuole of a plant cell. Its pumps, channels and carriers (V-ATPase, H+-pyrophosphatase, antiporters) control the movement of ions and metabolites into and out of the vacuole. It does not fold ER proteins or provide structural support.

Q 42 | bio | Cell Biology
What is the function of the cristae in mitochondria?
(1) The cristae are involved in the synthesis of ATP and provide increased surface area of inner membrane.
(2) The cristae are involved in the synthesis of NADH, and they increase the surface area of the inner membrane.
(3) The cristae are involved in the citric acid cycle, and they increase the surface area of the inner membrane.
(4) The cristae are involved in the electron transport chain, and they increase the surface area of the outer membrane.
= 1
~ Cristae are folds of the inner mitochondrial membrane that carry the electron transport chain and ATP synthase, so they enlarge the surface for ATP synthesis. NADH is produced mainly in the matrix by the citric acid cycle, and the area increased is that of the inner, not the outer, membrane.

Q 43 | bio | Cell Biology
Which of the following enzymes is NOT typically found in lysosomes?
(1) Acid phosphatase
(2) Cathepsin D
(3) Lysosomal acid lipase
(4) Alkaline phosphatase
= 4
~ Lysosomal enzymes are acid hydrolases that work at about pH 4.5 to 5, such as acid phosphatase, cathepsins and lysosomal acid lipase. Alkaline phosphatase works at alkaline pH and is found mainly on cell membranes, not in lysosomes.

Q 44 | bio | Cell Biology
Which of the following enzymes is typically found in the Golgi body?
(1) Protease
(2) Glycosidase
(3) Phospholipase
(4) ATPase
= 2
~ The Golgi body modifies glycoproteins with glycosyltransferases and glycosidases (for example mannosidases that trim N-linked sugar chains). Phospholipases and general proteases are characteristic of lysosomes, and ATPase is typical of mitochondria and membrane pumps.

Q 45 | bio | Cell Biology
Read the statement carefully and determine which conclusion logically follows without making assumptions.
Statement: The plasma membrane is dynamic and can change its shape and structure in response to various stimuli.
Which of the conclusion is correct?
(1) This involves the movement of molecules across the membrane in response to various stimuli.
(2) The components of the membrane are rigid and do not reform in response to external signal.
(3) The components of the membrane are broken down by degrading enzymes.
(4) The component lipids and proteins of the membrane can change in response to external signal.
= 1,4
~ A dynamic plasma membrane (fluid mosaic model) means that its lipids and proteins can rearrange, and that molecules move across or within it, in response to signals. Both conclusions 1 and 4 follow from this, so APPSC accepted either. Rigid, non-reforming components (2) contradict the statement, and enzymatic breakdown (3) is not implied.

Q 46 | bio | Cell Biology
Ribophorins I and II present in the rough endoplasmic reticulum (RER) are __________.
(1) phospholipids
(2) glycoproteins
(3) polypeptides
(4) steroids
= 2
~ Ribophorins I and II are integral glycoproteins of the rough ER membrane that bind the large ribosomal subunit and help hold ribosomes on the ER during translation. They are proteins, not phospholipids or steroids.

Q 48 | bio | Cell Biology
What is the primary function of the Arp2/3 complex in the regulation of actin filament dynamics?
(1) To stabilise existing actin filaments
(2) To nucleate branched actin filaments
(3) To enhance Rho GTPase activity
(4) To enhance actin-tubulin interaction
= 2
~ The Arp2/3 complex binds to the side of an existing actin filament and nucleates a new filament at about 70 degrees, producing branched actin networks such as those in lamellipodia. Formins, not Arp2/3, nucleate unbranched filaments, and stabilisation is done by other proteins.

Q 49 | bio | Cell Biology
What is the function of nucleosomes?
(1) To unwind DNA
(2) To condense DNA
(3) To denature DNA
(4) To replicate DNA
= 2
~ A nucleosome is about 146 bp of DNA wrapped around a histone octamer (two each of H2A, H2B, H3 and H4), and it is the basic unit of chromatin packaging that condenses DNA. Unwinding is done by helicases and replication by DNA polymerases, and nucleosomes do neither.

Q 50 | bio | Cell Biology
During what stage of the cell cycle, does the size of each daughter centriole reach maturity?
(1) Metaphase
(2) Late prophase
(3) Anaphase
(4) Telophase
= 2
~ Centrioles duplicate once per cycle: a daughter procentriole appears next to each mother centriole in S phase, lengthens through G2 and is generally described as reaching full size by late prophase, when the duplicated centrosomes move apart to form the spindle poles. Textbooks vary on whether this is late G2 or late prophase. (Official key; some sources differ.)

Q 51 | bio | Cell Biology
Which of the following types of chromatin is characterised by a high degree of compaction and a low gene expression?
(1) Euchromatin
(2) Heterochromatin
(3) Chromatin fibres
(4) Nucleosomes
= 2
~ Heterochromatin is densely compacted, stains darkly and is mostly transcriptionally silent, whereas euchromatin is loosely packed and active. Chromatin fibres and nucleosomes are general structural terms, not compaction states linked with low gene expression.

Q 52 | bio | Molecular Biology
Based on the given statement, which of the following conclusions is correct?
Statement: Nucleosomes can be modified by the addition of histone modifications
(1) Nucleosomes are important for regulating gene expression.
(2) Nucleosomes are non-active regions of chromosome.
(3) Nucleosomes are important for mitosis.
(4) Nucleosomes do not undergo gene silencing.
= 1
~ Histone modifications such as acetylation, methylation and phosphorylation change how tightly nucleosomes pack DNA and which regulatory proteins can bind, so nucleosomes help regulate gene expression. Nucleosomes occur along all chromatin (not only inactive regions) and can take part in gene silencing, so options 2 and 4 are wrong.

Q 53 | bio | Cell Biology
What is the primary function of the nuclear lamina in the nuclear membrane?
(1) To regulate gene expression
(2) To facilitate DNA replication
(3) To maintain the structure of the nucleus
(4) To control the movement of molecules in and out of the nucleus
= 3
