# NCERT Class 12 Fine Arts (Art & Culture), An Introduction to Indian Art Part II, Chapter 1: The Manuscript Painting Tradition
# Questions written by Learn with Anil from the chapter (current NCERT edition, Reprint 2026-27). 16 Nov 2026.

Q 1 | art | An Introduction to Indian Art, Part II
Manuscript illustrations, as described in the chapter, are:
(1) Paintings made directly on the walls of temples and palaces
(2) Pictorial translations of verses from epics and canonical, literary, bardic or music texts, with the verses written in a box at the top or on the back
(3) Printed pictures pasted into books
(4) Large cloth scrolls used by storytellers
= 2
~ A manuscript illustration translates a verse into a picture; the verse is handwritten in a demarcated box at the top of the painting, or sometimes on its reverse.

Q 2 | art | An Introduction to Indian Art, Part II
Why are these paintings called 'miniatures'?
(1) Because cheap pigments were used
(2) Because they were painted by apprentices
(3) Because they were made in small towns
(4) Because of their small size: they were held in the hand and viewed closely
= 4
~ Miniatures are small, meant to be held in the hand and seen up close, not hung on walls, where murals were used instead.

Q 3 | art | An Introduction to Indian Art, Part II
In a set of manuscript paintings, the colophon page records:
(1) The patron, the artist or scribe, and the date and place of commission or completion
(2) The price paid for the set
(3) A list of the pigments used
(4) The names of the owners who later bought the set
= 1
~ The colophon gives the patron, artist or scribe, date and place. Colophons are often missing, so undated sets are given hypothetical dates on the basis of style.

Q 4 | art | An Introduction to Indian Art, Part II
Early Jain paintings were made on palm leaves. In which century was paper introduced?
(1) Tenth century
(2) Twelfth century
(3) Fourteenth century
(4) Sixteenth century
= 3
~ Paper was introduced in the fourteenth century. The earliest surviving palm-leaf manuscript from western India dates to the eleventh century.

Q 5 | art | An Introduction to Indian Art, Part II
Palm-leaf manuscripts were held together by a string through a central hole and protected by wooden covers called:
(1) Pothis
(2) Bhandars
(3) Patlis
(4) Patas
= 3
~ The wooden covers were called patlis. Bhandars were the monastery libraries where such manuscripts were kept.

Q 6 | art | An Introduction to Indian Art, Part II
Among the Jains, donating illustrated books to monastery libraries (bhandars) was considered a charitable act called:
(1) Shaastradaan
(2) Annadaan
(3) Bhoodan
(4) Vidyadaan
= 1
~ Shaastradaan, the gift of books, was an act of charity. The largely Jain merchant class of Gujarat, prosperous from its ports and trade routes, patronised such manuscripts.

Q 7 | art | An Introduction to Indian Art, Part II
The Kalpasutra illustrates five key incidents in the lives of the Tirthankaras. Which of the following is NOT one of them?
(1) Conception
(2) Birth
(3) Renunciation
(4) Coronation
= 4
~ The five incidents are conception, birth, renunciation, enlightenment and first sermon, and salvation. The Kalpasutra covers the lives of the 24 Tirthankaras.

Q 8 | art | An Introduction to Indian Art, Part II
In the Kalpasutra, Queen Trishala dreams of how many auspicious objects before the birth of Mahavira?
(1) 8
(2) 14
(3) 16
(4) 24
= 2
~ She dreams of 14 objects, including an elephant, a bull, a tiger, the goddess Shri, a kalash, a heap of jewels, the Sun and the Moon. An astrologer foretells that her son will be a sovereign or a great saint.

Q 9 | art | An Introduction to Indian Art, Part II
The Kalakacharyakatha narrates the story of:
(1) Acharya Kalaka, who rescues his abducted sister, a Jain nun, from an evil king
(2) Mahavira's teachings on the conduct of monks
(3) The structure of the universe
(4) The 24 Tirthankaras' previous births
= 1
~ The Kalakacharyakatha tells how Acharya Kalaka rescued his sister from an evil king. Mahavira's teachings on monks' conduct are in the Uttaradhyana Sutra.

Q 10 | art | An Introduction to Indian Art, Part II
The Sangrahini Sutra is:
(1) A book of recipes painted at Mandu
(2) A twelfth-century cosmological text describing the structure of the universe and the mapping of space
(3) Mahavira's teachings on the conduct of monks
(4) A Buddhist text in 8,000 lines
= 2
~ The Sangrahini Sutra, composed in the twelfth century, is a cosmological text. The chapter also mentions Tirthapatas and mandalas among Jain subjects.

Q 11 | art | An Introduction to Indian Art, Part II
Which of the following is NOT a feature of the Western Indian (Jain) style of manuscript painting?
(1) Thin, wiry lines
(2) Bright colours and lavish use of gold and lapis lazuli
(3) A further eye added to suggest a three-dimensional face
(4) Naturalistic, detailed landscapes
= 4
~ The style is schematic, with thin wiry lines, bright colours, interest in textile patterns and an extra projecting eye; its landscapes are suggestive rather than detailed.

Q 12 | art | An Introduction to Indian Art, Part II
The most creative phase of the Western Indian style of painting was about:
(1) 1050–1150
(2) 1200–1300
(3) 1350–1450
(4) 1550–1650
= 3
~ From about 1350 to 1450 the style moved beyond iconic images to landscapes, dancing figures and musicians in the margins.

Q 13 | art | An Introduction to Indian Art, Part II
In the indigenous (pre-Mughal) style of the late fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, seen in sets such as the Chaurpanchashika, the heroine's odhni (veil) is typically shown:
(1) Hanging limp to the ground
(2) Ballooned out around her head, with stiff, standing edges
(3) Wrapped as a turban
(4) Omitted altogether
= 2
~ Ballooned odhnis with stiff edges, suggestive architecture and formalised hatching for water, horizons and plants mark this style, and continue into early Rajasthani painting.

Q 14 | art | An Introduction to Indian Art, Part II
The Nimatnama (Book of Delicacies) was painted at:
(1) Jaunpur, under the Sharqi sultans
(2) Ahmedabad, under Mahmud Begada
(3) Bijapur, under Ibrahim Adil Shah II
(4) Mandu, during the reign of Nasir Shah Khalji
= 4
~ The Nimatnama was painted at Mandu during the reign of Nasir Shah Khalji (1500–1510), an example of the Sultanate school's blend of Persian and indigenous elements.

Q 15 | art | An Introduction to Indian Art, Part II
The Nimatnama is a book on:
(1) Recipes, along with hunting, medicines, cosmetics and perfumes
(2) The lives of Sufi saints
(3) Astronomy and astrology
(4) Ragas and raginis
= 1
~ It is a book of recipes that also covers hunting, the making of medicines, cosmetics and perfumes.

Q 16 | art | An Introduction to Indian Art, Part II
Which of the following illustrated works is noted in the chapter for its Sufi undertones?
(1) Kalpasutra
(2) Sangrahini Sutra
(3) Laurchanda
(4) Astasahasrika Prajnaparamita

Q 17 | art | An Introduction to Indian Art, Part II
Pala manuscripts showing Vajrayana deities on palm leaves were produced at which great Buddhist centres of learning?
(1) Ajanta and Ellora
(2) Sanchi and Sarnath
(3) Taxila and Takht-i-Bahi
(4) Nalanda and Vikramshila
= 4
~ Nalanda and Vikramshila produced illustrated Buddhist manuscripts and had bronze-casting workshops. Students and pilgrims carried Pala art to Nepal, Tibet, Burma, Sri Lanka and Java.

Q 18 | art | An Introduction to Indian Art, Part II
The Astasahasrika Prajnaparamita ('Perfection of Wisdom' in 8,000 lines) in the Bodleian Library was painted at Nalanda in the fifteenth year of the reign of which Pala king?
(1) Ramapala
(2) Dharmapala
(3) Devapala
(4) Gopala
= 1
~ It was painted in the fifteenth year of Ramapala's reign, in the last quarter of the eleventh century; it has six pages of illustrations and wooden covers painted on both sides.

Q 19 | art | An Introduction to Indian Art, Part II
Pala painting is characterised by:
(1) Angular lines and bright primary colours
(2) Flowing, sinuous lines and subdued tones, close to Pala sculpture and the Ajanta murals
(3) European-style perspective
(4) Heavy gold backgrounds and Persian skies
= 2
~ Pala paintings have sinuous lines and subdued colours, similar in style to Pala sculpture and to Ajanta. The Pala period (about 750 CE to the mid-twelfth century) was the last great phase of Buddhist art in India.

Q 20 | art | An Introduction to Indian Art, Part II
Pala art came to an end in the first half of the thirteenth century because:
(1) Paper replaced palm leaf
(2) The Mughals conquered Bengal
(3) Muslim invaders attacked and destroyed the Buddhist monasteries
(4) Floods destroyed Nalanda
= 3
~ The chapter says Pala art ended when invaders attacked and destroyed the monasteries in the first half of the thirteenth century.
