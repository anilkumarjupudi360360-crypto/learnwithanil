Q 1 | his | Themes in Indian History, Part II
The Ain-i Akbari, the main source for the agrarian history of the Mughal empire, was written by:
(1) Badauni
(2) Babur
(3) Abul Fazl, Akbar's court historian
(4) Al-Biruni
= 3
~ It was completed in 1598 and forms the third part of the Akbar Nama. It records arrangements for the empire's administration, revenue and agriculture.

Q 2 | his | Themes in Indian History, Part II
Indo-Persian sources of the Mughal period commonly used which terms for a peasant?
(1) Zamindar and jagirdar
(2) Raiyat (plural riaya), muzarian, kisan or asami
(3) Mansabdar and amil
(4) Patwari and muqaddam
= 2
~ Sources of the seventeenth century refer to two kinds of peasants: khud-kashta and pahi-kashta.

Q 3 | his | Themes in Indian History, Part II
"Khud-kashta" peasants were those who:
(1) Were non-resident cultivators who farmed land elsewhere on a contractual basis
(2) Were landless labourers
(3) Were residents of the village in which they held their lands
(4) Were village headmen
= 3
~ Pahi-kashta peasants were non-resident cultivators who belonged to some other village. They moved for better terms or because of famine.

Q 4 | his | Themes in Indian History, Part II
Which of the following crops were introduced into India from the New World during the Mughal period?
(1) Wheat and rice
(2) Cotton and sugarcane
(3) Indigo and opium
(4) Maize, tomatoes, potatoes and chillies
= 4
~ Maize came through Africa and Spain, and by the seventeenth century it was listed among the major crops of western India.

Q 5 | his | Themes in Indian History, Part II
In Mughal sources, "jins-i kamil" (perfect crops) referred to:
(1) Cash crops such as cotton and sugarcane, which brought more revenue to the state
(2) Wheat and barley grown for food
(3) Fruits from Central Asia
(4) Crops grown only in Bengal
= 1
~ The state encouraged cultivation of such crops. Oilseeds and pulses were also cash crops.

Q 6 | his | Themes in Indian History, Part II
Match the categories of land in the Ain-i Akbari:
A. Polaj        1. Land left out of cultivation for five years or more
B. Parauti      2. Land annually cultivated, never left fallow
C. Chachar      3. Land left fallow for a time to recover its strength
D. Banjar       4. Land that had lain fallow for three or four years
(1) A-1, B-2, C-3, D-4
(2) A-3, B-4, C-1, D-2
(3) A-4, B-1, C-2, D-3
(4) A-2, B-3, C-4, D-1
= 4
~ The classification was used to assess land revenue. Land in each category was further graded into good, middling and bad.

Q 7 | his | Themes in Indian History, Part II
The village panchayat in Mughal times was headed by a person known as:
(1) Patwari
(2) Muqaddam or mandal
(3) Zamindar
(4) Qazi
= 2
~ The headman supervised the preparation of village accounts with the help of the accountant (patwari). The panchayat dealt with disputes and ensured that caste boundaries were maintained.

Q 8 | his | Themes in Indian History, Part II
In Mughal revenue terms, "jama" and "hasil" referred to:
(1) Jama - land tax on wheat; hasil - tax on rice
(2) Jama - the amount of revenue assessed; hasil - the amount actually collected
(3) Jama - tax on trade; hasil - tax on cattle
(4) Both meant the same thing
= 2
~ Akbar's revenue minister Todar Mal carried out careful surveys of crop yields, prices and areas under cultivation over ten years, which formed the basis of the revenue system.

Q 9 | his | Themes in Indian History, Part II
The zamindars of the Mughal period were:
(1) Landless labourers
(2) Officials appointed only for one year
(3) Moneylenders without land
(4) People who held extensive personal lands (milkiyat) and had the right to collect revenue from peasants on behalf of the state
= 4
~ Zamindars also had military resources, such as forts and armed retainers. They were an exploitative class, but their relationship with the peasants also had elements of reciprocity and patronage.

Q 10 | his | Themes in Indian History, Part II
Babur described a device used for irrigation in the northern Indian plains, where a wheel lifted water from a well. This was the:
(1) Persian wheel (saqiya)
(2) Canal lock
(3) Water mill
(4) Shaduf only
= 1
~ The Mughal state also repaired old canals, such as the Shah Nahr in Punjab, and dug new ones.

Q 11 | his | Themes in Indian History, Part II
Women in peasant households in Mughal India were involved in:
1. Sowing, weeding, threshing and winnowing the harvest
2. Spinning yarn, sifting and kneading clay for pottery, and embroidery
Which of the statements given above is/are correct?
(1) 1 only
(2) 2 only
(3) Neither 1 nor 2
(4) Both 1 and 2
= 4
~ Women's labour was valued, but they were also controlled by family and community. Among landed gentry, women had the right to inherit property; in Bengal, some women zamindars are known.

Q 12 | his | Themes in Indian History, Part II
The "jajmani" system referred to:
(1) A tax collected by the state
(2) A military rank
(3) An arrangement in which artisans and service providers were paid by peasants with a share of the harvest or land for their services
(4) A type of land grant to temples
= 3
~ Village artisans such as potters, blacksmiths and carpenters were part of village life. In Bengal, zamindars paid such service groups.

Q 13 | his | Themes in Indian History, Part II
Forest dwellers in Mughal India were described in contemporary texts as "jangli". This term:
(1) Did not mean an absence of civilisation; it described people who lived by gathering forest produce, hunting and shifting agriculture
(2) Meant that they were violent
(3) Meant that they had no contact with the state
(4) Was used for city dwellers
= 1
~ Forest people supplied products like honey, beeswax and gum lac. The state demanded peshkash (gifts), often including elephants, from them.

Q 14 | his | Themes in Indian History, Part II
Which official was responsible for revenue collection in a Mughal district?
(1) The kotwal
(2) The amil-guzar
(3) The qazi
(4) The mir bakhshi
= 2
~ Akbar ordered that the amil-guzar should collect revenue in cash, though payment in kind was also allowed.

Q 15 | his | Themes in Indian History, Part II
The flow of silver into India in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries was mainly due to:
(1) Mining in the Deccan
(2) Tribute from Central Asia
(3) Expanding world trade, as payment for Indian goods, much of it silver from the Americas
(4) The fall of the Mughals
= 3
~ India had few silver mines of its own. The flow of silver allowed the Mughal state to collect revenue in cash. The Italian traveller Giovanni Careri described this flow around 1690.

Q 16 | his | Themes in Indian History, Part II
Which of the following best describes the structure of the Ain-i Akbari?
(1) It is a single volume of poetry
(2) It is divided into five books (daftars); the first three describe the administration
(3) It is a list of temples
(4) It is a translation of the Mahabharata
= 2
~ The first book deals with the imperial household, the second with the military and civil administration, and the third with fiscal matters. The fourth and fifth deal with the people of India and with Akbar's sayings.

Q 17 | his | Themes in Indian History, Part II
In the Mughal period, agriculture was based mainly on:
(1) Two major crop seasons, kharif (autumn) and rabi (spring), and in some places three
(2) Only one crop a year everywhere
(3) Only cash crops
(4) Only rice
= 1
~ Most places with good rain or irrigation grew at least two crops. Agriculture was organised around these seasons.

Q 18 | his | Themes in Indian History, Part II
Which of the following is a limitation of the Ain-i Akbari as a source, noted by historians?
(1) It contains no numbers
(2) It was written by a foreigner
(3) It was written centuries after Akbar
(4) It has errors in totals, the data is uneven across regions, and it describes the empire from the point of view of the court
= 4
~ Even with its limitations, the Ain gives detailed data that is unique for its time. Historians also use village records and East India Company documents.

Q 19 | his | Themes in Indian History, Part II
Which statement about the village community in the Mughal period is correct?
(1) Villages were cut off from markets and towns
(2) Villages had no headmen
(3) Villages were linked to wider markets through trade in cash crops, and payment of revenue in cash
(4) Villages paid no revenue
= 3
~ The British once described Indian villages as "little republics" unaffected by change, but the Mughal evidence shows that they were connected to the wider economy.

Q 20 | his | Themes in Indian History, Part II
The Mughal state's interest in expanding cultivation is shown by:
(1) Its use of tax concessions and loans to encourage cultivators to extend agriculture to new lands
(2) Its policy of leaving land uncultivated
(3) Its ban on irrigation
(4) Its refusal to collect revenue
= 1
~ Agricultural expansion meant more revenue for the state. The state also had an interest in protecting peasants from excessive demands.
