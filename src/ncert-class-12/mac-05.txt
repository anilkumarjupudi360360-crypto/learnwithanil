# NCERT Class 12 Economics, Introductory Macroeconomics, Chapter 5: Government Budget and the Economy
# Questions written by Learn with Anil from the chapter (current NCERT edition, Reprint 2026-27). 16 Nov 2026.

Q 1 | eco | Introductory Macroeconomics
Under which Article of the Constitution is the 'Annual Financial Statement', the main budget document, laid before Parliament?
(1) Article 110
(2) Article 112
(3) Article 280
(4) Article 266
= 2
~ Article 112 requires an Annual Financial Statement of the estimated receipts and expenditure of the Government of India to be laid before Parliament every financial year.

Q 2 | eco | Introductory Macroeconomics
Public goods such as national defence and roads are:
(1) Non-rivalrous and non-excludable
(2) Rivalrous and excludable
(3) Rivalrous but non-excludable
(4) Excludable but non-rivalrous only
= 1
~ One person's use of a public good does not reduce its availability to others (non-rivalrous), and it is hard to keep anyone from using it (non-excludable).

Q 3 | eco | Introductory Macroeconomics
The 'free-rider problem' with public goods means that:
(1) Public goods are always free
(2) The government gives free transport
(3) Private firms produce too many public goods
(4) People can enjoy a public good without paying for it, so markets cannot collect fees and do not provide such goods well
= 4
~ Since non-payers cannot be excluded, people may avoid paying while still benefiting. That is why public goods are provided by the government and paid for through taxes.

Q 4 | eco | Introductory Macroeconomics
When the government provides national defence, roads and administration, which function of the budget is it performing?
(1) Redistribution function
(2) Stabilisation function
(3) Allocation function
(4) Regulation function
= 3
~ The allocation function is the provision of public goods, which the market would not supply adequately.

Q 5 | eco | Introductory Macroeconomics
Through taxes and transfer payments, the government changes the distribution of disposable income to make it fairer. This is the:
(1) Allocation function
(2) Stabilisation function
(3) Redistribution function
(4) Public provision of goods
= 3
~ The redistribution function uses taxes and transfers to bring the distribution of personal disposable income closer to what society considers fair.

Q 6 | eco | Introductory Macroeconomics
The stabilisation function of the government budget refers to:
(1) Fixing the exchange rate permanently
(2) Keeping tax rates unchanged
(3) Providing public goods
(4) Intervening to reduce fluctuations in output and employment, for example by raising spending when demand is low
= 4
~ Output and employment fluctuate with aggregate demand. The government can raise or cut spending and taxes to steady the economy.

Q 7 | eco | Introductory Macroeconomics
Which of the following is a revenue receipt of the government?
(1) Borrowing from the RBI
(2) Dividends and profits from public sector enterprises
(3) Proceeds from disinvestment
(4) Recovery of loans given to state governments
= 2
~ Revenue receipts neither create liabilities nor reduce assets: taxes and non-tax revenue such as dividends, profits, interest receipts and fees. The other options are capital receipts.

Q 8 | eco | Introductory Macroeconomics
Which of the following is a direct tax?
(1) Corporation tax
(2) Goods and Services Tax
(3) Customs duty
(4) Excise duty
= 1
~ Direct taxes, such as personal income tax and corporation tax, are paid by the person or firm on whom they are imposed. GST, customs and excise are indirect taxes on goods and services.

Q 9 | eco | Introductory Macroeconomics
Capital receipts of the government are receipts that:
(1) Create a liability or reduce the financial assets of the government
(2) Come only from taxes
(3) Neither create liabilities nor reduce assets
(4) Are spent only on salaries
= 1
~ Borrowing creates a liability to repay; recovery of loans and disinvestment reduce assets. All of these are capital receipts.

Q 10 | eco | Introductory Macroeconomics
Which of the following is a non-debt creating capital receipt?
(1) Market borrowing
(2) Proceeds from disinvestment
(3) Borrowing from foreign governments
(4) Borrowing from the RBI
= 2
~ Disinvestment and recovery of loans bring in capital without creating any debt. Borrowings of every kind create debt.

Q 11 | eco | Introductory Macroeconomics
Which of the following is revenue expenditure?
(1) Building a new highway
(2) Purchase of machinery
(3) Loans to state governments
(4) Interest payments on government debt
= 4
~ Revenue expenditure does not create physical or financial assets: salaries, pensions, subsidies, grants and interest payments. The other options are capital expenditure.

Q 12 | eco | Introductory Macroeconomics
Which of the following is capital expenditure of the Union government?
(1) Salaries of government employees
(2) Pensions
(3) Loans and advances to state governments
(4) Subsidies
= 3
~ Capital expenditure creates physical or financial assets or reduces financial liabilities. A loan to a state government creates a financial asset for the Union government.

Q 13 | eco | Introductory Macroeconomics
Revenue expenditure is ₹1,000 crore and revenue receipts are ₹800 crore. The revenue deficit is:
(1) ₹1,800 crore
(2) ₹800 crore
(3) ₹1,000 crore
(4) ₹200 crore
= 4
~ Revenue deficit = revenue expenditure − revenue receipts = 1,000 − 800 = ₹200 crore.

Q 14 | eco | Introductory Macroeconomics
Fiscal deficit is defined as:
(1) Total expenditure − (revenue receipts + non-debt creating capital receipts)
(2) Revenue expenditure − revenue receipts
(3) Total expenditure − total receipts including borrowing
(4) Interest payments − revenue receipts
= 1
~ Fiscal deficit is the gap between what the government spends and what it receives without borrowing. It equals the government's net borrowing requirement.

Q 15 | eco | Introductory Macroeconomics
Total expenditure is ₹1,500 crore, revenue receipts are ₹800 crore and non-debt creating capital receipts are ₹100 crore. What is the fiscal deficit?
(1) ₹700 crore
(2) ₹600 crore
(3) ₹800 crore
(4) ₹1,400 crore
= 2
~ Fiscal deficit = 1,500 − (800 + 100) = ₹600 crore.

Q 16 | eco | Introductory Macroeconomics
The fiscal deficit is ₹600 crore and interest payments are ₹250 crore. What is the primary deficit?
(1) ₹850 crore
(2) ₹600 crore
(3) ₹350 crore
(4) ₹250 crore
= 3
~ Primary deficit = fiscal deficit − interest payments = 600 − 250 = ₹350 crore.

Q 17 | eco | Introductory Macroeconomics
What does the fiscal deficit show?
(1) The government's total borrowing requirement from all sources
(2) Only the interest burden of the government
(3) The government's profit
(4) The trade deficit of the country
= 1
~ The fiscal deficit has to be financed by borrowing, from the RBI, from the market or from abroad. So it measures how much the government must borrow.

Q 18 | eco | Introductory Macroeconomics
The primary deficit is useful because it shows:
(1) The deficit on foreign trade
(2) Only the revenue deficit
(3) The borrowing done to pay salaries
(4) The borrowing requirement excluding interest payments, which reflects the present fiscal imbalance
= 4
~ Interest payments arise from past borrowing. Removing them from the fiscal deficit shows how much the government's current policies are adding to borrowing.

Q 19 | eco | Introductory Macroeconomics
The Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management (FRBM) Act was passed in:
(1) 1991
(2) 2003
(3) 2017
(4) 1999
= 2
~ The FRBM Act of 2003 set a legal framework for reducing deficits, to ensure inter-generational equity in fiscal management and long-term macroeconomic stability.

Q 20 | eco | Introductory Macroeconomics
What does a revenue deficit imply, according to the chapter?
(1) The government is investing heavily in assets
(2) The government has a surplus
(3) The government is dissaving and using the savings of other sectors to finance part of its consumption expenditure
(4) Taxes are too high
= 3
~ A revenue deficit means current receipts do not cover current spending, so the government borrows to pay for consumption, which does not create assets to repay the debt.
