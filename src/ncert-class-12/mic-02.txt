Q 1 | eco | Introductory Microeconomics
The law of diminishing marginal utility states that:
(1) Total utility always falls as consumption rises
(2) The marginal utility from successive units of a commodity declines as more of it is consumed
(3) Marginal utility rises with each unit consumed
(4) Utility cannot be measured
= 2
~ Each additional unit of a good adds less satisfaction than the one before. A thirsty person enjoys the first glass of water most.

Q 2 | eco | Introductory Microeconomics
The total utility from consuming 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 units of a good is 12, 20, 25, 27 and 27. What is the marginal utility of the third unit?
(1) 25
(2) 8
(3) 2
(4) 5
= 4
~ Marginal utility of the third unit = TU(3) − TU(2) = 25 − 20 = 5. The marginal utilities are 12, 8, 5, 2 and 0; when MU becomes zero, total utility is at its maximum.

Q 3 | eco | Introductory Microeconomics
A demand curve shaped like a rectangular hyperbola has a price elasticity that is:
(1) Zero at every point
(2) Infinite at every point
(3) Equal to one (unitary) at every point
(4) Greater than one at high prices and less than one at low prices
= 3
~ On a rectangular hyperbola, price × quantity is constant, so total expenditure does not change with price. That happens only when elasticity is unitary throughout.

Q 4 | eco | Introductory Microeconomics
Using the cardinal utility approach, a consumer buying a single good is in equilibrium when:
(1) The marginal utility of the good, in money terms, equals its price
(2) Total utility is zero
(3) Marginal utility is at its highest
(4) The price of the good is zero
= 1
~ If MU were greater than the price, buying more would add to satisfaction; if less, buying less would. The consumer stops where MU equals price.

Q 5 | eco | Introductory Microeconomics
With two goods X and Y, the condition for consumer equilibrium in the marginal utility approach is:
(1) MUx ÷ Px = MUy ÷ Py
(2) MUx = MUy
(3) Px = Py
(4) MUx × Px = MUy × Py
= 1
~ The consumer allocates income so that the last rupee spent on each good gives the same marginal utility. Otherwise, shifting spending would raise total utility.

Q 6 | eco | Introductory Microeconomics
For a consumer with income M buying two goods with prices p1 and p2, the budget line is:
(1) p1 + p2 = M
(2) p1x1 − p2x2 = M
(3) p1x1 + p2x2 = M
(4) x1 + x2 = M
= 3
~ The budget line shows all bundles that cost exactly the consumer's income. Bundles on or below it form the budget set.

Q 7 | eco | Introductory Microeconomics
A consumer has an income of ₹100. The price of good 1 is ₹5 and the price of good 2 is ₹10. What is the maximum quantity of good 1 she can buy?
(1) 10
(2) 20
(3) 5
(4) 50
= 2
~ If she spends everything on good 1, she can buy 100 ÷ 5 = 20 units. This is where the budget line meets the good 1 axis.

Q 8 | eco | Introductory Microeconomics
The slope of the budget line is:
(1) −M ÷ p1
(2) p1 × p2
(3) −p2 ÷ p1
(4) −p1 ÷ p2
= 4
~ The slope, −p1/p2, shows the rate at which the market allows the consumer to substitute good 2 for good 1: to buy one more unit of good 1 she must give up p1/p2 units of good 2.

Q 9 | eco | Introductory Microeconomics
If the consumer's income rises while prices stay the same, the budget line:
(1) Rotates around the good 2 axis
(2) Becomes steeper
(3) Does not change
(4) Shifts outward parallel to the original line
= 4
~ Higher income raises both intercepts in the same proportion while the slope (−p1/p2) is unchanged, so the line shifts out in parallel.

Q 10 | eco | Introductory Microeconomics
If the price of good 1 falls while income and the price of good 2 are unchanged, the budget line:
(1) Shifts inward parallel
(2) Rotates outward: its intercept on the good 1 axis increases while the intercept on the good 2 axis stays the same
(3) Does not change
(4) Rotates inward on the good 2 axis
= 2
~ The consumer can now buy more of good 1 with the same income, but the maximum amount of good 2 is unchanged, so the line pivots outward around its good 2 intercept and becomes flatter.

Q 11 | eco | Introductory Microeconomics
A consumer's preferences are 'monotonic' if she:
(1) Prefers bundles with less of both goods
(2) Is indifferent between all bundles
(3) Prefers a bundle that has more of at least one good and no less of the other
(4) Always buys the same quantity
= 3
~ Monotonic preferences mean 'more is better'. This is why indifference curves slope downward and higher curves give more satisfaction.

Q 12 | eco | Introductory Microeconomics
An indifference curve shows:
(1) All bundles of two goods that give the consumer the same level of satisfaction
(2) All bundles the consumer can afford
(3) The prices of two goods
(4) The demand for one good
= 1
~ The consumer is indifferent between all bundles on the same curve. A set of such curves is an indifference map, and higher curves represent higher satisfaction.

Q 13 | eco | Introductory Microeconomics
Indifference curves are usually convex to the origin because of:
(1) Increasing income
(2) Rising prices
(3) A diminishing marginal rate of substitution
(4) Constant marginal utility
= 3
~ As the consumer has more of good 1, she is willing to give up less and less of good 2 for an extra unit of good 1. This diminishing MRS gives the curve its convex shape.

Q 14 | eco | Introductory Microeconomics
Why can two indifference curves not intersect?
(1) Because intersection would contradict monotonic preferences: the point of intersection would give the same satisfaction as bundles on both curves, which have different satisfaction levels
(2) Because prices are fixed
(3) Because the budget line prevents it
(4) Because utility cannot be measured
= 1
~ If two curves crossed, the common point would be equally good as a bundle on the higher curve and as one on the lower curve, though one of those has more of a good. This is impossible with monotonic preferences.

Q 15 | eco | Introductory Microeconomics
In the indifference curve approach, the consumer's optimum is where:
(1) The budget line cuts the indifference curve twice
(2) The indifference curve is vertical
(3) Total utility is zero
(4) The budget line is tangent to an indifference curve, so that MRS = p1 ÷ p2
= 4
~ At the point of tangency, the rate at which the consumer is willing to substitute one good for another equals the rate at which the market allows it.

Q 16 | eco | Introductory Microeconomics
An 'inferior good' is one whose demand:
(1) Rises when income rises
(2) Falls when income rises
(3) Does not change with income
(4) Rises when its price rises
= 2
~ For normal goods, demand rises with income. For inferior goods, such as coarse cereals for some consumers, demand falls as income rises because people switch to better goods.

Q 17 | eco | Introductory Microeconomics
Tea and coffee are substitutes. If the price of coffee rises, what happens to the demand for tea?
(1) It falls
(2) It remains unchanged
(3) It becomes zero
(4) It increases
= 4
~ Coffee becomes relatively costlier, so some consumers switch to tea. For complements, such as tea and sugar, a rise in the price of one reduces the demand for the other.

Q 18 | eco | Introductory Microeconomics
For a normal good, an increase in the consumer's income leads to:
(1) A rightward shift of the demand curve
(2) A movement along the demand curve
(3) A fall in demand
(4) No change in demand
= 1
~ A change in the good's own price causes a movement along the demand curve. Changes in income, prices of related goods or tastes shift the whole curve.

Q 19 | eco | Introductory Microeconomics
The price of a good rises from ₹10 to ₹12, and the quantity demanded falls from 100 to 90 units. What is the price elasticity of demand (in absolute value)?
(1) 2
(2) 0.5
(3) 1
(4) 0.2
= 2
~ Percentage change in quantity = (−10 ÷ 100) × 100 = −10 per cent. Percentage change in price = (2 ÷ 10) × 100 = 20 per cent. Elasticity = 10 ÷ 20 = 0.5, so demand is inelastic.

Q 20 | eco | Introductory Microeconomics
If the demand for a good is price elastic (elasticity greater than one), a fall in its price will:
(1) Reduce total expenditure on the good
(2) Leave total expenditure unchanged
(3) Increase total expenditure on the good
(4) Make quantity demanded fall
= 3
~ With elastic demand, quantity rises proportionally more than price falls, so price × quantity goes up. With inelastic demand, expenditure would fall; with unitary elasticity, it would stay the same.
