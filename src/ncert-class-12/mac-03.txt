# NCERT Class 12 Economics, Introductory Macroeconomics, Chapter 3: Money and Banking
# Questions written by Learn with Anil from the chapter (current NCERT edition, Reprint 2026-27). 16 Nov 2026.

Q 1 | eco | Introductory Macroeconomics
What is the main difficulty of a barter system?
(1) The need for a double coincidence of wants
(2) The high rate of interest
(3) The shortage of currency notes
(4) The presence of banks
= 1
~ In barter, a person who wants to exchange goods must find someone who has what he wants and also wants what he has. Money removes this need by acting as a medium of exchange.

Q 2 | eco | Introductory Macroeconomics
When the prices of all goods are expressed in rupees so that they can be compared easily, money serves as a:
(1) Store of value
(2) Medium of exchange
(3) Unit of account
(4) Legal tender
= 3
~ As a unit of account, money gives a common measure in which the value of every good and service can be stated and compared.

Q 3 | eco | Introductory Macroeconomics
Money lets people keep their wealth now and spend it later. This function of money is:
(1) Medium of exchange
(2) Unit of account
(3) Double coincidence of wants
(4) Store of value
= 4
~ Money does not perish and its storage costs are low, so it can be used to carry purchasing power into the future.

Q 4 | eco | Introductory Macroeconomics
'Supply of money' refers to:
(1) The money printed by the RBI in a year
(2) The total stock of money held by the public at a point of time
(3) The money held by banks only
(4) The total income of the economy
= 2
~ Money supply is a stock, measured at a point of time, of the money held by the public.

Q 5 | eco | Introductory Macroeconomics
In measuring the money supply held by the 'public', whose holdings are excluded?
(1) Households
(2) The government and the banking system
(3) Firms
(4) Local bodies and non-banking companies
= 2
~ The public includes all economic units except the producers of money, namely the government and the banking system.

Q 6 | eco | Introductory Macroeconomics
M3, one of the RBI's measures of money supply, is equal to:
(1) Currency held by the public only
(2) M1 + savings deposits with post office savings banks
(3) M2 + total deposits with the Post Office savings organisation
(4) M1 + net time deposits of commercial banks
= 4
~ M3 = M1 + net time deposits of commercial banks. M2 adds post office savings bank deposits to M1, and M4 adds total post office deposits to M3.

Q 7 | eco | Introductory Macroeconomics
Which measure of money supply is the most commonly used and is also called aggregate monetary resources?
(1) M3
(2) M1
(3) M2
(4) M4
= 1
~ M3 is the most commonly used measure of money supply and is known as aggregate monetary resources.

Q 8 | eco | Introductory Macroeconomics
Which statement about the RBI's measures of money supply is correct?
(1) All four are called broad money
(2) M1 and M3 are narrow money; M2 and M4 are broad money
(3) M1 and M2 are narrow money; M3 and M4 are broad money
(4) Only M4 is counted as money
= 3
~ M1 and M2 are known as narrow money and M3 and M4 as broad money. Liquidity falls as we move from M1 to M4.

Q 9 | eco | Introductory Macroeconomics
'Legal tender' refers to:
(1) Cheques drawn on savings accounts
(2) Credit cards
(3) Currency notes and coins that cannot be refused by anyone in settlement of a transaction
(4) Government bonds
= 3
~ Currency notes and coins are legal tender by law. Cheques are not legal tender; a seller can refuse to accept them.

Q 10 | eco | Introductory Macroeconomics
'Fiat money' is money that:
(1) Has no intrinsic value and is accepted because of the authority of the government
(2) Is made of gold
(3) Is issued by commercial banks only
(4) Can be refused in transactions
= 1
~ Modern currency has little value as paper or metal. People accept it because the government declares it money, so it is fiat money.

Q 11 | eco | Introductory Macroeconomics
If the required reserve ratio for banks is 20 per cent, the money multiplier is:
(1) 20
(2) 0.2
(3) 2
(4) 5
= 4
~ Money multiplier = 1 ÷ reserve ratio = 1 ÷ 0.2 = 5. Banks keep 20 per cent as reserves and lend the rest, which comes back as fresh deposits.

Q 12 | eco | Introductory Macroeconomics
A new deposit of ₹1,000 enters the banking system and the reserve ratio is 10 per cent. With banks lending out everything above required reserves, total deposits finally created are:
(1) ₹1,100
(2) ₹10,000
(3) ₹9,000
(4) ₹100
= 2
~ Total deposits = initial deposit × (1 ÷ reserve ratio) = 1,000 × (1 ÷ 0.1) = ₹10,000.

Q 13 | eco | Introductory Macroeconomics
High powered money (the monetary base) consists of:
(1) Only time deposits of banks
(2) Only government bonds
(3) Only demand deposits
(4) Currency held by the public plus the reserves of commercial banks
= 4
~ High powered money is the total liability of the monetary authority: currency held by the public and banks' reserves, as vault cash and deposits with the RBI.

Q 14 | eco | Introductory Macroeconomics
The Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR) is:
(1) The rate at which the RBI lends to banks
(2) The share of deposits banks must keep in government securities
(3) The fraction of deposits that commercial banks must keep as reserves with the RBI
(4) The interest rate paid on savings accounts
= 3
~ CRR is the share of deposits banks must keep with the RBI. Raising it leaves banks less to lend, which reduces money supply.

Q 15 | eco | Introductory Macroeconomics
The Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR) requires banks to:
(1) Keep a fraction of their deposits in specified liquid assets, such as government securities
(2) Keep all deposits as cash
(3) Lend only to the government
(4) Pay a fixed interest rate on loans
= 1
~ Under SLR, banks must hold a minimum share of their deposits in liquid assets like government securities, apart from the CRR.

Q 16 | eco | Introductory Macroeconomics
The repo rate is the rate at which:
(1) Commercial banks lend to the public
(2) The RBI lends to commercial banks for short periods against government securities under a repurchase agreement
(3) The RBI borrows from commercial banks
(4) The government borrows from abroad
= 2
~ Under a repo, banks sell securities to the RBI with an agreement to buy them back, getting short-term funds. Raising the repo rate makes such borrowing costlier.

Q 17 | eco | Introductory Macroeconomics
The reverse repo rate is the rate at which:
(1) Banks lend to households
(2) The RBI lends to the government
(3) The RBI borrows from commercial banks
(4) Banks pay interest on fixed deposits
= 3
~ In a reverse repo, the RBI borrows funds from banks against securities. It is the reverse of the repo transaction.

Q 18 | eco | Introductory Macroeconomics
If the RBI buys government securities from the public in open market operations, the money supply:
(1) Falls
(2) Stays the same
(3) Becomes zero
(4) Increases
= 4
~ When the RBI buys securities, it pays with new money, which increases high powered money and, through the multiplier, money supply. Selling securities does the opposite.

Q 19 | eco | Introductory Macroeconomics
To reduce the money supply in order to control inflation, the RBI can:
(1) Raise the CRR or the repo rate, or sell government securities
(2) Lower the CRR and buy government securities
(3) Lower the repo rate
(4) Print more currency
= 1
~ Higher reserve requirements, costlier borrowing from the RBI and sale of securities all reduce the funds banks can lend, lowering money supply.

Q 20 | eco | Introductory Macroeconomics
The RBI is called the 'lender of last resort' because:
(1) It lends only to the government
(2) It stands ready to lend to banks facing a crisis, so that they do not fail
(3) It lends to the public when banks refuse
(4) It is the last bank to open each day
= 2
~ If depositors rush to withdraw money from a bank, the RBI can provide funds to it. This guarantee prevents bank failures and keeps confidence in the banking system.
