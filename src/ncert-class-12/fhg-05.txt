# NCERT Class 12 Geography, Fundamentals of Human Geography, Chapter 5: Secondary Activities
# Questions written by Learn with Anil from the chapter (current NCERT edition, Reprint 2026-27). November 2026.

Q 1 | geo | Fundamentals of Human Geography
Secondary activities add value to natural resources by:
(1) Collecting them from nature
(2) Transforming raw materials into valuable products
(3) Providing services to people
(4) Researching new ideas
= 2
~ Turning cotton into yarn or iron ore into steel adds value. Manufacturing, processing and construction are secondary activities.

Q 2 | geo | Fundamentals of Human Geography
Under mass production, as described in the chapter:
(1) Factories make only a few made-to-order pieces
(2) Large quantities of standardised parts are produced, with each worker doing only one task repeatedly
(3) Each worker makes a complete product by hand
(4) No machines are used
= 2
~ Specialisation of skills is a characteristic of modern large-scale manufacturing; the craft method makes only a few pieces to order.

Q 3 | geo | Fundamentals of Human Geography
Automation is described in the chapter as:
(1) An advanced stage of mechanisation, where processes run without human thinking during manufacturing
(2) The use of hand tools in cottage industries
(3) Government ownership of industries
(4) Locating industries near raw materials
= 1
~ Automatic factories with feedback and closed-loop computer control systems, where machines can "think", have come up all over the world.

Q 4 | geo | Fundamentals of Human Geography
According to the chapter, the major concentrations of modern manufacturing cover what share of the world's land area?
(1) Less than 10 per cent
(2) About 25 per cent
(3) About 50 per cent
(4) More than 75 per cent
= 1
~ Uneven geographic distribution is a feature of modern manufacturing; the nations that have it have become centres of economic and political power.

Q 5 | geo | Fundamentals of Human Geography
Industries based on cheap, bulky and weight-losing raw materials, such as steel, sugar and cement, are usually located:
(1) Close to the sources of raw material
(2) Only near ports
(3) Only in big cities
(4) Anywhere, regardless of raw material
= 1
~ Locating near the raw material reduces transport costs. Perishable materials, as in agro-processing and dairy, also need processing close to the source.

Q 6 | geo | Fundamentals of Human Geography
The aluminium industry is given in the chapter as an example of an industry that tends to locate close to:
(1) The market
(2) Sources of energy (power)
(3) Forests
(4) Ports only
= 2
~ Industries that use a lot of power locate close to energy sources such as coal, hydroelectricity or petroleum.

Q 7 | geo | Fundamentals of Human Geography
Many industries benefit from being near a leader industry and other industries. These savings derived from linkages between industries are called:
(1) Footloose economies
(2) Economies of barter
(3) Regional policies
(4) Agglomeration economies
= 4
~ Agglomeration economies arise when industries cluster together and share services, suppliers and markets.

Q 8 | geo | Fundamentals of Human Geography
Consider the following statements about footloose industries:
1. They are not dependent on any specific raw material and use component parts that can be obtained anywhere.
2. They are usually heavily polluting.
3. Accessibility by road network is an important factor in their location.
Which of the statements given above are correct?
(1) 1 and 2 only
(2) 2 and 3 only
(3) 1 and 3 only
(4) 1, 2 and 3
= 3
~ Footloose industries produce in small quantities, employ a small labour force and are generally non-polluting.

Q 9 | geo | Fundamentals of Human Geography
Which of the following is the smallest manufacturing unit, where artisans use local raw materials and simple tools to make goods at home?
(1) Small-scale manufacturing
(2) High-tech industry
(3) Large-scale manufacturing
(4) Household or cottage industry
= 4
~ The chapter's examples include pottery in Nagaland and bamboo basket weaving in Arunachal Pradesh.

Q 10 | geo | Fundamentals of Human Geography
How does small-scale manufacturing differ from household industry?
(1) It needs enormous capital and advanced technology
(2) It always uses imported raw materials
(3) It uses a workshop outside the home, simple power-driven machines and semi-skilled labour
(4) It is always owned by the government
= 3
~ Countries like India, China, Indonesia and Brazil have developed labour-intensive small-scale manufacturing to provide employment.

Q 11 | geo | Fundamentals of Human Geography
Large-scale manufacturing developed during the last 200 years first in:
(1) Australia and New Zealand
(2) India and China
(3) Africa and South America
(4) The UK, north-eastern USA and Europe
= 4
~ Large-scale manufacturing needs a large market, varied raw materials, enormous energy, specialised workers, advanced technology and large capital. It has now spread almost all over the world.

Q 12 | geo | Fundamentals of Human Geography
Match the industries with the category of raw material on which they are based:
A. Cement and pottery — 1. Agro-based
B. Paper and lac — 2. Mineral-based (non-metallic)
C. Leather and wool — 3. Forest-based
D. Canning and confectionery — 4. Animal-based
Select the correct answer:
(1) A-2, B-4, C-3, D-1
(2) A-3, B-2, C-1, D-4
(3) A-2, B-3, C-4, D-1
(4) A-1, B-3, C-4, D-2
= 3
~ Industries are classified by inputs as agro-based, mineral-based, chemical-based, forest-based and animal-based.

Q 13 | geo | Fundamentals of Human Geography
Which of the following is an example of a chemical-based industry?
(1) Iron and steel
(2) Pottery
(3) Furniture
(4) Petrochemicals
= 4
~ Chemical-based industries use natural chemical minerals such as mineral oil (petroleum), salts, sulphur and potash; synthetic fibres and plastics are also made by them.

Q 14 | geo | Fundamentals of Human Geography
Iron and steel is classified as which type of mineral-based industry?
(1) Ferrous metallic
(2) Non-ferrous metallic
(3) Non-metallic
(4) Chemical
= 1
~ Industries using ferrous minerals (containing iron) include iron and steel. Aluminium, copper and jewellery are non-ferrous; cement and pottery are non-metallic.

(1) 1 only
(2) 2 only

Q 16 | geo | Fundamentals of Human Geography
Industries managed jointly by private and public sectors, or by joint stock companies, are classified as:
(1) Public sector industries
(2) Private sector industries
(3) Joint sector industries
(4) Cottage industries
= 3
~ By ownership, industries are public sector, private sector or joint sector. Mixed economies have both public and private enterprises.

Q 17 | geo | Fundamentals of Human Geography
Which of the following is a characteristic of high technology industries?
(1) Blue-collar workers greatly outnumber professional workers
(2) Intensive research and development, with professional (white-collar) workers making up a large share of the workforce
(3) Massive assembly structures and storage areas
(4) Use of only manual labour
= 2
~ High-tech landscapes have neatly spaced, low, modern office-plant-lab buildings rather than massive factories. Robotics and computer-aided design are examples.

Q 18 | geo | Fundamentals of Human Geography
Regionally concentrated, self-sustained and highly specialised high-tech industrial areas are called:
(1) Technopolies
(2) Conurbations
(3) Special economic zones
(4) Agro-factories
= 1
~ The chapter gives Silicon Valley near San Francisco and Silicon Forest near Seattle as examples.

Q 19 | geo | Fundamentals of Human Geography
The term "agri-business" in the chapter refers to:
(1) Shifting cultivation in forests
(2) Government purchase of food grains
(3) Kitchen gardens of households
(4) Commercial farming on an industrial scale, often financed by businesses whose main interests lie outside agriculture
= 4
~ Agri-business farms, such as large tea plantations owned by corporations, are mechanised, large and highly productive; they are sometimes called agro-factories.

(1) Paper industry
