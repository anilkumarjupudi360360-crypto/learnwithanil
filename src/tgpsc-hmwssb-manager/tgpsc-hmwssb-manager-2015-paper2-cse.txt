Q 1 | dms | Sets, Relations & Functions
Which of the following are partitions of {1,2,......8}?
(1) {{1,3,5},{1,2,6},{4,7,8}}
(2) {{1,3,5},{2,6,7},{4,8}}
(3) {{1,3,5},{2,6},{2,6},{4,7,8}}
(4) {{1,5},{2,6},{4,8}}
= 2
~ A partition of a set is a collection of non-empty, pairwise disjoint blocks whose union is the whole set. In (2) the blocks {1,3,5}, {2,6,7}, {4,8} share no element and together contain 1 to 8, so it is a partition. (1) has 1 in two blocks; (3) lists the block {2,6} twice, so its listed blocks are not pairwise disjoint (read strictly as a set, the repeated block would collapse into one, but the examiner treats the repetition as overlapping blocks); (4) leaves out 3 and 7.

Q 2 | dms | Combinatorics & Probability
Let S = {1,2,..., n}. Let S₁ be the set of all subsets of S that contain 1. Let T₁ denote the set of all subsets of S that don't contain 1. Which of the following statement is correct?
(1) |T₁| = |S₁| = 2⁽ⁿ⁻¹⁾
(2) |T₁| ≠ |S₁|
(3) |T₁| + |S₁| = 2⁽ⁿ⁻¹⁾
(4) |T₁| + |S₁| = 2ⁿ
= 1,4
~ A subset of S that contains 1 is fixed by choosing any subset of the other n − 1 elements, so |S₁| = 2ⁿ⁻¹; likewise a subset without 1 is any subset of {2,...,n}, so |T₁| = 2ⁿ⁻¹. Hence statement (1) is true, and since every subset of S falls in exactly one of the two classes, |T₁| + |S₁| = 2ⁿ⁻¹ + 2ⁿ⁻¹ = 2ⁿ, so statement (4) is also true. Because both are correct, TSPSC accepted both (1) and (4); (2) and (3) are false.

Q 3 | dms | Sets, Relations & Functions
Let A = {0,1} × {0,1} and B = {a, b, c}. Suppose A is listed in lexicographic order based on 0 < 1 and B is in alphabetic order. If A × B × A is listed in lexicographic order, then the next element after ((1,0), c, (1,1)) is ________.
(1) ((1,0), a, (0,0))
(2) ((1,1), c, (0,0))
(3) ((1,1) a, (0,0))
(4) ((1,1), a, (1,1))
= 3
~ A in lexicographic order is (0,0), (0,1), (1,0), (1,1). In ((1,0), c, (1,1)) the last component (1,1) is the largest element of A and c is the largest element of B, so both "roll over" to their smallest values (0,0) and a, and the first component moves up from (1,0) to (1,1). The next element is ((1,1), a, (0,0)).

Q 4 | dms | Sets, Relations & Functions
Let P(A) denote the power set of A. If P(A) ⊆ B then
(1) 2^|A| ≤ |B|
(2) 2^|A| ≥ |B|
(3) 2^|A| ≥ 2^|B|
(4) 2|A| < |B|
= 1
~ The power set of A has 2^|A| elements. If P(A) is a subset of B, then B has at least as many elements as P(A), so |P(A)| = 2^|A| ≤ |B|.

Q 5 | dms | Sets, Relations & Functions
The inverse of the function f(x) = x² − 5 is
(1) f⁻¹(y) = √(y + 5)
(2) f⁻¹(y) = √(y − 5)
(3) f⁻¹(y) = √y − 5
(4) f⁻¹(y) = √y + 5
= 1
~ Put y = x² − 5 and solve for x: x² = y + 5, so x = √(y + 5) (taking the non-negative root, i.e. treating f on x ≥ 0, where it is one-one). Check: f(√(y + 5)) = (y + 5) − 5 = y.

Q 6 | dms | Lattices & Algebraic Structures
Let D₃₀ = {1,2,3,5,6,10,15,30} and let the relation / (divides) be a partial order relation on D₃₀. Which of the following statements is correct?
(1) All the lower bounds of 10 and 15 are 1,2,3,5,6
(2) All the lower bounds of 10 and 15 are 1,2,3,5
(3) All the lower bounds of 10 and 15 are 1,5
(4) All the lower bounds of 10 and 15 are 2,3,5
= 3
~ Under divisibility, a lower bound of 10 and 15 is an element of D₃₀ that divides both 10 and 15, i.e. a common divisor. The divisors of 10 are 1, 2, 5, 10 and of 15 are 1, 3, 5, 15, so the common ones are 1 and 5 (the greatest lower bound is 5 = gcd(10, 15)). 2, 3 and 6 each fail to divide one of the two numbers.

Q 7 | dms | Lattices & Algebraic Structures
Let P(S) be the power set of S = {1,2,3}, and then define a lattice L under partial ordering of set inclusion induced on P(S). Which of the following sets are the sub-lattices of L.
A = {∅, {1,2}, {2,3}, {1,2,3}}
B = {∅, {1},{1,2}, {1,2,3}}
C = {∅, {3}, {1,3}, {1,2,3}}
D = {{1}, {3}, {1,3}, {1,2,3}}
E = {∅,{3}, {1,2}, {1,2,3}}
(1) A and D are sub-lattices of L
(2) B and C are sub-lattices of L
(3) A is a sub-lattice of L
(4) D is a sub-lattice of L
= 2
~ In L the join is ∪ and the meet is ∩, and a sub-lattice must be closed under both. B and C are chains (∅ ⊆ {1} ⊆ {1,2} ⊆ {1,2,3} and ∅ ⊆ {3} ⊆ {1,3} ⊆ {1,2,3}), and a chain is always closed under ∪ and ∩, so both are sub-lattices. A is not, because {1,2} ∩ {2,3} = {2} ∉ A, and D is not, because {1} ∩ {3} = ∅ ∉ D. (E is also closed under ∪ and ∩, but no option mentions it.)

Q 8 | dld | Boolean Algebra & Minimisation
In a 5-variable Boolean function, what are the "minterms" that differ from m₂₀ by one literal?
(1) m₀, m₅, m₁₀, m₁₅, m₂₅
(2) m₄, m₈, m₁₆, m₂₄, m₂₈
(3) m₄, m₁₆, m₂₁, m₂₂, m₂₈
(4) m₄, m₂₁, m₂₂, m₂₄, m₂₈
= 3
~ m₂₀ = 10100 in binary. A minterm differing in exactly one literal differs in exactly one bit: flipping each bit in turn gives 00100 = 4, 11100 = 28, 10000 = 16, 10110 = 22 and 10101 = 21. So the adjacent minterms are m₄, m₁₆, m₂₁, m₂₂, m₂₈ (m₂₄ = 11000 differs in two bits).

Q 9 | dld | Boolean Algebra & Minimisation
Given the Boolean function J(A, B, C) = Σ(0,1,7) + d(2,5,6), which of the following sum of products expressions (not necessarily minimal) represent J(A, B, C)?
(i) A'B' + AB
(ii) A'B' + BC
(iii) A'C' + B'C + AB
(iv) A'B' + AC
(v) A'B' + ABC
(1) (i), (ii) and (iii)
(2) (iii), (iv) and (v)
(3) (i), (ii), (iv) and (v)
(4) (i), (iii), (iv) and (v)
= 4
~ A valid expression must cover minterms 0, 1, 7 and may use only the don't-cares 2, 5, 6, never 3 or 4 (the 0s). (i) A'B' = {0,1}, AB = {6,7}: valid. (ii) BC = {3,7} covers the 0-minterm 3: invalid. (iii) A'C' = {0,2}, B'C = {1,5}, AB = {6,7}: valid. (iv) AC = {5,7}: valid. (v) ABC = {7}: valid. So (i), (iii), (iv) and (v).

Q 10 | dms | Lattices & Algebraic Structures
A group has 11 elements. The number of proper sub-groups it can have is
(1) 0
(2) 4
(3) 5
(4) 8
= 1
~ By Lagrange's theorem the order of a subgroup divides the order of the group. Since 11 is prime, the only subgroups are {e} (order 1) and the whole group (order 11), which are the trivial (improper) subgroups, so the group has no proper (non-trivial) subgroup. (Under the convention that {e} counts as proper there would be exactly one, which is not offered.)

Q 11 | ops | Memory Management & Virtual Memory
Which of the following techniques can be used to counter the problem of external fragmentation that occurs in dynamic partitioning memory management?
(1) Compaction
(2) Segmentation
(3) Swapping
(4) Splitting
= 1
~ In dynamic (variable) partitioning, free memory gets broken into many small holes between allocated partitions (external fragmentation). Compaction shuffles the allocated partitions together so that all the free holes merge into one large contiguous block, which counters this problem. Segmentation itself suffers from external fragmentation, and swapping or splitting do not merge the holes.

Q 12 | ops | Memory Management & Virtual Memory
The addresses generated by a particular program in a pure demand paging system with 100 records per page and one free main memory frame are as follows
0100, 0200, 0420, 0489, 0510, 0530, 0599, 0120, 0220, 0230, 0260, 0320, 0370
The number of page faults generated in order to execute the program is
(1) 13
(2) 8
(3) 7
(4) 10
= 3
~ With 100 records per page, page number = address ÷ 100, so the reference string is 1, 2, 4, 4, 5, 5, 5, 1, 2, 2, 2, 3, 3. With only one frame, a fault occurs every time the page changes: 1 (F), 2 (F), 4 (F), 4, 5 (F), 5, 5, 1 (F), 2 (F), 2, 2, 3 (F), 3. That is 7 page faults.

Q 13 | ops | Processes, Threads & CPU Scheduling
In the process state transition diagram, transition from running to ready state indicates that
(1) the running process is preempted by a higher priority process
(2) the running process initiated an input/output operation
(3) the running process is blocked for a semaphore operation
(4) that the OS is not ready to service the running process
= 1
~ A process goes from running back to ready when it is preempted, i.e. it is still able to run but loses the CPU, for example because a higher-priority process has become ready or its time slice has expired. Starting an I/O operation or blocking on a semaphore moves the process from running to the waiting (blocked) state, not to ready.

Q 14 | ops | Memory Management & Virtual Memory
Which of the following activities is not done by memory management?
(1) Keep an account of which parts of memory are currently being used by each of the processes
(2) Decide which processes are to be brought into memory when memory space becomes available
(3) Allocate and de-allocate memory space as needed
(4) Storage allocation
= 4
~ The memory-management activities of an OS are keeping track of which parts of main memory are in use and by whom, deciding which processes (or parts of them) to move into and out of memory, and allocating and de-allocating memory space as needed. Storage allocation (allocating space on disk) is part of secondary-storage (disk) management, together with free-space management and disk scheduling.

Q 15 | ops | Processes, Threads & CPU Scheduling
Which of the following statements is incorrect with regard to operating system design?
(1) Layered approach is easy to design and debug
(2) Layered approach is less efficient
(3) Changes to the kernel tend to be fewer with microkernel approach
(4) Microkernels are less secure and reliable
= 4
~ A microkernel keeps only minimal services in the kernel and runs most services as user-level processes, so a failing service does not crash the kernel; microkernels are therefore more secure and reliable, which makes (4) incorrect. The layered approach is simple to construct and debug but less efficient because a call passes through several layers, and in a microkernel new services are added in user space, so the kernel changes less often.

Q 16 | ops | Processes, Threads & CPU Scheduling
When a process creates a new process using the fork() operation, then which of the following structures are shared between the parent process and the child process?
(1) Stack
(2) Heap
(3) Shared Memory Segments
(4) Stack, Heap and Shared Memory Segments
= 3
~ fork() gives the child its own copy of the parent's address space (stack, heap and data, usually implemented with copy-on-write), so a later change by one process is not seen by the other. Shared memory segments attached by the parent stay attached in the child and refer to the same physical memory, so only they are shared.

Q 17 | ops | Processes, Threads & CPU Scheduling
Which of the following statements is incorrect with respect to threads?
(1) User-level threads are unknown by the kernel
(2) Kernel threads are more expensive
(3) User-level threads are more expensive
(4) Thread creation uses fewer resources than process creation
= 3
~ User-level threads are managed by a thread library in user space, so the kernel does not know about them and creating or switching them needs no system call; they are cheaper than kernel threads, whose every operation needs a trap into the kernel. So "user-level threads are more expensive" is the incorrect statement; the others are true.

Q 18 | ops | Synchronisation & Deadlocks
Semaphore can be used for ________.
(1) detecting deadlock
(2) process synchronization
(3) process scheduling
(4) defining priority of the processes
= 2
~ A semaphore is an integer variable accessed only through the atomic operations wait (P) and signal (V); it is a tool for process synchronization, for example to enforce mutual exclusion on a critical section or to order events between processes. It does not detect deadlocks, schedule processes or set priorities.

Q 19 | ops | Processes, Threads & CPU Scheduling
Which scheduler controls the degree of multiprogramming?
(1) Short-term scheduler
(2) Long-term scheduler
(3) Middle-term scheduler
(4) Disk Scheduler
= 2
~ The long-term (job) scheduler decides which jobs are admitted from the job pool into main memory, so it controls the degree of multiprogramming, i.e. the number of processes in memory. The short-term scheduler only picks which ready process gets the CPU next.

Q 20 | ops | Synchronisation & Deadlocks
Let S and Q be two semaphores initialized to 1, where P₀ and P₁ are the processes defined as follows:
```
Process P₁      Process P₂
wait (S);       wait(Q);
wait(Q);        wait(S);
    ---;            ----;
signal(S);      signal(Q);
signal(Q);      signal(S);
```
Which one of the following statements is correct with respect to the above construct?
(1) Causes starvation
(2) Causes livelock
(3) Causes a deadlock
(4) Does not ensure mutual exclusion
= 3
~ The two processes acquire the semaphores in opposite orders. If the first process executes wait(S) (S becomes 0) and is then preempted, and the second executes wait(Q) (Q becomes 0), the first now blocks on wait(Q) and the second blocks on wait(S). Each holds what the other needs (circular wait), so neither can proceed: a deadlock.

Q 21 | coa | Memory Hierarchy & I/O
The number of address and data lines for a memory of 4K × 16 is:
(1) 12 and 16
(2) 10 and 16
(3) 12 and 12
(4) 16 and 16
= 1
~ 4K × 16 means 4K words of 16 bits each. 4K = 4 × 1024 = 2¹² words, so 12 address lines are needed to select a word, and each word is 16 bits wide, so there are 16 data lines.

Q 22 | coa | CPU, Pipelining & Computer Arithmetic
The instruction of a micro programmed control unit is called as
(1) micro-instruction
(2) micro-operation
(3) machine instruction
(4) micro-program
= 1
~ In a microprogrammed control unit each word stored in the control memory is a micro-instruction; it specifies one or more micro-operations to be performed, and a sequence of micro-instructions makes up a micro-program (one routine per machine instruction).

Q 23 | coa | Memory Hierarchy & I/O
Suppose that a bus has 16 data lines and requires 4 cycles of 250nsecs each to transfer data. The bandwidth of this bus would be 2 Megabytes/sec. If the cycle time of the bus was reduced to 125nsecs and the number of cycles required for transfer stayed the same what would the bandwidth of the bus?
(1) 1 Megabyte/sec
(2) 4 Megabytes/sec
(3) 8 Megabytes/sec
(4) 2 Megabytes/sec
= 4
~ 16 data lines move 2 bytes per transfer. Originally one transfer takes 4 × 250 ns = 1 µs, giving 2 bytes/µs = 2 Megabytes/sec, the value TSPSC's key keeps for the faster bus. However, with a 125 ns cycle and the same 4 cycles a transfer takes 4 × 125 ns = 0.5 µs, so the usual calculation gives 2 bytes ÷ 0.5 µs = 4 Megabytes/sec (option 2): halving the cycle time doubles the bandwidth. (Official final key; some sources differ.)

Q 24 | coa | Memory Hierarchy & I/O
In a memory-mapped I/O system, which of the following will not be there?
(1) LOAD
(2) IN
(3) OUT
(4) ADD
= 1
~ In memory-mapped I/O the device registers are given ordinary memory addresses, so the CPU reaches them with the normal memory-reference instructions (LOAD/STORE, MOV, and even arithmetic such as ADD with a memory operand); the separate I/O instructions IN and OUT are needed only in isolated (I/O-mapped) I/O. TSPSC's key nevertheless gives LOAD; by the standard reasoning it is IN and OUT that will not be there, so the question itself has two equally valid answers among the other options. (Official final key; some sources differ.)

Q 25 | coa | CPU, Pipelining & Computer Arithmetic
In signed-magnitude binary division, if the dividend is (11100)₂ and divisor is (10011)₂ then the result is
(1) (00100)₂
(2) (10100)₂
(3) (11001)₂
(4) (01100)₂
= 2
~ In signed-magnitude form the leftmost bit is the sign: the dividend 1 1100 is −12 and the divisor 1 0011 is −3. Dividing the magnitudes gives 1100 ÷ 0011 = 0100 (12 ÷ 3 = 4), and TSPSC's key (10100)₂ attaches a sign bit of 1 to this magnitude, i.e. −4. By the usual rule, though, the sign of the quotient is the XOR of the two sign bits (1 ⊕ 1 = 0), since (−12) ÷ (−3) = +4, which gives (00100)₂, option (1). (Official final key; some sources differ.)

Q 26 | coa | Memory Hierarchy & I/O
How many RAM chips of size 8K×16 are required to build 1 Megabyte memory?
(1) 128
(2) 64
(3) 32
(4) 16
= 2
~ 1 Megabyte = 2²⁰ bytes = 2²³ bits. One 8K × 16 chip holds 2¹³ × 16 = 2¹⁷ bits (16 Kbytes). Number of chips = 2²³ ÷ 2¹⁷ = 2⁶ = 64.

Q 27 | dld | Number Systems & Codes
How many 1's are present in 5×512+3×64+7×8+4
(1) 9
(2) 8
(3) 7
(4) 6
= 2
~ 512 = 8³, 64 = 8², 8 = 8¹, so the number is 5374 in octal. Each octal digit is 3 binary bits: 5 = 101, 3 = 011, 7 = 111, 4 = 100, giving 101 011 111 100 (decimal 2812). Counting ones: 2 + 2 + 3 + 1 = 8.

Q 28 | coa | CPU, Pipelining & Computer Arithmetic
In a 32-bit floating-point format, the leftmost bit stores the sign of the number. The exponent value is stored in the next eight bits. A bias of 127 is subtracted from the field to get the true exponent value. The base is assumed to be 2. The format stores a normalized floating-point number, with the left-most being implicit. Thus remaining 23 bits are used to store 24-bit significand. What is the highest positive integer that can be represented
(1) 2¹²⁸
(2) (2−2⁻²³)×2¹²⁸
(3) 2¹²⁷
(4) (2−2⁻²³)×2¹²⁷
= 2
~ The largest significand is 1.111...1 (the implicit 1 followed by 23 ones) = 2 − 2⁻²³. In this format (as in Stallings' example) every 8-bit exponent field is usable, so the largest field 255 gives a true exponent of 255 − 127 = 128, and the largest value is (2 − 2⁻²³) × 2¹²⁸. (In IEEE 754, where the all-ones exponent is reserved for infinity/NaN, the maximum would instead be (2 − 2⁻²³) × 2¹²⁷.)

Q 29 | ops | Processes, Threads & CPU Scheduling
Listed below are some of the Operating System abstractions and related hardware components:
i. Thread | p) Interrupt
ii. Virtual Address Space | q) Memory
iii. File System | r) CPU
iv. Signal | s) Disk
Which of the following correctly relates the Operating System abstractions and related hardware components?
(1) i-p ii-q iii-r iv-s
(2) i-r ii-s iii-q iv-p
(3) i-r ii-q iii-s iv-p
(4) i-r ii-p iii-q iv-s
= 3
~ Each OS abstraction virtualises a hardware resource: a thread is an abstraction of the CPU, a virtual address space is an abstraction of physical memory, a file system is an abstraction of the disk, and a signal is the software counterpart of a hardware interrupt. So i-r, ii-q, iii-s, iv-p.

Q 30 | coa | CPU, Pipelining & Computer Arithmetic
A non-pipeline system takes 50ns to process a task. The same task can be processed in six-segment pipeline with a clock cycle of 10ns. What is the speed-up ratio of the pipeline for 100 tasks?
(1) 4.76ns
(2) 40ns
(3) 40.76ns
(4) 1ns
= 1
~ Without pipelining, 100 tasks take 100 × 50 = 5000 ns. A k = 6 segment pipeline needs (k + n − 1) = 6 + 99 = 105 clock cycles for n = 100 tasks, i.e. 105 × 10 = 1050 ns. Speed-up = 5000 ÷ 1050 ≈ 4.76 (a pure ratio; the "ns" in the options is a slip).

Q 31 | coa | Memory Hierarchy & I/O
An interface that provides I/O transfer of data directly to and from the memory unit and peripheral is termed as
(1) DMA
(2) UART
(3) USRT
(4) Serial Interface
= 1
~ In Direct Memory Access the DMA controller takes over the system buses and transfers data directly between the peripheral and memory without passing each word through the CPU. UART and USRT are asynchronous/synchronous serial interfaces that only convert between parallel and serial data.

Q 32 | dbm | Normalisation
Which of the following normal forms eliminates the anomalies of multivalued dependencies?
(1) 3NF
(2) BCNF
(3) 4NF
(4) 5NF
= 3
~ Fourth normal form requires that for every non-trivial multivalued dependency X →→ Y, X is a superkey; this removes the redundancy and update anomalies caused by independent multivalued facts stored in one relation. 3NF and BCNF deal only with functional dependencies, and 5NF deals with join dependencies.

Q 33 | dbm | Transactions, Concurrency & Indexing
Which one of the following state transitions is made when the transaction has been rolled back and the database is restored to the state prior to the start of the transition?
(1) Active→Partially Committed
(2) Active→Committed
(3) Active→Aborted
(4) Partially Committed→Committed
= 3
~ A transaction that cannot complete normally is rolled back, i.e. its changes are undone so the database returns to the state before the transaction started; the transaction is then in the aborted state. Of the transitions offered, only Active→Aborted describes this; the others lead towards a successful commit.

Q 34 | dbm | SQL & Relational Algebra
Consider relations R(A,B) and S(C,D); and the following SQL query.
```
Select distinct A,B,
from R,S
```
Under what conditions the result is guaranteed to be same as R.
(1) R has no duplicate and S is non-empty
(2) R and S have no duplicates
(3) S has no duplicates and R is non-empty
(4) R and S have the same number of tuples
= 1
~ FROM R,S forms the Cartesian product, in which every tuple of R appears once for each tuple of S. If S is empty the product, and hence the result, is empty even when R is not; if S is non-empty every (A,B) of R appears, and DISTINCT removes repeats. So the result equals R exactly when S is non-empty and R has no duplicate tuples (DISTINCT would collapse any duplicates of R).

Q 35 | dbm | Normalisation
Let R = (A, B, C, D) and F = {A → B, A → C, BC → D}, then which of the following is correct?
(1) B → D
(2) A → B
(3) A → D
(4) D → A
= 3
~ From A → B and A → C, the union rule gives A → BC; with BC → D, transitivity gives A → D, so A → D is implied by F (in F⁺). A → B also holds, but it is simply one of the given dependencies; the question looks for the dependency that follows from F. B → D and D → A cannot be derived.

Q 36 | dbm | SQL & Relational Algebra
Which one of the following relational algebra transformations is incorrect?
(1) σ_C1(σ_C1(R)) → σ_C2(σ_C2(R))
(2) σ_C(π_A(R)) → π_A(σ_C(R))
(3) σ_C(R ∪ S) → σ_C(R) ∪ σ_C(S)
(4) σ_C(R) → π_A(R)
= 4
~ σ_C(R) keeps whole rows of R that satisfy C, while π_A(R) keeps every row but only the columns A, so the two are not equivalent and (4) is not a valid transformation. (2) is valid when C involves only attributes of A (selection and projection commute), (3) is valid because selection distributes over union, and (1) is the commutativity of selections, σ_C1(σ_C2(R)) = σ_C2(σ_C1(R)), with the subscripts misprinted.

Q 37 | dbm | SQL & Relational Algebra
If relation R has m tuples and S has n tuples, what is the maximum and minimum number of tuples in R ⋈ S?
(1) m + n and 0
(2) mn and 0
(3) m + n and |m − n|
(4) mn and m + n
= 2
~ In the worst case every tuple of R matches every tuple of S on the common attributes (or there are no common attributes, so the join is a Cartesian product), giving m × n tuples. If no tuple of R agrees with any tuple of S on the common attributes, the join is empty, so the minimum is 0.

Q 38 | dbm | ER & Relational Model
The referential integrity rule requires that
(1) Every null foreign key value must reference an existing primary key value.
(2) It makes it possible for an attribute to have a corresponding value.
(3) Every non-null foreign key value must reference an existing primary key value.
(4) It makes it possible to delete a row in one table whose primary key does not have a matching foreign key value in another table.
= 3
~ Referential integrity says that a foreign key value must either be null or equal an existing primary key value in the referenced relation. So every non-null foreign key must reference an existing primary key; a null foreign key references nothing, so (1) is wrong.

Q 39 | dbm | Normalisation
Which of the following statements is correct?
(1) Every relation in 2NF is also in BCNF
(2) Every relation in BCNF is also in 3NF
(3) No relation can be in both BCNF and 3NF
(4) Every relation is ZNF is also in 3 NF
= 2
~ BCNF is stricter than 3NF: it requires the left side of every non-trivial FD to be a superkey, which already satisfies the 3NF condition, so every BCNF relation is in 3NF (and hence (3) is false). A 2NF relation may still have transitive dependencies, so it need not be in 3NF or BCNF; this rules out (1) and (4) ("ZNF" is a misprint for 2NF).

(3) Linked list

= 2

(1) It is a Contradiction.
(2) It is a Tautology
(3) It is not a well formed statement
(4) It is a Inverse
= 2
~ q → (p → q) ≡ ¬q ∨ (¬p ∨ q) ≡ ¬p ∨ (q ∨ ¬q) ≡ ¬p ∨ T ≡ T. It could be false only if q were true and p → q false, but p → q is false only when q is false; so it is true for every assignment, a tautology.

Q 131 | dms | Combinatorics & Probability
The mean of the distribution whose probability density function f(x) = {αe^(−αx), x ≥ 0; 0, x < 0} is
(1) 0
(2) 1
(3) 1/α
(4) α
= 3
~ This is the exponential distribution with rate α. Mean = ∫₀^∞ x·αe^(−αx) dx; integrating by parts, = [−x e^(−αx)]₀^∞ + ∫₀^∞ e^(−αx) dx = 0 + 1/α = 1/α.

Q 132 | dms | Combinatorics & Probability
For a poisson distribution
(1) Mean = standard deviation
(2) Mean = variance
(3) Mean = mean deviation
(4) Mean = median
= 2
~ For a Poisson distribution with parameter λ, P(X = k) = e^(−λ)λᵏ/k!, and both the mean E[X] and the variance Var(X) equal λ. The standard deviation is √λ, so it equals the mean only when λ = 1.

Q 133 | emt | Linear Algebra
The Eigen values of a matrix are 1,2,−1 then the sum of Eigenvalues of the inverse of that matrix are
(1) 0
(2) 1/2
(3) 2
(4) 4
= 2
~ If Av = λv and A is invertible, then A⁻¹v = (1/λ)v, so the eigenvalues of A⁻¹ are the reciprocals 1, 1/2 and −1. Their sum is 1 + 1/2 − 1 = 1/2.

Q 134 | emt | Linear Algebra
The possible Eigenvector of the matrix [[3, 0, −2], [0, 2, 0], [−2, 0, 0]] corresponding to one of its Eigen value (-1) is
(1) [1, 1, 1]ᵀ
(2) [1, 0, 2]ᵀ
(3) [−1, 0, 12]ᵀ
(4) [2, 0, 1]ᵀ
= 2
~ The y-component separates (eigenvalue 2), and the x–z part [[3, −2], [−2, 0]] gives λ² − 3λ − 4 = 0, so λ = 4 or −1. For λ = −1, (A + I)v = 0 gives 4x − 2z = 0 and 3y = 0, so z = 2x, y = 0 and v = (1, 0, 2). Check: A(1, 0, 2)ᵀ = (3 − 4, 0, −2)ᵀ = (−1, 0, −2)ᵀ = −1·(1, 0, 2)ᵀ. Q 135 | emt | Numerical Methods
In the LU factorization method, to solve the system of equations AX=B, we solve the system of equations in the order
(1) First UX=Y and then LY=B
(2) First LY=B and then UX=Y
(3) First LY=X and then UY=B
(4) First UY=B and then LX=Y
= 2
~ With A = LU the system becomes L(UX) = B. Putting UX = Y, we first solve LY = B for Y by forward substitution (L is lower triangular), and then solve UX = Y for X by back substitution (U is upper triangular).

Q 136 | emt | Numerical Methods
If f(a)f(b) < 0, then at least one root of the equation f(x) = 0 lies between a and b. The repeated application of this is
(1) Newton-Raphson method
(2) Bisection method
(3) Secant method
(4) LU decomposition method
= 2
~ The bisection method starts from an interval [a, b] with f(a)f(b) < 0, evaluates f at the midpoint c = (a + b)/2, and keeps whichever half [a, c] or [c, b] still has a sign change; repeating this halves the interval containing the root each time. Newton–Raphson and the secant method use tangents/secants and do not need a sign change.

Q 137 | emt | Calculus
If f(x) = (e^(1/x) − 1)/(e^(1/x) + 1) for x ≠ 0, f(0) = 0 then at x=0 f(x) is
(1) continuous
(2) discontinuous
(3) differentiable
(4) continuous but not differentiable
= 2
~ As x → 0⁺, 1/x → +∞, so f(x) = (1 − e^(−1/x))/(1 + e^(−1/x)) → 1. As x → 0⁻, 1/x → −∞, e^(1/x) → 0, so f(x) → (0 − 1)/(0 + 1) = −1. The left and right limits differ (−1 ≠ 1), so the limit does not exist and f is discontinuous at x = 0 (and therefore not differentiable either).

Q 138 | emt | Calculus
If y = √(x + √(x + √(x……..∞))), then dy/dx =
(1) 1/y
(2) 1/x
(3) 1/(2x−1)
(4) 1/(2y−1)
= 4
~ Since the expression under the first root repeats itself, y = √(x + y), so y² = x + y. Differentiating, 2y·(dy/dx) = 1 + dy/dx, so (2y − 1)(dy/dx) = 1 and dy/dx = 1/(2y − 1).

Q 139 | emt | Calculus
∫₋₁¹ (x − [2x]) dx =
(1) 1
(2) 0
(3) 2
(4) 4
= 1
~ ∫₋₁¹ x dx = 0 since x is odd. [2x] is the greatest integer function: it is −2 on [−1, −1/2), −1 on [−1/2, 0), 0 on [0, 1/2) and 1 on [1/2, 1), each interval of length 1/2, so ∫₋₁¹ [2x] dx = (1/2)(−2 − 1 + 0 + 1) = −1. Hence the integral = 0 − (−1) = 1.

Q 140 | prg | C Programming
Consider the following C declaration
```
int (*f)();
```
Which of the following statements correctly interprets the above declaration?
(1) f is a function which returns an integer
(2) f is a function which returns a pointer to an integer
(3) f is a function to pointer which returns an integer
(4) f is pointer to a function which returns an integer
= 4
~ The parentheses bind * to f first, so f is a pointer; the () after (*f) says it points to a function, and int is that function's return type. Without the parentheses, int *f(); would declare a function returning a pointer to int (option 2), and int f(); a function returning int (option 1).

Q 141 | cnw | Transport & Application Layers
Which of the following statements is false about DNS?
(1) DNS uses TCP for its implementation
(2) DNS uses both TCP and UDP for its implementation.
(3) DNS is implemented as a distributed database system.
(4) DNS maps internet domain name onto the IP address.
= 1
~ DNS is a hierarchical, distributed database that maps domain names to IP addresses. Ordinary queries and replies are carried over UDP port 53, and TCP (port 53) is used for zone transfers and for replies too large for UDP. So (2) is true, and (1), which says DNS is implemented over TCP and ignores UDP, its main transport, is the false statement.

Q 142 | dst | Arrays, Stacks, Queues & Linked Lists
Which of the following is the correct sequence of the statements to be executed to insert a node after a node whose address is p? The pointer field nextptr points to the next node and the address of the new node is q.
(1) p->nextptr=q; q->nextptr=p;
(2) p->nextptr=q; q->nextptr=p->nextptr;
(3) q->nextptr=p->nextptr; p->nextptr=q;
(4) q->nextptr=p->nextptr; p->nextptr=q->nextptr;
= 3
~ First the new node must point to p's old successor (q->nextptr = p->nextptr), and only then can p be made to point to the new node (p->nextptr = q). In option (2) the order is reversed: after p->nextptr = q, the statement q->nextptr = p->nextptr makes q point to itself and the rest of the list is lost.

Q 143 | cnw | Network Models & Data Link Layer
A bit string, 0111101111101111110, needs to be transmitted at the data link layer. What is the string actually transmitted after the bit stuffing?
(1) 011110111110011111100
(2) 011110111110011111010
(3) 011110111110111111000
(4) 011110011111011111100
= 2
~ With the flag 01111110, the sender inserts a 0 after every run of five consecutive 1s in the data. The data is 0 1111 0 11111 0 111111 0: a 0 is stuffed after the run 11111 (before the original 0), and another after the first five 1s of the run 111111. Result: 0 1111 0 11111 0 0 11111 0 1 0 = 011110111110011111010 (19 data bits + 2 stuffed bits = 21 bits).

Q 144 | cnw | Network Models & Data Link Layer
A channel has a bit rate of 4000 bps and a propagation delay of 20 msec. For what range of frame sizes(S) (in number of bits) does stop-and-wait protocol give an efficiency of at least 50 percent?
(1) S ≤ 160
(2) S ≥ 160
(3) 80 ≤ S ≤ 160
(4) S ≥ 40, 20 ≤ S ≤ 30
= 2
~ Stop-and-wait efficiency = Tt/(Tt + 2Tp), where Tt = S/4000 s is the transmission time. Efficiency ≥ 1/2 requires Tt ≥ 2Tp = 2 × 20 ms = 40 ms, so S ≥ 4000 × 0.04 = 160 bits.

Q 145 | cnw | Network Models & Data Link Layer
Which of the following sliding window protocols has the receiver window size more than 1?
(1) Stop-and-wait protocol
(2) One-bit window protocol
(3) Go-Back-N protocol
(4) Selective Repeat protocol
= 4
~ In Selective Repeat the receiver accepts and buffers out-of-order frames within its window and asks only for the missing ones to be resent, so its receive window is greater than 1 (up to 2ᵐ⁻¹ for m-bit sequence numbers). Stop-and-wait (the one-bit sliding window protocol) has window 1 at both ends, and Go-Back-N has a sender window up to 2ᵐ − 1 but a receiver window of 1, since it accepts frames only in order.

Q 146 | cnw | Network Models & Data Link Layer
Consider building a CSMA/CD network running at 1 Gbps over a 1-km cable with no repeaters. The signal speed in the cable is 200,000 km/sec. What is the minimum frame size?
(1) 10⁶ bits
(2) 64 bytes
(3) 1250 bytes
(4) 125 bytes
= 3
~ One-way propagation time Tp = 1 km/(200,000 km/s) = 5 µs. For a collision to be detected before the sender finishes, a frame must last at least the round-trip time 2Tp = 10 µs, so the minimum frame size = 10⁹ bits/s × 10 × 10⁻⁶ s = 10,000 bits = 1250 bytes.

Q 147 | cnw | IP Addressing & Routing
Which one of the following statements is incorrect about Count-to-infinity problem?
(1) It reacts rapidly to good news
(2) It reacts slowly to good news
(3) It reacts leisurely to bad news
(4) Distance Vector Routing suffers from Count-to-infinity problem
= 2
~ In distance vector routing, good news (a new or shorter route) spreads quickly, one hop per exchange of vectors. Bad news (a link or router going down) spreads very slowly, because routers keep raising their distances to the lost destination step by step towards "infinity"; this is the count-to-infinity problem. So "it reacts slowly to good news" is the incorrect statement.

Q 148 | cnw | IP Addressing & Routing
A network on the Internet has a subnet mask of 255.255.240.0. What is the maximum number of hosts it can handle?
(1) 4096
(2) 3556
(3) 4080
(4) 4094
= 4
~ 240 = 11110000₂, so the mask has 8 + 8 + 4 = 20 network bits and 4 + 8 = 12 host bits. That gives 2¹² = 4096 addresses, of which the all-0s (network) and all-1s (broadcast) host parts cannot be assigned: 4096 − 2 = 4094 hosts.

Q 149 | cnw | Transport & Application Layers
Consider a transport layer with maximum segment size as 1KB. Also, consider at a particular point of time, the parameters receiver's window, congestion window are set to 1024KB, 256KB respectively, at which time, the transmission of a packet resulted in a timeout. What will be the values of congestion window and threshold respectively for the next transmission?
(1) 1024KB, 256KB
(2) 1KB, 128KB
(3) 1KB,256KB
(4) 1024KB, 128Kb
= 2
~ On a timeout, TCP sets the threshold to half of the current congestion window, 256 KB/2 = 128 KB, and restarts slow start with the congestion window at 1 MSS = 1 KB. The receiver's window (1024 KB) only caps the sending window and does not change here. So the next values are 1 KB and 128 KB.

Q 150 | cnw | IP Addressing & Routing
Dynamic Host Configuration Protocol (DHCP) is used to:
(1) assign CIDR subnet masks to routers
(2) allocate the host part of an IP address to a host
(3) assign the network component of an IP address to a router
(4) manage the assignment of MAC addresses
= 2
~ DHCP lets a host joining a network get its IP configuration automatically: a DHCP server leases it an unused address from that network's pool (along with the mask, default gateway and DNS server). The network part is fixed by the network the host is on, so what DHCP actually allocates is the host part. It does not configure routers, and MAC addresses are built into the network card.
