# NCERT Class 11 Geography, Fundamentals of Physical Geography, Chapter 4: Distribution of Oceans and Continents
# Questions written by Learn with Anil from the chapter (current NCERT edition, Reprint 2026-27). November 2026.

Q 1 | geo | Fundamentals of Physical Geography
According to the chapter, continents cover about what share of the earth's surface?
(1) 29 per cent
(2) 50 per cent
(3) 71 per cent
(4) 15 per cent
= 1
~ About 29 per cent of the surface is land; the rest is covered by oceans.

Q 2 | geo | Fundamentals of Physical Geography
Who first suggested, in 1596, that the Americas, Europe and Africa may once have been joined together?
(1) Alfred Wegener
(2) Abraham Ortelius
(3) Antonio Pellegrini
(4) Harry Hess
= 2
~ The Dutch map maker Abraham Ortelius made this suggestion in 1596. Antonio Pellegrini later drew a map showing the three continents together.

Q 3 | geo | Fundamentals of Physical Geography
Consider the following statements about the continental drift theory:
1. It was put forward in 1912 by Alfred Wegener, a German meteorologist.
2. Wegener called the single super continent "Panthalassa" and the mega ocean around it "Pangaea".
Which of the statements given above is/are correct?
(1) 1 only
(2) 2 only
(3) Both 1 and 2
(4) Neither 1 nor 2
= 1
~ Statement 2 reverses the names: Pangaea ("all earth") was the super continent and Panthalassa ("all water") was the mega ocean.

Q 4 | geo | Fundamentals of Physical Geography
According to Wegener, when Pangaea began to split about 200 million years ago, the two large landmasses formed were:
(1) Lemuria and Atlantis
(2) Panthalassa and Tethys
(3) Eurasia and Antarctica
(4) Laurasia and Gondwanaland
= 4
~ Pangaea broke into Laurasia in the north and Gondwanaland in the south, which later split into the present continents.

Q 5 | geo | Fundamentals of Physical Geography
In 1964, Bullard used a computer program to show that the shorelines of Africa and South America fit together remarkably well. The fit was matched at the:
(1) Coastline at high tide
(2) 1,000-fathom line
(3) Equator
(4) Tropic of Capricorn
= 2
~ The best fit was found along the 1,000-fathom line, the edge of the continental shelf, rather than the present coastlines. This "jig-saw fit" supports continental drift.

Q 6 | geo | Fundamentals of Physical Geography
"Tillite", used as evidence for continental drift, is:
(1) A volcanic rock found on mid-oceanic ridges
(2) A gold-bearing vein
(3) A fossil of an ancient reptile
(4) A sedimentary rock formed from glacial deposits
= 4
~ Tillite forms from deposits left by glaciers. Gondwana tillites of India have counterparts in Africa, the Falkland Islands, Madagascar, Antarctica and Australia, showing these lands were once together.

Q 7 | geo | Fundamentals of Physical Geography
Rich placer deposits of gold occur on the Ghana coast although there is no source rock in that region. The chapter explains this as evidence that:
(1) Rivers carried the gold from the Himalayas
(2) Volcanoes in Ghana erupted gold
(3) Gold forms in sea water
(4) Ghana's gold came from gold-bearing veins in Brazil when Africa and South America lay side by side
= 4
~ The gold-bearing veins are in Brazil. The Ghana deposits make sense only if the two continents were once joined.

Q 8 | geo | Fundamentals of Physical Geography
Mesosaurus, a small reptile adapted to shallow brackish water, has been found in South Africa and Brazil. Why is this important for the drift theory?
(1) Its remains are found only in deep oceans
(2) The two places are about 4,800 km apart with an ocean between them, which the reptile could not have crossed
(3) It shows that reptiles could fly across oceans
(4) It proves that Brazil and South Africa were always far apart
= 2
~ Identical Mesosaurus remains on two continents now separated by the Atlantic suggest the lands were once joined.

Q 9 | geo | Fundamentals of Physical Geography
The idea of a contiguous landmass called "Lemuria" was based on the presence of lemurs in:
(1) India, Madagascar and Africa
(2) Brazil, Ghana and Australia
(3) Antarctica, Chile and New Zealand
(4) Europe, Greenland and Canada
= 1
~ Lemurs found in India, Madagascar and Africa led some scientists to suggest these lands were once linked, as "Lemuria".

Q 10 | geo | Fundamentals of Physical Geography
Which two forces did Wegener suggest caused the continents to drift?
(1) Convection currents and slab pull
(2) Gravity and magnetism
(3) Pole-fleeing force and tidal force
(4) Coriolis force and friction
= 3
~ Wegener proposed the pole-fleeing force (linked to the earth's rotation and equatorial bulge) and the tidal force (attraction of the moon and sun). Most scholars found them too weak.

Q 11 | geo | Fundamentals of Physical Geography
Who discussed, in the 1930s, the possibility of convection currents in the mantle caused by heat from radioactive elements?
(1) Harry Hess
(2) McKenzie
(3) Arthur Holmes
(4) Edwin Hubble
= 3
~ Arthur Holmes suggested that radioactive heating drives convection currents in the mantle. This idea later explained the force behind plate movement.

Q 12 | geo | Fundamentals of Physical Geography
Based on depth and relief, the ocean floor is divided into which three major divisions in the chapter?
(1) Continental margins, abyssal plains and mid-oceanic ridges
(2) Trenches, seamounts and atolls
(3) Shelves, canyons and guyots
(4) Islands, bays and estuaries
= 1
~ Continental margins link the continents to the deep sea, abyssal plains lie between the margins and the ridges, and the mid-oceanic ridges form an interconnected chain of mountains.

Q 13 | geo | Fundamentals of Physical Geography
The concept of "sea floor spreading" was put forward in 1961 by:
(1) Alfred Wegener
(2) Arthur Holmes
(3) Morgan
(4) Harry Hess
= 4
~ Harry Hess argued that new crust forms at the mid-oceanic ridges and spreads away, while older crust sinks into the trenches.

Q 14 | geo | Fundamentals of Physical Geography
Consider the following observations that led to the idea of sea floor spreading:
1. Rocks closer to the mid-oceanic ridges are younger, and age increases away from the crest.
2. The rocks of the oceanic crust are nowhere older than 200 million years.
3. Sediments on the ocean floor are unexpectedly very thin.
Which of the observations given above are mentioned in the chapter?
(1) 1 and 2 only
(2) 2 and 3 only
(3) 1 and 3 only
(4) 1, 2 and 3
= 4
~ All three are listed. In contrast, some continental rocks are about 3,200 million years old. Rocks on both sides of the crest also match in age, composition and magnetic properties.

Q 15 | geo | Fundamentals of Physical Geography
The theory of plate tectonics was put forward in 1967 by:
(1) McKenzie and Parker, and also Morgan
(2) Wegener and Holmes
(3) Hess and Bullard
(4) Ortelius and Pellegrini
= 1
~ In 1967 McKenzie and Parker, and independently Morgan, outlined plate tectonics, building on continental drift and sea floor spreading.

Q 16 | geo | Fundamentals of Physical Geography
Which one of the following is a MINOR plate, not one of the seven major plates listed in the chapter?
(1) Pacific plate
(2) India–Australia–New Zealand plate
(3) Nazca plate
(4) Eurasia and adjacent oceanic plate
= 3
~ The seven major plates are the Antarctic, North American, South American, Pacific, India–Australia–New Zealand, African and Eurasian plates. Cocos, Nazca, Arabian, Philippine and Caroline are minor plates.

Q 17 | geo | Fundamentals of Physical Geography
Match the plate boundaries with their descriptions:
A. Divergent — 1. Crust is neither produced nor destroyed as plates slide past each other
B. Convergent — 2. New crust is generated as plates pull apart
C. Transform — 3. Crust is destroyed as one plate dives under another
Select the correct answer:
(1) A-1, B-2, C-3
(2) A-2, B-3, C-1
(3) A-3, B-1, C-2
(4) A-2, B-1, C-3
= 2
~ The Mid-Atlantic Ridge is a divergent boundary, subduction zones are convergent, and transform faults usually run at right angles to the mid-oceanic ridges.

Q 18 | geo | Fundamentals of Physical Geography
Where is the fastest rate of plate movement mentioned in the chapter, at more than 15 cm a year?
(1) Arctic Ridge
(2) Mid-Atlantic Ridge near Iceland
(3) East Pacific Rise near Easter Island
(4) Himalayan collision zone
= 3
~ The East Pacific Rise near Easter Island spreads at over 15 cm a year, while the Arctic Ridge is the slowest at under 2.5 cm a year.

Q 19 | geo | Fundamentals of Physical Geography
Consider the following statements about the movement of the Indian plate:
1. India began its northward journey about 200 million years ago.
2. The Deccan Traps outpouring started about 200 million years ago.
3. India collided with Asia about 40–50 million years ago, leading to the rapid uplift of the Himalayas.
Which of the statements given above are correct?
(1) 1 and 2 only
(2) 2 and 3 only
(3) 1 and 3 only
(4) 1, 2 and 3
= 3
~ The Deccan Traps outpouring started about 60 million years ago, when India was close to the equator. India began moving north about 200 million years ago and collided with Asia about 40–50 million years ago.

Q 20 | geo | Fundamentals of Physical Geography
The northern boundary of the Indian plate, along the Himalayas, is an example of:
(1) An ocean–ocean convergence
(2) A continent–continent convergence
(3) A transform boundary
(4) A divergent boundary
= 2
~ The Himalayas mark a continent–continent collision. In the east the boundary runs through the Rakinyoma mountains of Myanmar towards the Java Trench, and in the west it follows the Kirthar mountains and the Makran coast.
