Q 1 | his | Themes in World History
The name "Mesopotamia" comes from Greek words meaning:
(1) Land of two seas
(2) Land of the city-states
(3) Land of writing
(4) Land between the rivers
= 4
~ Mesos means middle and potamos means river. Mesopotamia is the land between the Euphrates and the Tigris, now part of the Republic of Iraq.

Q 2 | his | Themes in World History
Mesopotamia lies mostly in which present-day country?
(1) Egypt
(2) Iraq
(3) Turkey
(4) Iran
= 2
~ The rivers Tigris and Euphrates flow through present-day Iraq. Its civilisation is known for its prosperity, city life, literature, mathematics and astronomy.

Q 3 | his | Themes in World History
The earliest Mesopotamian tablets, written around 3200 BCE, mostly contained:
(1) Poems about kings and gods
(2) Law codes
(3) Letters between rulers
(4) Lists of goods such as oxen, fish and bread, kept as records of transactions
= 4
~ The first known writing was about keeping records. It was needed in cities where goods came in, went out and were exchanged, so that people could remember who owed what.

Q 4 | his | Themes in World History
The term "cuneiform" for the Mesopotamian script comes from Latin words meaning:
(1) Clay and reed
(2) Sign and sound
(3) Picture and word
(4) Wedge and shape
= 4
~ Cuneus means wedge and forma means shape. Scribes pressed a sharpened reed into damp clay, which left wedge-shaped marks.

Q 5 | his | Themes in World History
Mesopotamian writing was done on:
(1) Clay tablets, written on with a sharp reed while the clay was damp and then dried in the sun
(2) Papyrus rolls
(3) Palm leaves
(4) Silk cloth
= 1
~ Once a tablet dried hard, it could not be changed. So each transaction, however small, needed a separate tablet. That is why Mesopotamian sites have thousands of tablets.

Q 6 | his | Themes in World History
Consider the following statements about literacy in Mesopotamia:
1. Very few Mesopotamians could read and write.
2. A sign stood for a syllable, not a single consonant or vowel, so there were hundreds of signs to learn.
Which of the statements given above is/are correct?
(1) 1 only
(2) 2 only
(3) Both 1 and 2
(4) Neither 1 nor 2
= 3
~ Scribes had to learn hundreds of signs, and handle a wet tablet before it dried. Writing was a skilled job, so literacy was limited to a few.

Q 7 | his | Themes in World History
According to a Mesopotamian story, which king of Uruk is said to have first used writing, because his messenger could not remember a long message?
(1) Gilgamesh
(2) Sargon
(3) Enmerkar
(4) Zimrilim
= 3
~ Enmerkar wanted lapis lazuli from the distant land of Aratta. When the messenger could not carry the long message, the king patted some clay and wrote it down, according to the story.

Q 8 | his | Themes in World History
Which of the following was among the earliest temple towns of Mesopotamia, and by about 3000 BCE one of its largest cities?
(1) Mari
(2) Babylon
(3) Uruk
(4) Nineveh
= 3
~ Uruk grew to about 250 hectares by 3000 BCE. Its craftspeople produced fine work such as the Warka Head, a woman's head sculpted in white marble.

Q 9 | his | Themes in World History
Mesopotamia was rich in food, but it lacked minerals and wood. Which of the following best describes how it obtained metals, stone and timber?
(1) By trade, sending textiles and farm produce in return, with places such as present-day Turkey, Iran and lands across the Gulf
(2) By mining in the river valleys
(3) It never used metals
(4) By growing timber in the southern desert
= 1
~ Copper, tin, silver, gold, shell and various stones were brought from other regions. Organising this trade was one reason the city's institutions grew strong.

Q 10 | his | Themes in World History
The cylinder seal of Mesopotamia was used by:
(1) Stamping it like a coin
(2) Rolling it over wet clay to leave a picture, for example on a sealed bundle of goods or on a letter
(3) Wearing it as a crown
(4) Burning it in a temple fire
= 2
~ A cylinder of stone with a carving on its surface was rolled over clay. It marked ownership and showed that the goods or letter had not been tampered with.

Q 11 | his | Themes in World History
Which of the following is true of the town of Ur, excavated in the 1930s?
(1) It had straight, wide streets laid out in a grid
(2) Its streets were narrow and winding, and houses had no windows on the street side; light came from courtyards
(3) Each house had a covered drain joined to a city drain
(4) It had no houses, only temples
= 2
~ There was no town planning in Ur. Drains were in the inner courtyards of houses, and rooms opened onto the courtyard, which gave privacy.

Q 12 | his | Themes in World History
Which of the following was a superstition about houses recorded in Ur?
(1) Houses must face east
(2) A raised threshold brought wealth, and a front door should not open towards another house
(3) Houses must have three storeys
(4) Doors must be painted blue
= 2
~ Omen texts from Ur list such beliefs. They show how ordinary townspeople thought about luck and their homes.

Q 13 | his | Themes in World History
Mari, a royal capital that flourished after 2000 BCE, stood:
(1) On the Tigris near the Gulf
(2) In the mountains of Iran
(3) Upstream on the Euphrates, where farming and herding communities lived side by side
(4) On the coast of the Mediterranean
= 3
~ Mari's kings were Amorites, whose ancestors were pastoral people. The city was on a trade route, and its officials checked boats and taxed the cargo passing by.

Q 14 | his | Themes in World History
King Zimrilim of Mari (1810-1760 BCE) is known for his:
(1) Great palace of about 260 rooms, the royal residence and centre of administration
(2) Collection of law codes
(3) Library at Nineveh
(4) Building of the first ziggurat
= 1
~ The palace was famous across the region. Its records tell us about the food served at the king's table and the visitors who came.

Q 15 | his | Themes in World History
The Epic of Gilgamesh, written on twelve tablets, tells of a king of Uruk who, after the death of his friend Enkidu, set out to:
(1) Conquer Egypt
(2) Build a temple to the moon god
(3) Find a new river
(4) Find the secret of immortality, and in the end took comfort in the city walls his people had built
= 4
~ Gilgamesh failed to become immortal. He returned to Uruk and took pride in its walls, a sign of how Mesopotamians valued their cities.

Q 16 | his | Themes in World History
Which of the following do we owe to Mesopotamian time-reckoning?
(1) The seven-day week only
(2) The solar year of 365 days
(3) The decimal system
(4) The division of a day into 24 hours and an hour into 60 minutes
= 4
~ The division of the year into 12 months, of the month into four weeks, the day into 24 hours and the hour into 60 minutes has come down to us from the Mesopotamians.

Q 17 | his | Themes in World History
Mesopotamian mathematics, preserved in tablets, included:
(1) Only counting up to ten
(2) Multiplication and division tables, square and square-root tables, and tables of compound interest
(3) Calculus
(4) Logarithm tables
= 2
~ Mesopotamian tablets of about 1800 BCE have such tables. One even gives the square root of 2 to a close approximation.

Q 18 | his | Themes in World History
Assurbanipal (668-627 BCE), an Assyrian king, is remembered for:
(1) Collecting a library at his capital Nineveh, with tablets on history, epics, omens and more
(2) Inventing the potter's wheel
(3) Founding the city of Uruk
(4) Writing the Epic of Gilgamesh himself
= 1
~ He had tablets copied and collected from the south. The library shows the value Mesopotamians placed on their past.

Q 19 | his | Themes in World History
Nabonidus, the last ruler of independent Babylon, showed a remarkable interest in:
(1) The past, recording, for example, a statue of Sargon of Akkad found in a temple
(2) Sea voyages
(3) Iron weapons
(4) Building pyramids
= 1
~ Nabonidus had a damaged statue of Sargon repaired and recorded the find. It shows a sense of history in Mesopotamia.

Q 20 | his | Themes in World History
Which of the following is the best summary of the legacy of Mesopotamia given in the chapter?
(1) Its pyramids and mummies
(2) Its iron weapons and empires
(3) Its scholarly tradition of time-reckoning and mathematics, and above all its writing
(4) Its sea trade with China
= 3
~ The chapter stresses that Mesopotamia's greatest legacy to the world is its writing, together with its traditions of calculation and record-keeping that spread to other lands.
