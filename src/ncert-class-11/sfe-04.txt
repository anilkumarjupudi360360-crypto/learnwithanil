# NCERT Class 11 Economics, Statistics for Economics, Chapter 4: Presentation of Data
# Questions written by Learn with Anil from the chapter (current NCERT edition, Reprint 2026-27). 16 Nov 2026.

Q 1 | eco | Statistics for Economics
Which are the three ways of presenting data discussed in the chapter?
(1) Primary, secondary and tertiary
(2) Textual (descriptive), tabular and diagrammatic
(3) Census, sample and pilot
(4) Mean, median and mode
= 2
~ Data can be presented in text, in tables and in diagrams. The choice depends on the amount of data and the purpose.

Q 2 | eco | Statistics for Economics
Textual (descriptive) presentation of data is most suitable when:
(1) The data are very large and complex
(2) Data are to be compared over many years
(3) Data have many categories
(4) The quantity of data is not too large
= 4
~ When there are only a few figures, they can be included in the text itself. Large data are better shown in tables or diagrams.

Q 3 | eco | Statistics for Economics
In a statistical table, the column headings are called:
(1) Captions
(2) Stubs
(3) The body
(4) The source
= 1
~ Captions are the headings of the columns. Stubs are the headings of the rows, and the body contains the actual figures.

Q 4 | eco | Statistics for Economics
In a statistical table, the row headings are called:
(1) Captions
(2) Titles
(3) Stubs
(4) Notes
= 3
~ The row designations are called stubs. Together with the captions, they tell the reader what each figure in the body stands for.

Q 5 | eco | Statistics for Economics
The part of a table that tells the reader where the data came from, usually given at the bottom, is the:
(1) Source
(2) Caption
(3) Stub
(4) Table number
= 1
~ The source note gives the origin of the data, such as a government report, so readers can check them. A table also has a number, title, captions, stubs, body, unit of measurement and notes.

Q 6 | eco | Statistics for Economics
Which statement about a simple bar diagram is correct?
(1) Bars may be of different widths
(2) Bars touch each other with no gaps
(3) It can be used only for continuous variables
(4) Bars are of equal width with equal gaps, and the height of each bar shows the value it represents
= 4
~ In a bar diagram, width carries no meaning; only height does. Bars have equal width and equal spacing, and the diagram suits discrete or qualitative categories.

Q 7 | eco | Statistics for Economics
A multiple bar diagram is used when:
(1) Only one set of figures is shown
(2) Two or more sets of data are compared, such as exports and imports of a country over several years
(3) A total is divided into its parts
(4) Data form a continuous frequency distribution
= 2
~ Multiple bars, placed side by side for each year or category, make comparisons between two or more data sets easy.

Q 8 | eco | Statistics for Economics
A component bar diagram (sub-diagram) is used to show:
(1) Changes over time only
(2) A frequency distribution
(3) The parts that make up a total, for example enrolment of boys and girls in different classes
(4) The relationship between two variables
= 3
~ Each bar shows a total and is divided into segments for its components, so both the total and its make-up are visible.

Q 9 | eco | Statistics for Economics
In a pie diagram, a component that forms 25 per cent of the total is shown by a sector with an angle of:
(1) 25 degrees
(2) 45 degrees
(3) 90 degrees
(4) 180 degrees
= 3
~ Angle = (component ÷ total) × 360° = 0.25 × 360° = 90°. Equivalently, multiply the percentage by 3.6.

Q 10 | eco | Statistics for Economics
A family spends ₹2,400 on food out of a total monthly expenditure of ₹7,200. In a pie diagram of its expenditure, the angle for food is:
(1) 120 degrees
(2) 100 degrees
(3) 33 degrees
(4) 240 degrees
= 1
~ Angle = (2,400 ÷ 7,200) × 360° = (1/3) × 360° = 120°.

Q 11 | eco | Statistics for Economics
A histogram is:
(1) A circle divided into sectors
(2) A set of bars with gaps between them
(3) A line joining values over time
(4) A set of adjacent rectangles with the class intervals as bases and the frequencies as heights, with no gaps between them
= 4
~ In a histogram the rectangles touch each other because the classes are continuous. The area of each rectangle represents the frequency of its class.

Q 12 | eco | Statistics for Economics
A histogram is drawn for:
(1) Qualitative data such as religion
(2) A continuous variable grouped into a frequency distribution
(3) A time series
(4) The components of a total
= 2
~ A histogram represents a frequency distribution of a continuous variable. Qualitative and discrete categories are shown with bar diagrams instead.

Q 13 | eco | Statistics for Economics
When a frequency distribution has unequal class intervals, what must be done before drawing a histogram?
(1) Nothing; heights are always the frequencies
(2) The classes must be dropped
(3) The data must be shown as a pie diagram instead
(4) The frequencies must be adjusted (for example, frequency per unit of class width), because the area of each rectangle must be proportional to frequency
= 4
~ Since area, not height alone, represents frequency, wider classes would look too big unless the frequencies are adjusted for the class width.

Q 14 | eco | Statistics for Economics
Which measure of central tendency can be found graphically from a histogram?
(1) Mean
(2) Median
(3) Mode
(4) Weighted mean
= 3
~ The mode can be located from the tallest rectangle of a histogram. The median is located graphically using ogives.

Q 15 | eco | Statistics for Economics
A frequency polygon is drawn by:
(1) Joining the tops of bars in a bar diagram
(2) Joining the mid-points of the tops of adjacent rectangles of a histogram, and closing it at both ends on the x-axis
(3) Drawing a circle and dividing it into sectors
(4) Plotting cumulative frequencies against class limits
= 2
~ The polygon is closed by joining it to the mid-points of hypothetical classes before the first and after the last class on the x-axis. It can also be drawn by plotting frequencies against class marks.

Q 16 | eco | Statistics for Economics
A frequency curve is obtained by:
(1) Drawing a smooth freehand curve through the points of a frequency polygon
(2) Joining the upper class limits with straight lines
(3) Drawing rectangles of equal width
(4) Plotting the data on a pie chart
= 1
~ The frequency curve smooths out the angles of the frequency polygon by passing a freehand curve through its points.

Q 17 | eco | Statistics for Economics
An ogive is also known as:
(1) A pie diagram
(2) A component bar diagram
(3) A cumulative frequency curve
(4) A time series graph
= 3
~ An ogive plots cumulative frequencies. There are two kinds: the 'less than' ogive and the 'more than' ogive.

Q 18 | eco | Statistics for Economics
The point where the 'less than' ogive and the 'more than' ogive intersect gives the:
(1) Median
(2) Mean
(3) Mode
(4) Range
= 1
~ At the point of intersection, the number of observations below and above is equal, so its x-value is the median.

Q 19 | eco | Statistics for Economics
A 'less than' ogive is drawn by plotting:
(1) Lower class limits against 'more than' cumulative frequencies
(2) Upper class limits against 'less than' cumulative frequencies, giving a rising curve
(3) Class marks against simple frequencies
(4) Years against values
= 2
~ The 'less than' ogive uses upper class limits and rises as cumulative frequencies grow. The 'more than' ogive uses lower class limits and falls.

Q 20 | eco | Statistics for Economics
An arithmetic line graph (time series graph) is drawn with:
(1) Frequencies on the x-axis and class marks on the y-axis
(2) Components shown as sectors of a circle
(3) Two variables in a two-way table
(4) Time on the x-axis and the values of the variable on the y-axis, which helps in studying trends over time
= 4
~ A time series graph shows how a variable, such as exports or prices, changes over time, and helps in understanding its trend and periodicity.
