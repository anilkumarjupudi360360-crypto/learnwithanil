Q 1 | eco | Statistics for Economics
Correlation studies and measures:
(1) The average of a variable
(2) The spread of a single variable
(3) Changes in prices over time
(4) The direction and intensity of the relationship between variables
= 4
~ Correlation tells us whether two variables move together (direction) and how closely (intensity). It measures covariation, not causation.

Q 2 | eco | Statistics for Economics
Income and consumption expenditure of households usually show:
(1) No correlation
(2) Positive correlation
(3) Negative correlation
(4) Perfect negative correlation
= 2
~ When income rises, consumption also tends to rise. Variables that move in the same direction are positively correlated.

Q 3 | eco | Statistics for Economics
The price of a good and the quantity demanded usually show:
(1) Negative correlation
(2) Positive correlation
(3) No correlation
(4) Perfect positive correlation
= 1
~ When price rises, quantity demanded usually falls. Variables that move in opposite directions are negatively correlated.

Q 4 | eco | Statistics for Economics
Which statement about correlation and causation is correct?
(1) A high correlation proves that one variable causes the other
(2) Correlation can be calculated only when one variable causes the other
(3) Correlation measures covariation, not causation; a high correlation does not prove that one variable causes the other
(4) Correlation and causation mean the same thing
= 3
~ Two variables may move together because of chance or a third factor affecting both. Correlation should never be interpreted as cause and effect by itself.

Q 5 | eco | Statistics for Economics
Which statement about the scatter diagram is correct?
(1) It gives the exact numerical value of correlation
(2) It can be drawn only for qualitative data
(3) It gives a visual idea of the nature of the relationship, without a numerical measure
(4) It uses ranks instead of values
= 3
~ A scatter diagram plots pairs of values as points. How closely the points cluster around a line shows the strength of the relationship, but it gives no exact number.

Q 6 | eco | Statistics for Economics
Karl Pearson's coefficient of correlation is also known as the:
(1) Product moment correlation coefficient
(2) Rank correlation coefficient
(3) Coefficient of variation
(4) Index number
= 1
~ Karl Pearson's coefficient, r, is also called the product moment correlation coefficient. It measures the degree of linear relationship between two variables.

Q 7 | eco | Statistics for Economics
The value of the correlation coefficient r lies between:
(1) 0 and 1
(2) 0 and 100
(3) −100 and +100
(4) −1 and +1
= 4
~ r can never be less than −1 or more than +1. Values near ±1 show a strong linear relationship; values near 0 show a weak one.

Q 8 | eco | Statistics for Economics
If r = 0 between two variables, it means that:
(1) The variables are perfectly related
(2) There is no linear relationship between them, though a non-linear relationship may exist
(3) One variable causes the other
(4) Both variables are constant
= 2
~ r measures only linear relationship. A value of zero means there is no linear relation, but the variables could still be related in a curved, non-linear way.

Q 9 | eco | Statistics for Economics
r = −1 indicates:
(1) No correlation
(2) Perfect negative linear correlation
(3) Perfect positive linear correlation
(4) A weak negative correlation
= 2
~ When r = −1, all points lie exactly on a downward-sloping straight line: as one variable rises, the other falls in exact proportion.

Q 10 | eco | Statistics for Economics
In which unit is Karl Pearson's correlation coefficient expressed?
(1) In the unit of the first variable
(2) In the unit of the second variable
(3) In percentages
(4) It has no unit; it is a pure number
= 4
~ r is unit-free, so it can compare relationships between variables measured in different units, such as rupees and kilograms.

Q 11 | eco | Statistics for Economics
How is Karl Pearson's coefficient affected by a change of origin and scale of the variables?
(1) It is unaffected by a change of origin and scale
(2) It changes with any change of origin
(3) It doubles when the scale is doubled
(4) It becomes zero
= 1
~ Subtracting a constant from every value (change of origin) or dividing by a positive constant (change of scale) does not change r. This property simplifies calculation.

Q 12 | eco | Statistics for Economics
For two variables, the sum of the products of deviations from their means is Σxy = 40, N = 10, σx = 2 and σy = 4. What is r?
(1) 0.25
(2) 1
(3) 0.5
(4) 0.8
= 3
~ r = Σxy ÷ (N × σx × σy) = 40 ÷ (10 × 2 × 4) = 40 ÷ 80 = 0.5.

Q 13 | eco | Statistics for Economics
Spearman's rank correlation is especially useful when:
(1) The variables cannot be measured precisely, such as beauty or honesty, and only ranks can be given
(2) The data are in a time series
(3) Only one variable is available
(4) The data are already in percentages
= 1
~ Qualities like honesty or beauty cannot be measured, but individuals can be ranked. Spearman's method works with these ranks.

Q 14 | eco | Statistics for Economics
Spearman's rank correlation coefficient is calculated as:
(1) rs = 1 + 6ΣD² ÷ (n³ − n)
(2) rs = 1 − 6ΣD² ÷ (n³ − n)
(3) rs = ΣD² ÷ n
(4) rs = 6ΣD ÷ n²
= 2
~ Here D is the difference between the ranks of each pair and n is the number of pairs. Like r, rs lies between −1 and +1.

Q 15 | eco | Statistics for Economics
Five pairs of ranks give ΣD² = 4. What is Spearman's rank correlation coefficient?
(1) 0.2
(2) 0.6
(3) 0.9
(4) 0.8
= 4
~ rs = 1 − (6 × 4) ÷ (5³ − 5) = 1 − 24 ÷ 120 = 1 − 0.2 = 0.8.

Q 16 | eco | Statistics for Economics
Two judges rank five contestants exactly in reverse order: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 5, 4, 3, 2, 1. What is the rank correlation?
(1) 0
(2) +1
(3) −1
(4) −0.5
= 3
~ The differences are −4, −2, 0, 2, 4, so ΣD² = 16 + 4 + 0 + 4 + 16 = 40. rs = 1 − (6 × 40) ÷ 120 = 1 − 2 = −1, a perfect negative relationship.

Q 17 | eco | Statistics for Economics
Why may rank correlation be preferred to Karl Pearson's coefficient even when data can be measured?
(1) When extreme values are present, because ranks reduce their influence
(2) Because rank correlation always uses more information
(3) Because rank correlation can exceed +1
(4) Because Pearson's coefficient cannot be calculated for numbers
= 1
~ Extreme values can distort Pearson's r. Converting data into ranks limits their effect, so rank correlation is preferred in such cases.

Q 18 | eco | Statistics for Economics
Why is the rank correlation coefficient generally less accurate than Karl Pearson's coefficient?
(1) Because it is always zero
(2) Because it uses a wrong formula
(3) Because it can be used only for small samples
(4) Because it does not use all the information in the data, only the ranks
= 4
~ By replacing actual values with ranks, the size of the differences between values is lost. So when values are reliable, Pearson's r is more precise.

Q 19 | eco | Statistics for Economics
In a scatter diagram, a high degree of positive correlation appears as:
(1) Points spread all over the graph with no pattern
(2) Points lying close to a falling straight line
(3) Points lying close to a rising straight line
(4) All points on the x-axis
= 3
~ When the points cluster closely around an upward-sloping line, the two variables rise together strongly: high positive correlation.

Q 20 | eco | Statistics for Economics
X takes the values 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and Y takes the values 2, 4, 6, 8, 10. What is the correlation coefficient between X and Y?
(1) 0
(2) +1
(3) 2
(4) 0.5
= 2
~ Y is exactly twice X, so all points lie on a rising straight line. The correlation is perfect and positive: r = +1.
