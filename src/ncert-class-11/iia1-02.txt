# Questions written by Learn with Anil from the chapter (current NCERT edition, Reprint 2026-27). 16 Nov 2026.

Q 1 | art | An Introduction to Indian Art, Part I
The arts of the Indus Valley Civilisation emerged during:
(1) The first half of the fourth millennium BCE
(2) The first half of the second millennium BCE
(3) The second half of the third millennium BCE
(4) The first millennium BCE
= 3
~ The chapter opens by placing the arts of the Indus Valley in the second half of the third millennium BCE: sculptures, seals, pottery, jewellery and terracotta figures.

Q 2 | art | An Introduction to Indian Art, Part I
Match the Harappan site with the present-day Indian state in which it lies:
A. Lothal — 1. Gujarat
B. Rakhigarhi — 2. Haryana
C. Ropar — 3. Punjab
D. Kalibangan — 4. Rajasthan
Select the correct answer:
(1) A-1, B-2, C-3, D-4
(2) A-1, B-3, C-2, D-4
(3) A-4, B-2, C-3, D-1
(4) A-2, B-1, C-4, D-3
= 1
~ Lothal and Dholavira are in Gujarat, Rakhigarhi in Haryana, Ropar in Punjab and Kalibangan in Rajasthan. Harappa and Mohenjodaro, the two major cities on the Indus, are in Pakistan.

Q 3 | art | An Introduction to Indian Art, Part I
Consider the following statements about the bust of a bearded man interpreted as a priest:
1. A shawl decorated with trefoil patterns is draped under the right arm and covers the left shoulder.
2. The eyes are slightly elongated and half-closed, as in meditative concentration.
3. The hair is parted in the middle and a plain woven fillet is passed round the head.

(1) 1 only
(2) 1 and 2 only
(3) 2 and 3 only
(4) 1, 2 and 3

~ All three details are given in the chapter. The figure also has a close-cut moustache, short beard and whiskers, ears like double shells, an armlet on the right hand and holes at the neck suggesting a necklace.

Q 4 | art | An Introduction to Indian Art, Part I
The red sandstone male torso from the Indus Valley has socket holes in the neck and shoulders. These were most likely meant for:
(1) Hanging the figure on a wall
(2) Attaching a head and arms made separately
(3) Setting precious stones as ornaments
(4) Pouring offerings over the figure
= 2
~ The socket holes in the neck and shoulders were for attaching the head and arms. The torso has a deliberately frontal posture, well-formed shoulders and a slightly prominent abdomen.

Q 7 | art | An Introduction to Indian Art, Part I
The Harappans cast their metal figures by which technique?
(1) The lost-wax technique
(2) Repoussé (hammering from behind)
(3) Granulation
(4) Sand casting
= 1
~ They used the lost-wax technique: a wax figure was coated with clay, the wax was melted out through a tiny hole, molten metal was poured into the hollow mould, and the clay was removed once the metal cooled.

Q 8 | art | An Introduction to Indian Art, Part I
Which of the following pairs (metal figure — site) is correctly matched?
(1) Bronze bull — Lothal
(2) Copper dog and bird — Kalibangan
(3) Copper dog and bird — Lothal
(4) Charging bronze bull with its head turned to the right — Harappa
= 3
~ The copper dog and bird come from Lothal, the bronze bull from Kalibangan, and the massive bronze bull in charging fury with its head turned to the right from Mohenjodaro.

Q 9 | art | An Introduction to Indian Art, Part I
Which feature is described as a distinct decorative feature of the Indus Valley mother goddess figures?
(1) A crown of peacock feathers
(2) A lotus held in both hands
(3) A halo behind the head
(4) A fan-shaped head-dress with a cup-like projection on each side
= 4
~ The mother goddess figures are crude standing females with necklaces, a loin cloth and a girdle; the fan-shaped head-dress with a cup-like projection on each side is their distinctive feature. Pellet eyes, a beaked nose and a slit mouth complete the face.

Q 10 | art | An Introduction to Indian Art, Part I
Terracotta figurines of bearded males with coiled hair appear again and again in exactly the same rigid, upright posture. What does this repetition suggest?
(1) They were children's toys
(2) They were portraits of rulers
(3) They represented a deity
(4) They were figures of temple dancers
= 3
~ The repetition of the same rigid figure, legs slightly apart and arms parallel to the body, suggests it was a deity. A terracotta mask of a horned deity has also been found.

Q 11 | art | An Introduction to Indian Art, Part I
Most of the thousands of seals found at Indus Valley sites are made of:
(1) Steatite
(2) Ivory
(3) Copper
(4) Faience
= 1
~ Most seals are of steatite, and occasionally of agate, chert, copper, faience, terracotta or ivory.

Q 12 | art | An Introduction to Indian Art, Part I
The standard Harappan seal is described as:
(1) A round terracotta disc about one inch across
(2) A square plaque of 2 × 2 square inches, usually of steatite
(3) A rectangular copper sheet with no inscription
(4) A triangular ivory plate
= 2
~ The standard Harappan seal is a square steatite plaque of 2 × 2 square inches, with a pictographic script that is yet to be deciphered. The figures on it are carved in intaglio.

Q 14 | art | An Introduction to Indian Art, Part I
The figures on the Harappan seals were cut in which manner?
(1) Intaglio (cut into the surface)
(2) High relief
(3) Repoussé
(4) Inlay with gold
= 1
~ The seals carry animals and inscriptions carved in intaglio, so that they appear raised when pressed into clay.

Q 15 | art | An Introduction to Indian Art, Part I
Square or rectangular copper tablets with an animal or human figure on one side and an inscription on the other were most likely used as:
(1) Coins
(2) Weights
(3) Amulets
(4) Toys
= 3
~ The copper tablets, carved with a burin, seem to have been amulets; their inscriptions appear to be linked to the animals shown on them.

Q 16 | art | An Introduction to Indian Art, Part I
Consider the following statements about Harappan pottery:

Q 17 | art | An Introduction to Indian Art, Part I

Q 18 | art | An Introduction to Indian Art, Part I
Bead factories of the Indus Valley Civilisation have been found at:
(1) Harappa and Ropar
(2) Kalibangan and Rakhigarhi
(3) Dholavira and Banawali
(4) Chanhudaro and Lothal
= 4
~ Chanhudaro and Lothal had bead factories. Beads were made of carnelian, amethyst, jasper, crystal, quartz, steatite, turquoise, lapis lazuli, metals, shell, faience and terracotta.

Q 20 | art | An Introduction to Indian Art, Part I
Which of the following statements about the people of the Indus Valley is correct according to the chapter?
(1) Only women wore necklaces and finger-rings
(2) Cinnabar was used as a cosmetic, and lipstick and collyrium were known
(3) Beards were not popular among men
(4) Spindle whorls show that only silk was spun
= 2
~ Cinnabar was used as a cosmetic and face paint, and lipstick and collyrium were known. Necklaces, fillets, armlets and finger-rings were worn by both sexes, beards were popular, and spindle whorls show that cotton and wool were spun.
