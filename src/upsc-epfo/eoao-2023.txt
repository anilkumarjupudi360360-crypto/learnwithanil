Q 1 | eng | Sentence Arrangement
The first and sixth sentences of a passage are given as S1 and S6. The four sentences in between have been jumbled up and labelled P, Q, R and S. Choose the proper sequence of the four sentences.
S1: Geological mapping, when properly done, demands skill and judgment.
S6: Nothing is more naive than to believe that a field geologist should gather only 'facts', the interpretation of which is to be made at a later date.
P: While the field work progresses he should conceive as many interpretations as are consistent with the known facts.
Q: As the field work progresses and the larger geological picture begins to unfold, experience and judgment are essential if the geologist is to evaluate properly the vast number of facts gathered from thousands of outcrops.
R: Such mapping requires keen observation and a knowledge of what data are significant.
S: Above all, the field geologist must use the method of "working multiple hypotheses" to deduce the geological structure.
(1) SRQP
(2) RQSP
(3) PSQR
(4) SRPQ
= 2
~ The order is RQSP. R must come first because "Such mapping" refers back to the geological mapping of S1 and spells out the skill it demands: keen observation and knowing which data matter. Q moves on to the progress of the field work, when experience and judgment are needed to evaluate the facts. S ("Above all") gives the most important requirement, the method of multiple hypotheses, and P explains it: "he", the field geologist of S, should form many interpretations while the work goes on. That leads into S6, which rejects gathering facts now and interpreting them later. Only RQSP begins with R.

Q 2 | eng | Sentence Arrangement
The first and sixth sentences of a passage are given as S1 and S6. The four sentences in between have been jumbled up and labelled P, Q, R and S. Choose the proper sequence of the four sentences.
S1: Under the colonial regime, basic infrastructure such as railways, ports, water transport, posts and telegraphs did develop.
S6: Naturally, therefore, these areas remained inaccessible to the people.
P: There always remained an acute shortage of all-weather roads to reach out to the rural areas during the rainy season.
Q: Roads constructed in India prior to the advent of British rule were not fit for modern transport.
R: However, the real motive behind this development was not necessarily to provide basic amenities to the people but to subserve various colonial interests.
S: The roads that were built primarily served the purposes of mobilizing the army within India and drawing out raw materials from the countryside to the nearest railway station.
(1) SPQR
(2) PQSR
(3) QPRS
(4) RQSP
= 4
~ The order is RQSP. R comes first: "However" contrasts with S1's statement that infrastructure "did develop", and "this development" refers back to it before revealing the real colonial motive. Q and S narrow this down to roads: roads built before British rule were unfit for modern transport, and the roads the British built mainly served army movement and the carrying of raw materials to railway stations. P gives the result, a shortage of all-weather roads to rural areas, and S6's "these areas" means those rural areas, so P must come last. Only RQSP starts with R and ends with P.

Q 3 | eng | Sentence Arrangement
The first and sixth sentences of a passage are given as S1 and S6. The four sentences in between have been jumbled up and labelled P, Q, R and S. Choose the proper sequence of the four sentences.
S1: Contemporary history is necessary, but it is at best a tentative record.
S6: Some portentous events can be seen to be so from the moment of their occurrence, like the discovery of the New World; others of comparable momentousness are hidden in obscurity.
P: The relevance of the issue of proportion is significant.
Q: It possesses, indeed, a massive supply of public record, but it necessarily lacks the inner knowledge of politicians' minds, of planners' proposals, of scientists' discoveries, of technicians' break-throughs.
R: It also lacks perspective since it is bereft of a sense of proportion.
S: It is impossible, over just a few years of observation, to estimate accurately the significance of various events.
(1) QPSR
(2) SRPQ
(3) QSRP
(4) PRSQ
= 3
~ The order is QSRP. Q follows S1 directly: "It" is contemporary history, and its "massive supply of public record" picks up S1's "tentative record" before saying what it lacks. S gives a second limitation: a few years of observation cannot show the real significance of events. R adds, with "also lacks", the lack of perspective and of a sense of proportion, and P can only come after R, because "the issue of proportion" needs R to introduce it. S6 then illustrates proportion with portentous events, some obvious at once and others hidden. QPSR wrongly puts P before proportion has been mentioned.

Q 4 | eng | Sentence Arrangement
The first and sixth sentences of a passage are given as S1 and S6. The four sentences in between have been jumbled up and labelled P, Q, R and S. Choose the proper sequence of the four sentences.
S1: Income inequality in less-developed countries is worse than most estimates.
S6: The tax regime in these countries needs to be progressive and futuristic.
P: There is a staggering level of difference between the income metrics and the actual income of the prosperous in such countries.
Q: The effective tax rates are not really progressive with regard to income.
R: They need to be reengineered to bring into the tax net the enormous sums of 'missing' income.
S: They are even less so with regard to wealth.
(1) PQSR
(2) QSPR
(3) QSRP
(4) SRPQ
= 2
~ The keyed order is QSPR. Q and S are a fixed pair: "even less so" in S needs "not really progressive" in Q (on income, then wealth). P then points to the gap between measured income and the actual income of the prosperous, and R gives the remedy: reengineer the tax rates to bring this "missing" income into the tax net, as S6 sums up. SRPQ fails because S cannot open, and QSRP mentions the "missing" income before P explains it. PQSR, with P explaining S1's "worse than most estimates" and R's "They" directly after S's "They", reads at least as naturally. (Official key; some sources differ.)

Q 5 | eng | Sentence Arrangement
The first and sixth sentences of a passage are given as S1 and S6. The four sentences in between have been jumbled up and labelled P, Q, R and S. Choose the proper sequence of the four sentences.
S1: The history of English literature is usually divided into different periods.
S6: This is the real danger of periodization, which tends to give the impression of distinct categories where there are none.
P: However there are important discontinuities to be kept in mind.
Q: Each period has unique features that produce certain family resemblances between the writers of a particular period.
R: Scholars often specialize in periods such as Medieval, Renaissance, Romantic, Victorian, and Modern literature.
S: Failure to detect these discontinuities often produce a false sense of uniformity and consensus within a period.
(1) PQRS
(2) RQPS
(3) PSQR
(4) RSPQ
= 2
~ The order is RQPS. R follows S1 by naming the usual periods (Medieval, Renaissance, Romantic, Victorian, Modern), and Q explains what holds a period together: features that produce family resemblances among its writers. P, opening with "However", turns to the discontinuities this view can hide, and S must follow P because "these discontinuities" refers back to them. S6's "This is the real danger of periodization" sums up the false sense of uniformity in S, so S comes last. PSQR keeps P and S together but separates S from S6. (In S, printed "produce" should strictly be "produces", agreeing with "Failure".)

Q 6 | eng | Grammar & Usage
Find the part of the sentence that contains an error. If there is no error, choose "No error".
Ramesh liked his friend's music player to his own.
(1) Ramesh liked
(2) his friend's music player
(3) to his own.
(4) No error
= 1
~ The error is in part (1). The verb "like" cannot take a "to" phrase of comparison; the verb that does is "prefer", since one prefers one thing to another. The correct sentence is "Ramesh preferred his friend's music player to his own." Parts (2) and (3) are correct once the verb is changed: "his friend's music player" is the object, and "to his own" (to his own music player) completes the comparison. "Liked ... more than his own" would also be acceptable, but with "to" the sentence needs "preferred".

Q 7 | eng | Grammar & Usage
Find the part of the sentence that contains an error. If there is no error, choose "No error".
Given the complex of the problem the committee is not expected to arrive at a decision any time soon.
(1) Given the complex of the problem
(2) the committee is not expected
(3) to arrive at a decision any time soon.
(4) No error
= 1
~ The error is in part (1). As a noun, "complex" means a group of connected buildings or parts (a sports complex) or a psychological fixation (an inferiority complex); it does not mean the quality of being complicated. The noun needed is "complexity": "Given the complexity of the problem, the committee is not expected to arrive at a decision any time soon." Part (2) is a correct passive ("is not expected"), and part (3) uses the right collocation "arrive at a decision" and the idiom "any time soon", meaning in the near future.

Q 8 | eng | Grammar & Usage
Find the part of the sentence that contains an error. If there is no error, choose "No error".
The group of students on a visit to Srinagar circumnavigated around the Dal Lake.
(1) The group of students
(2) on a visit to Srinagar
(3) circumnavigated around the Dal Lake.
(4) No error
= 3
~ The error is in part (3). To circumnavigate means to sail, fly or travel all the way around something (Latin circum, around, and navigare, to sail), so "circumnavigated around" is a redundancy, like "return back" or "revert back". The correct sentence is "The group of students on a visit to Srinagar circumnavigated the Dal Lake" (or "went around the Dal Lake"). Parts (1) and (2) are correct: "The group of students" is the subject, and "on a visit to Srinagar" describes it.

Q 9 | eng | Grammar & Usage
Find the part of the sentence that contains an error. If there is no error, choose "No error".
Jitender was the smartest of the two children in the room.
(1) Jitender was the smartest
(2) of the two
(3) children in the room.
(4) No error
= 1
~ The error is in part (1). When two persons or things are compared, the comparative degree is used, not the superlative: "Jitender was the smarter of the two children in the room." The superlative "smartest" is used for three or more, as in "the smartest of all the children". Note that "the" stays before the comparative in the pattern "the + comparative + of the two" (the taller of the two, the better of the two). Parts (2) and (3) are correct as they stand.

Q 10 | eng | Grammar & Usage
Find the part of the sentence that contains an error. If there is no error, choose "No error".
He asked me if I would be interested in signing up as a member of the group.
(1) He asked me if I would be
(2) interested in signing up
(3) as a member of the group.
(4) No error
= 4
~ The sentence is correct, so the answer is "No error". It reports a yes/no question ("Would you be interested ...?"); such questions are reported with "if" or "whether" followed by normal statement word order, and "would" is correct after the past reporting verb "asked". "Interested in" takes the right preposition and is followed by the gerund "signing up", and "sign up as a member of" is idiomatic for enrolling in or joining a group. No part needs correction.

Q 11 | eng | Grammar & Usage
Select the most appropriate option for the blank space.
I ______ walking past this particular shop for the past three years.
(1) was
(2) have
(3) have been
(4) had been
= 3
~ The phrase "for the past three years" describes an action that began in the past and is still going on, which calls for the present perfect continuous: "I have been walking past this particular shop for the past three years." "Was walking" is the past continuous and cannot be combined with a period that runs up to the present, "have walking" is not a verb form at all, and "had been walking" (past perfect continuous) needs an earlier past point of reference, for example "... when it closed down".

Q 12 | eng | Grammar & Usage
Select the most appropriate option for the blank space.
All effects can be connected with immediate causes, but it ______ whether they provide the whole explanation.
(1) is doubted
(2) is a doubt
(3) may be a doubt
(4) may be doubted
= 4
~ The impersonal passive "it may be doubted whether ..." means "one may doubt whether" or "it is open to question whether": "All effects can be connected with immediate causes, but it may be doubted whether they provide the whole explanation." The modal "may" suits the tentative tone set by "can". "It is doubted whether" is grammatical but states that people actually doubt it, which is less apt. "Is a doubt" and "may be a doubt" are unidiomatic, since the noun would need "there is a doubt" or "it is doubtful".

Q 13 | eng | Vocabulary & Idioms
Select the most appropriate option for the blank space.
A bird ______ is better than two in the bush.
(1) in hand
(2) to hand
(3) handy
(4) in one's hands
= 1
~ The proverb is "A bird in hand is better than two in the bush", more often worded "A bird in the hand is worth two in the bush". It means that a small advantage you already have is better than a bigger one that is only possible, so one should not give up what is certain for what is uncertain. "In hand" means held, in one's possession. "To hand" means within reach, "handy" means convenient or useful, and "in one's hands" (plural) breaks the fixed wording of the proverb.

Q 14 | eng | Vocabulary & Idioms
Select the most appropriate option for the blank space.
Human history is ample proof that human ambition ______ no limits.
(1) knows
(2) keeps
(3) crosses
(4) marks
= 1
~ The idiom "to know no limits" (also "know no bounds") means to be boundless, to have no limit at all: "Human history is ample proof that human ambition knows no limits." "Know" is used figuratively here, as in "her generosity knew no bounds". "Keeps no limits" and "marks no limits" are not idiomatic, and "crosses no limits" would say the opposite of what is meant, since ambition that crosses no limits stays within them. The sentence means that history shows human ambition has always reached for more.

Q 15 | eng | Vocabulary & Idioms
Select the most appropriate option for the blank space.
Good history presents several perspectives on a period but these divergent views must ______ into an integrated whole.
(1) be made
(2) be fused
(3) fit
(4) be balanced
= 2
~ The sentence contrasts "several perspectives" and "divergent views" with "an integrated whole", so the verb must mean blending separate things into one: "these divergent views must be fused into an integrated whole." To fuse is to join or blend two or more things so that they become a single unit. "Be made into" is grammatical but vague, "fit into" suggests slotting into a whole that already exists rather than forming one, and "be balanced" does not go with "into". So "be fused" is the most precise collocation.

Q 16 | eng | Reading Comprehension
Read the passage given below and answer the question that follows.
The world's life-supporting resources are being depleted at a faster rate than ever before! We have to change our practices and those of the world as a whole towards a more sustainable future. To give us an idea about the dimensions of change, the General Assembly of the United Nations has announced its 'Agenda 2030'. The 17 Sustainable Development Goals describe the framework of our common global efforts. Within each goal, education holds a key position.
Of course education and learning do not function in isolation. In a traditional sense they help to understand the world one lives in and prepare all for a job market with both intrinsic and instrumental values. What are the many roles of education in sustainable development? Its purpose is transformative in nature, in the sense that it should widen one's worldview, make one question 'unsustainability', and help one build competencies to address global challenges and be accommodated into a global system. Beyond this, education for sustainable development means integrating the 'future' as a specific dimension of our learning activities and teaching. How to build a sustainable future must play a central role in educational processes. Globalization, which is sometimes merely seen as global capitalism, has an inevitability about it. Whether we like it or not, we live in a highly interconnected and interdependent world. It is now well recognized that 21st century challenges are global in nature and require cooperation beyond national boundaries. In a sustainable development sense we have to see ourselves as 'citizens' of the world and respond accordingly to the global challenges in the present century. In fact, global challenges have expanded the very notion of citizenship itself. There is an increasing recognition of the importance of global citizenship.
Which one of the following areas holds a common key position in United Nations 'Agenda 2030'?
(1) Environment
(2) Gender
(3) Globalization
(4) Education
= 4
~ The first paragraph says that the 17 Sustainable Development Goals describe the framework of our common global efforts and that "within each goal, education holds a key position". So education is the area common to all the goals of Agenda 2030, which the UN General Assembly adopted in September 2015. Environment and gender are the subjects of particular goals, and globalization comes up later only as a feature of the interconnected world, but none of them is said to hold a key position in every goal.

Q 21 | ih | Modern India & Freedom Struggle
Which one of the following was not provided in the Regulating Act of 1773?
(1) It made the Presidency of Bengal supreme over those of Bombay and Madras in matters relating to war and peace.
(2) The tenure of the Governor-General and his Councillors was fixed as five years.
(3) The Supreme Court of Justice was established at Calcutta.
(4) The Governor-General-in-Council was authorized to appoint successors to Bombay and Madras Presidencies.
= 4
~ The Regulating Act, 1773 made the Governor of Bengal the Governor-General (Warren Hastings) with a council of four, all named in the Act for a term of five years, and placed the Bombay and Madras Presidencies under Bengal in matters of war and peace, so (1) and (2) were provided. It also provided for a Supreme Court of Judicature at Fort William, Calcutta, set up in 1774 (3). It gave the Governor-General-in-Council only superintendence and control over the other two presidencies, not the power to appoint their governors or successors, so (4) was not provided.

Q 22 | ih | Modern India & Freedom Struggle
Which one of the following statements about the Government of India Act of 1935 is not correct?
(1) It was an outcome of the Round Table Conferences.
(2) It provided for the establishment of an All India Federation.
(3) It retained the authority of the Secretary of State over the Government of India.
(4) It introduced the provision of Provincial Autonomy.
= 3
~ The Government of India Act, 1935 grew out of the three Round Table Conferences, the 1933 White Paper and the Joint Parliamentary Committee's report (1); it provided for an All-India Federation of provinces and princely states, which never materialised (2); and it replaced provincial dyarchy with provincial autonomy (4). The key treats (3) as incorrect because the Act loosened London's grip: it dissolved the Council of India, leaving the Secretary of State three to six advisers, and his control receded where responsible provincial ministers acted. Yet Sections 14 and 314 kept the Governor-General, and until federation the Governor-General in Council, under his general control. (Official key; some sources differ.)

Q 43 | acc | Accounting Basics

(2) Errors of commission
(3) Errors of principle
(4) Errors of omission
= 3
~ An error of principle arises when a transaction is recorded against accounting principles, typically by treating a capital item as revenue or vice versa. Q 49 | sci | Computer Applications

(2) A + b = b + A
(3) A − b = b − A
(4) A * B = B * A
= 3

= 1

Q 73 | qa | Algebra & Numbers

(1) 2
(2) 4
(3) 6
(4) 8
= 3
~ The unit digits of the powers of 2 repeat in a cycle of four: 2, 4, 8, 6 (for 2¹, 2², 2³, 2⁴), then 2 again for 2⁵. Q 87 | sci | Physics
If the length of a current carrying wire is halved, for a given potential difference, the current in the wire would:
(1) be doubled
(2) be halved
(3) remain unchanged
(4) become zero
= 1
~ The resistance of a wire is R = ρL/A, so for the same material and cross-section it is directly proportional to the length. Halving the length halves the resistance. Q 101 | qa | Arithmetic
A train of length 400 m takes 15 seconds to cross a train of length 300 m travelling at 60 km per hour from the opposite direction along a parallel track. What is the speed of the longer train, in km per hour?
(1) 108
(2) 102
(3) 98
(4) 96
= 1
~ Trains moving in opposite directions have a relative speed equal to the sum of their speeds. To cross each other completely they must cover the sum of their lengths, 400 + 300 = 700 m, in 15 s. Relative speed = 700/15 m/s = 700/15 × 18/5 = 168 km/h. Speed of the longer train = 168 − 60 = 108 km/h. Using only the 400 m length gives 400/15 × 18/5 = 96 km/h, the trap in option (4), which also forgets to subtract the other train's speed.

(3) 14
(4) 15
= 3

Q 105 | qa | Reasoning
