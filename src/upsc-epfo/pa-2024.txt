# UPSC EPFO Recruitment Test for Personal Assistant (PA), Advt. 51/2024 (Special), held 7 July 2024. Booklet Series A (RTJL-A-GAT), General Ability Test, 120 questions, 300 marks.
# Source: the official UPSC question paper; answers from UPSC's official answer key, Series A (upsc.gov.in, published 29 September 2026). No question dropped. Explanations by Learn with Anil.

Q 1 | eng | Reading Comprehension
Read the passage given below and answer the question that follows.
The responsibility that devolves on the press is very great. The freedom of the press enjoys a sacred privilege, but it must not be exercised to serve communal and sectional interests. Passions and prejudices must not be allowed to dominate this privilege. It should not be abused to foster a partisan spirit and encourage fissiparous tendencies. It should mirror public opinion, but it also should guard public welfare. It must remember to exercise restraint and wisdom while throwing light on contemporary events.
Which one of the following statements best reflects the critical message conveyed by the author of the passage?
(1) The press must be responsible as it shapes public opinion.
(2) The press is the guardian of freedom of expression.
(3) The press is an independent institution and is free to influence people's way of thinking.
(4) The only task of press is to report events happening around.
= 1
~ Option 1 is correct. The passage opens with its key sentence, "The responsibility that devolves on the press is very great", and every later line spells out that responsibility: press freedom is a "sacred privilege" but must not serve communal or sectional interests, must not be ruled by passions and prejudices, must not foster a partisan spirit or "fissiparous" (divisive, tending to split) tendencies, and must guard public welfare while it mirrors public opinion. Because the press shapes how people think, it must use its freedom with restraint and wisdom, which is exactly option 1. Option 2 is wrong because the passage does not call the press a guardian of free expression; it talks about the limits on the press's own freedom. Option 3 stresses independence and the freedom to influence people, ignoring the author's warning against abuse of that freedom. Option 4 contradicts the passage, which expects more than reporting, namely guarding public welfare. Tip: in "critical message" questions, find the topic sentence and choose the option that sums up the whole passage, not a single detail.

Q 2 | eng | Grammar & Usage
Consider the following statements:
I. He regretted for his mistake.
II. We ought to make exercise daily.
III. This has been going on for a long time.
Which of the statements given above is/are grammatically correct?
(1) I and II
(2) II only
(3) I and III
(4) III only
= 4
~ Option 4, III only, is correct. Sentence III, "This has been going on for a long time", uses the present perfect continuous (has/have been + verb-ing) for an action that began in the past and is still continuing, with "for" before a period of time ("since" comes before a point of time, as in "since 2020"). Sentence I is wrong because the verb "regret" is transitive and takes its object directly: "He regretted his mistake." The noun can take "for" ("He expressed regret for his mistake"), and the verb "repent" takes "of" ("He repented of his mistake"), which is why the error is easy to miss. Sentence II is wrong because English says "take exercise" or "do exercise", not "make exercise": "We ought to take exercise daily." So options 1, 2 and 3, which include I or II, are wrong. Remember: verbs such as regret, discuss, describe, reach and resemble take a direct object without a preposition ("discuss the matter", not "discuss about the matter").

Q 3 | eng | Grammar & Usage
Consider the following statements:
I. He is now ashamed from his conduct.
II. He is now ashamed of his conduct.
III. I congratulate you for your success.
IV. I congratulate you on your success.
Which two of the statements given above are correct?
(1) I and III
(2) II and IV
(3) I and IV
(4) II and III
= 2
~ Option 2, II and IV, is correct. The question tests fixed prepositions that go with particular words. "Ashamed" is followed by "of": "He is now ashamed of his conduct", so II is correct and I ("ashamed from") is wrong. "Congratulate" takes "on" before the success, achievement or occasion: "I congratulate you on your success", so IV is correct. "Congratulate for" (III) is heard in casual speech, but standard grammar books such as Wren and Martin give "congratulate on", so III is treated as wrong. That rules out option 1 (I and III), option 3 (I and IV) and option 4 (II and III). Similar word-preposition pairs that examiners like to test: "proud of", "afraid of", "fond of", "jealous of", "compliment someone on", "accuse someone of" but "charge someone with", and "prefer this to that" (not "than"). Remember: one is ashamed OF something and congratulated ON it. Reading the four sentences in pairs (I against II, III against IV) is the quickest way to solve such questions.

Q 4 | eng | Grammar & Usage
Consider the following statements:
I. No less than seventy people were hurt in the accident.
II. One must finish his duty on time.
III. He took off his sweater.
Which of the statements given above is/are grammatically correct?
(1) I and II
(2) II only
(3) I and III
(4) III only
= 4
~ Option 4, III only, is correct. Sentence III uses the phrasal verb "take off" (remove clothing) correctly: "He took off his sweater" or "He took his sweater off". Sentence I breaks the rule that "fewer" goes with countable nouns (number) and "less" with uncountable nouns (quantity). People can be counted, so it should be "No fewer than seventy people were hurt in the accident", just as we say "less sugar" but "fewer apples". Sentence II breaks the rule that the indefinite pronoun "one" is followed by "one's" or "oneself", not "his" or "himself": "One must finish one's duty on time." Both rules are taught in standard Indian grammar texts such as Wren and Martin, although informal English is looser about them. Since I and II are wrong, options 1, 2 and 3 fall away. Remember: fewer for number, less for amount; and once a sentence begins with "one", it keeps "one's" all through.

Q 5 | eng | Vocabulary & Idioms
Consider the following:
The teacher has become so stern that his popularity is on the wane.
It means:
(1) The teacher's increasing popularity has made him more strict.
(2) The teacher's increasing strictness has made him more popular.
(3) The teacher's decreasing popularity has made him more strict.
(4) The teacher's increasing strictness has made him less popular.
= 4
~ Option 4 is correct. The idiom "on the wane" means decreasing or declining, like the moon in its waning phase; its opposite is "on the rise" or "waxing". In a "so ... that" sentence the "so" part gives the cause and the "that" clause gives the result: the teacher has become so stern (cause) that his popularity is declining (result). Put simply, his increasing strictness has made him less popular. Options 1 and 3 reverse cause and effect, making popularity the reason for his strictness, and option 1 also wrongly says his popularity is increasing. Option 2 keeps the right cause but turns the result around, saying he has become more popular. Related words: the verb "wane" means to grow weaker, as in "His influence waned after the election"; "dwindle", "ebb" and "decline" are near synonyms. Tip: in paraphrase questions, first identify the cause and the effect, then check the direction (more or less) of each before choosing an option.

Q 6 | eng | Vocabulary & Idioms
Consider the following:
He bears his troubles bravely, never begs for sympathy, never yields to sorrow and never shows pain.
What is the most appropriate word that describes him?
(1) Vivacious
(2) Stoical
(3) Magnanimous
(4) Effusive
= 2
~ Option 2, stoical, is correct. A stoical person endures pain and hardship patiently, without complaining and without showing his feelings, which matches the description exactly: he bears his troubles bravely, never begs for sympathy, never yields to sorrow and never shows pain. The word comes from the Stoics, philosophers of ancient Greece (the school was founded by Zeno of Citium) who taught calm acceptance of what cannot be changed. Option 1, "vivacious", means lively and full of spirit. Option 3, "magnanimous", means generous and forgiving, especially towards a rival or a defeated enemy. Option 4, "effusive", means expressing feelings in an unrestrained, gushing way, almost the opposite of stoical. Related words: "stoicism" (noun); "imperturbable" (not easily upset) and "phlegmatic" (calm and unemotional) are near synonyms, while "emotional" and "demonstrative" are antonyms. Remember: the stoic hides his pain; the effusive person pours out his feelings.

Q 7 | eng | Vocabulary & Idioms
Consider the following:
"This boy takes after his father."
What does it mean?
(1) This boy follows his father's advice.
(2) This boy steals from his father's wallet.
(3) This boy manages his father's affairs.
(4) This boy resembles his father.
= 4
~ Option 4 is correct. The phrasal verb "take after" means to resemble an older member of one's family, usually a parent, in looks, character or behaviour, as in "She takes after her mother; both are musical." So "This boy takes after his father" means the boy resembles his father. Option 1 is wrong because following advice is "listen to" or "follow" someone. Option 2 confuses the idiom with "take" in the sense of stealing. Option 3 describes "look after" or "manage", not "take after". Examiners often set phrasal verbs with "take", so learn them as a set: "take after" (resemble), "take to" (begin to like), "take up" (start a hobby or job), "take over" (assume control), "take in" (deceive, or give shelter) and "take off" (remove clothing, or of an aircraft, leave the ground). Trap: do not confuse "take after" with "look after" (care for) or "go after" (chase).

Q 8 | eng | Grammar & Usage
Consider the following statements:
I. We happened to meet at the house of common friend.
II. The book you mention is out of print.
III. The Himalayas lie to north of India.
In which of the above statements is an article missing?
(1) I and II
(2) II only
(3) I and III
(4) III only
= 3
~ Option 3, I and III, is correct. In I, "friend" is a singular countable noun, and such a noun cannot stand alone; it needs an article or another determiner: "We happened to meet at the house of a common friend." In III, the points of the compass take "the" when they name a direction or region after a preposition: "The Himalayas lie to the north of India" (compare "The sun rises in the east"). Sentence II needs no change: "The book you mention is out of print" uses "the" correctly because the book is specified by "you mention", and "out of print" (no longer being published) is a fixed phrase that takes no article. So options 1 and 2, which include II, and option 4, which leaves out I, are wrong. Note also that the names of mountain ranges always take "the" (the Himalayas, the Western Ghats), while single peaks do not (Mount Everest, Kanchenjunga).

Q 9 | eng | Grammar & Usage
Consider the following pairs of statements:
Affirmative form : Negative form
I. He is sometimes foolish. : He is not always wise.
II. Every man makes mistakes sometimes. : Every man does not make mistakes.
III. He is greater than I. : I am not as great as he.
Which of the pairs given above is/are correctly matched?
(1) I, II and III
(2) II only
(3) I and III only
(4) III only
= 3
~ Option 3, I and III only, is correct. An affirmative sentence may be changed into a negative one only if the meaning stays exactly the same; usually a word is replaced by "not" plus its opposite. In pair I, "sometimes foolish" becomes "not always wise", which keeps the meaning. In pair III, a comparative is turned around: "He is greater than I" equals "I am not as great as he" (the "not as/so ... as" pattern). Pair II is wrong: "Every man does not make mistakes" means that not every man makes mistakes, which contradicts "Every man makes mistakes sometimes". A correct negative would be "There is no man who does not make mistakes sometimes" or "No man is free from mistakes". So options 1 and 2, which include II, and option 4, which leaves out I, are wrong. Similar transformations: "He is always punctual" becomes "He is never late"; "Only a fool would say so" becomes "None but a fool would say so".

Q 10 | eng | Grammar & Usage
Consider the following pairs of statements:
Statement : Meaning
I. My heart is too full for words. : My heart is so full of words that I am compelled to talk.
II. If you eat too much you will be ill. : Do not eat too much lest you will be ill.
III. But for you I should have caught the train. : I should have caught the train to be with you.
Which of the pairs given above is/are correctly matched?
(1) I and II
(2) II only
(3) I and III
(4) III only
= 2
~ Option 2, II only, is correct. In pair II both versions give the same warning: eating too much will make you ill. "Lest" means "for fear that" or "so that ... not"; strictly it takes "should" ("Do not eat too much lest you should be ill") and is never followed by "not", but the meaning of the pair matches. Pair I is wrong: "My heart is too full for words" uses the "too ... for" pattern, which means "so full that I cannot speak"; the heart is full of emotion, not of words, so it is the opposite of being compelled to talk. Pair III is wrong: "But for you I should have caught the train" means "If it had not been for you, I would have caught the train", that is, because of you I missed it; it says nothing about catching the train to be with you. So options 1, 3 and 4 fall away. Remember: "but for" means "if it were not for" or "if it had not been for", and "too ... to/for" carries a negative sense.

Q 11 | eng | Vocabulary & Idioms
When somebody says "the boss was livid when yet another deadline was missed", which one of the following words appropriately describes the boss's state of mind?
(1) Contrite
(2) Furious
(3) Placid
(4) Amenable
= 2
~ Option 2, furious, is correct. In everyday use "livid" means extremely angry. Its older meaning is the dark bluish-grey colour of a bruise (a "livid bruise"), later extended to a face pale or discoloured with rage, so "the boss was livid when yet another deadline was missed" means he was furious. The phrase "yet another" also shows that the boss's patience had run out. Option 1, "contrite", means feeling sorry and guilty for a wrong one has done; it would describe the employee who missed the deadline, not the boss. Option 3, "placid", means calm and not easily upset, the opposite of livid. Option 4, "amenable", means willing to agree, obey or be persuaded. Near synonyms of livid: "irate", "incensed", "enraged" and "fuming"; antonyms include "calm", "composed" and "serene". Trap: do not choose "contrite" just because the sentence is about a mistake; the question asks about the boss's state of mind.

Q 12 | eng | Vocabulary & Idioms
If your friend reveals his personal life to you because he trusts you to be discreet, which one of the following appropriately describes you?
(1) Conciliatory
(2) Dauntless
(3) Gregarious
(4) Prudent
= 4
~ Option 4, prudent, is correct. To be discreet is to be careful in what one says and does, especially in keeping confidences and in not embarrassing others. A friend who shares his personal life with you because he trusts your discretion sees you as prudent: careful, sensible and showing good judgement. Of the four options, "prudent" is the nearest to "discreet". Option 1, "conciliatory", means intended to make peace or win someone over, as in "a conciliatory gesture". Option 2, "dauntless", means fearless and determined. Option 3, "gregarious", means fond of company and sociable; a sociable person is not necessarily one who keeps secrets. Related words: "discretion" (noun) and "indiscreet" (careless about revealing secrets); "circumspect" and "judicious" are near synonyms of prudent, while "rash" and "imprudent" are antonyms. Trap: do not confuse "discreet" (careful, tactful) with "discrete" (separate, distinct).

Q 13 | eng | Vocabulary & Idioms
The phrases 'look down upon' and 'intense dislike' denote which one of the following?
(1) Conceit
(2) Disdain
(3) Enigmatic
(4) Indignant
= 2
~ Option 2, disdain, is correct. "Disdain" is the feeling that someone or something is unworthy of one's respect: a mixture of contempt (looking down upon) and strong dislike, as in "She regarded his lies with disdain." It also works as a verb: "He disdained to reply." So it combines both phrases in the question. Option 1, "conceit", is an excessively high opinion of oneself, not a feeling of contempt for others. Option 3, "enigmatic", is an adjective meaning mysterious or puzzling, as in "an enigmatic smile". Option 4, "indignant", is an adjective meaning angry at something unjust or unfair; it involves anger but not looking down upon someone. Near synonyms of disdain: "contempt", "scorn" and "derision"; antonyms: "respect", "admiration" and "esteem". Tip: when a question combines two meanings, choose the word that covers both; "indignant" covers only the anger and "conceit" only the sense of one's own superiority.

Q 14 | eng | Vocabulary & Idioms
Consider the following pairs:
Phrase : Meaning
I. Likely to happen without delay : Imminent
II. Inclined to silence : Parsimonious
III. Unwilling to spend : Taciturn
Which of the above is/are correctly matched?
(1) I only
(2) II and III
(3) I and III
(4) II only
= 1
~ Option 1, I only, is correct. Pair I matches: "imminent" means likely to happen very soon, about to occur, as in "an imminent storm" or "imminent danger". Pairs II and III have their words swapped. "Inclined to silence", reserved and saying little, is "taciturn", as in "a taciturn old farmer". "Unwilling to spend", extremely frugal or stingy with money, is "parsimonious", as in "a parsimonious employer". So II and III are both mismatched, and options 2, 3 and 4 fall away. Two common traps: "imminent" (about to happen) is often confused with "eminent" (famous, distinguished) and "immanent" (present within); and "parsimonious" (stingy) is a stronger, more negative word than "frugal" or "thrifty" (careful with money). Synonyms worth learning: for taciturn, "reticent" and "uncommunicative"; for parsimonious, "miserly", "niggardly" and "tight-fisted".

Q 15 | eng | Vocabulary & Idioms
Consider the following words:
I. Advance
II. Escalate
III. Lighten
IV. Retain
Which two of the above words constitute one synonym and one antonym for the word "Alleviate"?
(1) I and II
(2) II and III
(3) I and IV
(4) III and IV
= 2
~ Option 2, II and III, is correct. "Alleviate" means to make pain, suffering or a problem less severe, as in "medicine to alleviate the pain" or "schemes to alleviate poverty". "Lighten" (III), to make a load or burden less heavy, is a synonym: alleviating distress lightens it. "Escalate" (II), to increase rapidly in intensity, extent or seriousness, as in "the conflict escalated", is an antonym. "Advance" (I) means to move forward or put forward, and "retain" (IV) means to keep or hold on to; neither is a synonym or an antonym of "alleviate". So options 1, 3 and 4, which include advance or retain, are wrong. Other synonyms of alleviate: "ease", "relieve", "mitigate" and "assuage"; antonyms: "aggravate", "intensify" and "worsen". Tip: when the answer must contain one synonym and one antonym, find a word for each role and discard the neutral words.

Q 16 | eng | Vocabulary & Idioms
Consider the following words:
I. Calm
II. Excite
III. Polite
IV. Relieve
Which of the above words are synonyms for the word 'Assuage'?
(1) I and II
(2) II and III
(3) I and IV
(4) III and IV
= 3
~ Option 3, I and IV, is correct. "Assuage" means to make an unpleasant feeling less intense, to soothe or relieve it, and also to satisfy an appetite or desire, as in "to assuage grief", "to assuage his anger" or "to assuage one's hunger". Its synonyms among the options are "calm" (I), to soothe or quieten, and "relieve" (IV), to lessen pain or distress. "Excite" (II) means to stir up strong feelings and is close to an antonym of assuage. "Polite" (III) is an adjective meaning courteous; it has nothing to do with lessening pain or anger. So options 1, 2 and 4, which include excite or polite, are wrong. Other synonyms: "allay", "alleviate", "mitigate", "pacify", "appease" and "mollify"; antonyms: "aggravate", "intensify", "provoke" and "inflame". Tip: "assuage" and "allay" often appear together in synonym questions; remember the common collocations "allay fears" and "assuage guilt".

Q 17 | eng | Vocabulary & Idioms
Which one of the following words correctly means "above board"?
(1) Across
(2) Honest
(3) Invincible
(4) Imperious
= 2
~ Option 2, honest, is correct. "Above board" (also written "above-board") means honest, open and legitimate, with nothing hidden, as in "The deal was completely above board." The phrase is said to come from card games: a player who keeps his hands above the table (the board) cannot change cards secretly. Option 1, "across", is a preposition or adverb meaning from one side to the other. Option 3, "invincible", means too strong to be defeated or overcome. Option 4, "imperious", means arrogant and domineering, expecting to be obeyed. None of these means honest. Related expressions: "fair and square" (honest and according to the rules) and "open and above board"; the opposite ideas are "underhand" (secret and dishonest), "under the table" (secret and illegal, especially of payments) and "fishy" (suspicious). Trap: "imperious" (commanding) is easily confused with "impervious" (not affected by something, not letting it through).

Q 18 | eng | Vocabulary & Idioms
What does 'be in the same boat' mean?
(1) Be in the same difficult circumstances as others
(2) Be in competition with others to win a race or prize
(3) To work tirelessly for the welfare of one's fellow beings
(4) To be faithful and loyal to norms and traditions of one's community
= 1
~ Option 1 is correct. To "be in the same boat" means to be in the same unpleasant or difficult situation as other people, as in "Don't complain about the delay; we are all in the same boat." The image is of passengers in one small boat at sea who all share the same danger, so none of them is better off than the rest. Option 2 describes a competition, which would be "in the running" or "in the race". Option 3 describes selfless service to others, and option 4 describes loyalty to the customs of one's community; the idiom has nothing to do with either. Other boat idioms that examiners use: "rock the boat" (disturb a stable situation, cause trouble), "miss the boat" (lose an opportunity by being too slow) and "burn one's boats" (take a step from which there is no going back). Remember: "in the same boat" means a shared difficulty, not a shared goal or a shared race.

Q 19 | eng | Vocabulary & Idioms
What does the phrase 'the coast is clear' mean?
(1) Life is pleasant and comfortable
(2) To be free of responsibilities and liabilities
(3) There is no danger of being caught
(4) Free of threat by sea
= 3
~ Option 3 is correct. "The coast is clear" means there is no one around to see or stop you, so there is no danger of being seen or caught: "When the coast was clear, the boys climbed over the wall." The expression is commonly traced to smugglers, who landed their goods only when no coastguard was watching the shore. Option 4, "free of threat by sea", takes the words literally; this literal reading is the trap the examiner has set. Option 1 describes a comfortable life, closer to "a bed of roses", and option 2 describes freedom from duties, closer to "footloose and fancy-free"; neither is the meaning of the idiom. Remember: idiom questions test the figurative meaning, not the literal one, so reject options that simply restate the words of the phrase. Related idioms: "lie low" (keep out of sight), "get off scot-free" (escape without punishment) and "catch someone red-handed" (catch someone in the act).

Q 20 | eng | Vocabulary & Idioms
Consider the following statement:
"The way he appears or behaves is not true or real."
Which one of the following words most appropriately describes the person's character?
(1) Caveat
(2) Felony
(3) Masquerade
(4) Narcissist
= 3
~ Option 3, masquerade, is correct. A "masquerade" is a false show or pretence, an outward appearance that hides the truth; the word first meant a party where people wear masks. As a verb, to masquerade as something is to pretend to be someone or something one is not: "He masqueraded as a doctor." This fits a person whose appearance and behaviour are not true or real. Option 1, "caveat", is a warning or condition (from Latin "let him beware", as in "caveat emptor", let the buyer beware). Option 2, "felony", is a serious crime. Option 4, "narcissist", is a person with excessive interest in and admiration for himself; a narcissist may be vain, but the word does not mean false. Near synonyms of masquerade: "facade", "pretence", "charade" and "sham". A person who pretends to have virtues he lacks is a "hypocrite", and one who pretends to be someone else is an "impostor".

Q 21 | eng | Vocabulary & Idioms
Consider the following statement:
"He does not reveal his thoughts or feelings readily."
Which one of the following words most appropriately describes the person's character?
(1) Reticent
(2) Intangible
(3) Upfront
(4) Transient
= 1
~ Option 1, reticent, is correct. A "reticent" person is reserved and does not readily reveal his thoughts or feelings; the statement in the question is practically the dictionary definition of the word, as in "He was reticent about his private life." The noun is "reticence". Option 3, "upfront", means frank and open, the opposite of reticent. Option 2, "intangible", describes something that cannot be touched or clearly grasped, such as goodwill or a vague feeling of unease. Option 4, "transient", means lasting only a short time, as in "transient fame". Neither of these describes a person who keeps his feelings to himself. Near synonyms of reticent: "taciturn", "reserved", "uncommunicative" and "tight-lipped"; antonyms: "forthcoming", "communicative", "candid" and "outspoken". Trap: "reticent" is sometimes loosely used to mean "reluctant"; in careful English it means unwilling to speak or reveal, as in "reticent about her plans".

Q 22 | eng | Grammar & Usage
Fill in the blank by choosing the correct option:
"I think she is busy at the moment because ______ (she, wash) her clothes."
(1) she was washing
(2) she has just washed
(3) she is washing
(4) she had been washing
= 3
~ Option 3, "she is washing", is correct. The present continuous tense (is/am/are + verb-ing) is used for an action going on at the time of speaking. The words "at the moment" and "I think she is busy" show that the washing is happening now: "I think she is busy at the moment because she is washing her clothes." Option 1, "she was washing" (past continuous), and option 4, "she had been washing" (past perfect continuous), put the action in the past and do not fit the present "is busy". Option 2, "she has just washed" (present perfect), means the washing is already finished, which would not explain why she is busy now. Time markers that signal the present continuous: "now", "at present", "at the moment", "currently", "Look!" and "Listen!". Remember: stative verbs such as know, believe, own and think (meaning "believe") are not normally used in the continuous form, so we say "I think she is busy", not "I am thinking she is busy".

Q 23 | eng | Vocabulary & Idioms
What is the meaning of the underlined part "paddle my own canoe" of the following sentence?
In future I will "paddle my own canoe" without seeking support.
(1) To lean on one's siblings for support
(2) To utilize one's contacts
(3) To be independent and self-reliant
(4) To use one's own pitch
= 3
~ Option 3 is correct. To "paddle one's own canoe" means to be independent and self-reliant, to manage one's own affairs without help from others; the image is of a person moving his canoe forward with his own paddle. The words "without seeking support" in the sentence point the same way. Option 1, leaning on one's siblings, and option 2, using one's contacts, are both forms of depending on others, the opposite of the idiom. Option 4, "to use one's own pitch", is a distractor with no connection to the idiom. Related expressions: "stand on one's own feet" (be independent), "fend for oneself" (look after oneself without help) and "a self-made man" (one who succeeds by his own efforts). The opposite idea is "lean on someone" or "be tied to someone's apron strings" (be too dependent on someone, usually one's mother). Tip: when the sentence gives a clue such as "without seeking support", use it to confirm the idiom's meaning.

Q 24 | eng | Vocabulary & Idioms
What is the meaning of the underlined part "roller-coaster" of the following sentence?
She went through an emotional "roller-coaster" while attending the tumultuous family event.
(1) Enjoying life to the fullest
(2) Taking the emotions very seriously
(3) Talking to people who are emotional

Q 25 | eng | Grammar & Usage
Read the following two statements, each given in the form of Active Voice and Passive Voice:
Statement | Active Voice | Passive Voice
I. | I know you. | I am known to you.
II. | His presence irritates me sometimes. | I am sometimes irritated by his presence.
In which of the above is the Active Voice correctly expressed in the Passive Voice?
(1) I only
(2) II only
(3) Both I and II
(4) Neither I nor II
= 2
~ Option 2, II only, is correct. In changing active voice to passive, the object of the active verb becomes the subject, the verb becomes "be" plus the past participle in the same tense, and the doer usually follows "by". In I, "I know you", the object is "you", so the passive is "You are known to me"; "I am known to you" reverses the meaning and says that you know me. ("Know" takes "to", not "by", in the passive, just as we say "pleased with" and "surprised at".) In II, the object "me" correctly becomes the subject "I", the simple present "irritates" becomes "am irritated", the adverb "sometimes" moves next to the verb, and the doer "his presence" follows "by". So options 1 and 3, which accept I, and option 4, which rejects II, are wrong. Trap: always check who does the action in both versions; a passive sentence that swaps the doer and the receiver changes the meaning.

Q 26 | eng | Grammar & Usage
Read the following two statements, each given in the form of Active Voice and Passive Voice:
Statement | Active Voice | Passive Voice
I. | We were investigating the matter. | The matter is being investigated by us.
II. | The Government gave him an award. | He got an award from the Government.
In which of the above is the Active Voice correctly expressed in the Passive Voice?
(1) I only
(2) II only
(3) Both I and II
(4) Neither I nor II
= 4
~ Option 4, neither I nor II, is correct. In a change of voice the tense of the verb must not change. Sentence I is in the past continuous ("were investigating"), so its passive needs was/were + being + past participle: "The matter was being investigated by us." The given "is being investigated" wrongly shifts it to the present continuous. (2) II only
(3) Both I and II
(4) Neither I nor II

Statement | Direct Speech | Indirect Speech
I. | I said to him, "Go away." | I ordered him to go away.
II. | I shouted, "Let me go." | I ordered them to let me go.

(1) I only
(2) II only
(3) Both I and II
(4) Neither I nor II
= 1
~ Option 1, I only, is correct. Q 48 | eng | Grammar & Usage
Consider the following pairs:
Pair | Sentence with a blank to fill in | Most appropriate word to fill in
I. | The members of a society must abide ______ its rules. | in
II. | Candidates must comply ______ the rules of the examination. | with
III. | I would not intrude ______ your privacy. | on
IV. | Every man must be fully alive ______ his responsibilities. | for
How many of the above pairs is/are correctly matched?
(1) Only one
(2) Only two
(3) Only three
(4) All four
= 2
~ Option 2, only two, is correct: pairs II and III are right, and I and IV are wrong. These words take fixed prepositions. One "complies with" rules, orders or requests, meaning obeys or acts according to them: "Candidates must comply with the rules of the examination." One "intrudes on (or upon)" someone's privacy, time or affairs, meaning enters where one is not wanted: "I would not intrude on your privacy." Pair I is wrong because the correct phrase is "abide by", meaning to obey or accept a rule or decision: "The members of a society must abide by its rules." "Abide in" means to live or remain in a place. Pair IV is wrong because "alive to" means fully aware of or alert to something: "Every man must be fully alive to his responsibilities." "Alive for" is not idiomatic in this sense. Similar fixed prepositions often tested in such pairs: adhere to, conform to, accede to, dispense with, insist on and refrain from.

Q 49 | eng | Grammar & Usage
Consider the following statements:
I. I take exception to your remark.
II. I have great respect for his learning.
Which of the statements given above is/are grammatically correct?
(1) I only
(2) II only
(3) Both I and II
(4) Neither I nor II
= 3
~ Option 3, both I and II, is correct: both sentences are grammatically sound. "Take exception to" is a fixed phrase meaning to object to something or to be offended by it, and it always takes the preposition "to": "I take exception to your remark", "He took exception to being called lazy." The noun "respect", meaning admiration or high regard for a person or a quality, takes "for": "I have great respect for his learning", where "learning" means scholarship, the knowledge gained by study. Errors would be forms such as "take exception against your remark" or "respect on his learning"; neither occurs here, so option 4 and the single-statement options 1 and 2 fall away. Note other fixed phrases with "respect": "with respect to" and "in respect of" (concerning), and "pay one's respects" (to visit or greet someone formally). Such questions test appropriate prepositions, so learn nouns together with them: exception to, respect for, fondness for, aversion to, confidence in.

IV. Worshiped
How many of the above words is/are misspelled?

(2) Only two
(3) Only three
(4) All four

Q 52 | eco | Money, Banking & Inflation
In India, the operations of monetary policy framework with the primary objective of maintaining price stability is the function of:
(1) Ministry of Finance
(2) Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation
(3) Reserve Bank of India
(4) NITI Aayog
= 3
~ Option 3, the Reserve Bank of India, is correct. The RBI Act, 1934, as amended in 2016, entrusts the RBI with operating the monetary policy framework, with price stability as the primary objective while keeping growth in mind. Under Section 45ZA the Central Government, in consultation with the RBI, sets the inflation target once every five years; the target fixed in 2016 and retained for 2021–26 is 4% CPI inflation with a tolerance band of 2% on either side (2–6%). A six-member Monetary Policy Committee (MPC), chaired by the RBI Governor, with three RBI members and three external members, fixes the policy repo rate. If inflation stays outside the band for three consecutive quarters, the RBI must report the reasons to the Government. The other options are wrong: the Ministry of Finance handles fiscal policy (taxes, spending and borrowing), MoSPI compiles statistics such as CPI and GDP, and NITI Aayog is the Government's policy think tank. Trap: do not confuse monetary policy (RBI) with fiscal policy (Finance Ministry).

Q 53 | pol | Union & State Government
With reference to Indian polity, consider the following statements:
I. The moneys received by the Union Government from Provident Fund transactions are kept in Public Account.
II. Parliamentary authorization is mandatory for all the disbursements made from Public Account.
Which of the statements given above is/are correct?
(1) I only
(2) II only
(3) Both I and II
(4) Neither I nor II
= 1
~ Option 1, I only, is correct. Under Article 266(2), all public money received by or on behalf of the Government of India, other than that credited to the Consolidated Fund of India, goes into the Public Account of India. Provident fund deposits, small savings collections, judicial deposits, other deposits and remittances are kept there; the Government holds this money only as a banker or trustee, because it belongs to others and must be repaid. Remember the three funds: Consolidated Fund (Art. 266(1)), Public Account (Art. 266(2)) and Contingency Fund (Art. 267).

Q 61 | pol | Union & State Government
The President of India has discretionary power in:
(1) the imposition of President's rule in a State.
(2) withholding assent to the Money Bill.
(3) the Proclamation of Emergency.
(4) the appointment of the Prime Minister.
= 4
~ Option 4, the appointment of the Prime Minister, is correct. The President normally acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers (Article 74), but has situational discretion in appointing the Prime Minister under Article 75(1) when no party or coalition has a clear majority in the Lok Sabha, or when a Prime Minister dies in office and there is no obvious successor. Similar discretion arises in dismissing a Council of Ministers that cannot prove its majority and in dissolving the Lok Sabha when the Council has lost its majority. The other options are not discretionary: President's rule in a State (Article 356) is imposed on the advice of the Union Council of Ministers, and since the 44th Amendment (1978) a National Emergency (Article 352) can be proclaimed only on the written advice of the Union Cabinet. A Money Bill is introduced with the President's prior recommendation and cannot be returned for reconsideration, so assent is in practice always given. Trap: an ordinary Bill can be returned once for reconsideration, but a Money Bill cannot.

(4) Ransomware
= 3

= 2

Q 94 | sci | Computer Applications
Which one of the following is the fastest type of memory?
(1) Secondary memory
(2) Cache memory
(3) RAM
(4) Primary memory
= 2
~ Option 2 is correct: cache memory is the fastest of the four. In the memory hierarchy, speed and cost per byte fall and capacity rises as we move from CPU registers to cache memory, then main (primary) memory, and finally secondary storage. Cache is a small, very fast memory made of static RAM (SRAM), built on or close to the processor in levels L1, L2 and L3, that keeps copies of frequently used instructions and data so that the CPU need not wait for the slower main memory. RAM, usually dynamic RAM (DRAM) that must be refreshed constantly, is the main or primary memory and is slower than cache, so options 3 and 4 name the same, slower memory. Secondary memory such as hard disks, SSDs and optical discs is the slowest but is non-volatile and has the largest capacity. Trap: CPU registers are faster still, but they are not among the options. Remember the order: registers, cache, RAM, secondary storage.

Q 96 | qa | Algebra & Numbers
Let A, B, C be distinct digits. Consider a 2-digit number (AB) and a 3-digit number (BCB). If (AB)² = (BCB) where (BCB) > 500, then what is the possible value of (A + B + C)?
(1) 12
(2) 13
(3) 15
(4) 20
= 3
~ Option 3 is correct: A + B + C = 15. Step 1: BCB lies between 500 and 999, and 22² = 484 while 32² = 1024, so AB lies between 23 and 31. Step 2: the square must end in B, the units digit of AB, so B² must end in B; this allows only B = 0, 1, 5 or 6. Within 23 to 31 that leaves 25, 26, 30 and 31. Question: If x, y and z are three integers, then what is the value of xyz?
Statement-I: x, y and z are distinct prime numbers.
Statement-II: xyz divides 10800.
Which one of the following is correct in respect of the above Question and the Statements?
(1) The Question can be answered by using one of the Statements alone, but cannot be answered using the other Statement alone.
(2) The Question can be answered by using either Statement alone.
(3) The Question can be answered by using both the Statements together, but cannot be answered using either Statement alone.
(4) The Question cannot be answered even by using both the Statements together.
= 3
~ Option 3 is correct: both statements together are needed. Statement I alone: x, y and z are distinct primes, but they could be 2, 3, 5 (product 30), 2, 3, 7 (product 42) and so on, so xyz is not fixed. Statement II alone: many integer triples have a product that divides 10800, for example 1 × 1 × 2 = 2 or 2 × 3 × 4 = 24, so xyz is not fixed. Together: factorise 10800 = 2⁴ × 3³ × 5² (16 × 27 × 25). The only primes that divide it are 2, 3 and 5, so three distinct primes whose product divides 10800 must be 2, 3 and 5 in some order, and xyz = 2 × 3 × 5 = 30. The order of x, y and z does not matter, because only the product is asked. Tip: in divisibility conditions, the prime factorisation of the given number at once lists the possible prime factors. Trap: picking option 1 by thinking Statement II alone forces 2, 3 and 5; without 'distinct primes' the factors could repeat or be composite.

Q 118 | qa | Algebra & Numbers
A Question is given followed by two Statements I and II. Consider the Question and the Statements and mark the correct option.
LCM of two numbers p and q is 869.
Question: What is the difference between the two numbers?
Statement-I: p > 1, q > 1.
Statement-II: Sum of the two numbers is 90.
Which one of the following is correct in respect of the above Question and the Statements?
(1) The Question can be answered by using one of the Statements alone, but cannot be answered using the other Statement alone.
(2) The Question can be answered by using either Statement alone.
(3) The Question can be answered by using both the Statements together, but cannot be answered using either Statement alone.
(4) The Question can be answered without using these Statements.
= 2
~ Option 2 is UPSC's keyed answer. Step 1: 869 = 11 × 79, both primes, so p and q must come from the divisors 1, 11, 79 and 869; the pairs with LCM 869 include (1, 869), (11, 79), (11, 869) and (79, 869). Statement II alone: the only such pair with sum 90 is 11 and 79 (1 + 869 = 870), so the difference is 79 − 11 = 68; this statement clearly suffices. Statement I (p > 1, q > 1): the key reads it as ruling out the trivial factor 1, so that p and q are the two prime factors 11 and 79, again giving 68; on that reading either statement alone answers the question. Strictly, however, (11, 869) and (79, 869) also have both numbers above 1 and LCM 869, giving differences 858 and 790, so Statement I alone does not fix the difference, which would point to option 1. Tip: always factorise the LCM first and list every divisor pair. (Official key; some sources differ.)

Q 119 | qa | Geometry & Mensuration
A Question is given followed by two Statements I and II. Consider the Question and the Statements and mark the correct option.
There are two spheres whose radii are r and R (r < R), each less than 21 units. The difference in their surface areas is 1848 square units. (take π = 22/7)
Question: What are the values of r and R?
Statement-I: r is an integer.
Statement-II: R is an integer.
Which one of the following is correct in respect of the above Question and the Statements?
(1) The Question can be answered by using one of the Statements alone, but cannot be answered using the other Statement alone.
(2) The Question can be answered by using either Statement alone.
(3) The Question can be answered by using both the Statements together, but cannot be answered using either Statement alone.
(4) The Question can be answered without using these Statements.
= 3
~ Option 3 is correct: both statements together are needed. Step 1: surface area of a sphere = 4πr², so 4 × 22/7 × (R² − r²) = 1848, giving R² − r² = 1848 × 7 ÷ 88 = 147, that is, (R − r)(R + r) = 147. Statement I alone: if only r is an integer, R = √(147 + r²) need not be one; r can be any of 1, 2, … 17 (r = 1 gives R = √148 ≈ 12.2), with R still below 21, so it is not sufficient. Statement II alone: R can be any integer from 13 to 20 with r = √(R² − 147) (R = 13 gives r = √22), so it is not sufficient. Together: with both integers, R − r and R + r form a factor pair of 147 = 3 × 7²: 1 × 147, 3 × 49 or 7 × 21, giving (r, R) = (73, 74), (23, 26) or (7, 14). Only r = 7, R = 14 is below 21. Check: 4 × 22/7 × (196 − 49) = 88/7 × 147 = 1848. Trap: ignoring the 'less than 21' condition, which is what removes the larger pairs.

Q 120 | qa | Algebra & Numbers
A Question is given followed by two Statements I and II. Consider the Question and the Statements and mark the correct option.
Question: What is the remainder when (x + 1)ⁿ is divided by x (n is a natural number)?
Statement-I: n is divisible by 4.
Statement-II: n is divisible by 3.
Which one of the following is correct in respect of the above Question and the Statements?
(1) The Question can be answered by using one of the Statements alone, but cannot be answered using the other Statement alone.
(2) The Question can be answered by using either Statement alone.
(3) The Question can be answered by using both the Statements together, but cannot be answered using either Statement alone.
(4) The Question can be answered without using these Statements.
= 4
~ Option 4 is correct: the question can be answered without either statement. By the binomial theorem, (x + 1)ⁿ = xⁿ + nC1·xⁿ⁻¹ + nC2·xⁿ⁻² + … + nC(n−1)·x + 1. Every term except the last, 1, contains x as a factor, so on dividing by x the remainder is always 1, whatever the natural number n. The remainder theorem gives the same result: the remainder on dividing f(x) by x − 0 is f(0) = (0 + 1)ⁿ = 1. Numerical check (for a whole number x greater than 1): 4³ = 64 = 3 × 21 + 1, and 6² = 36 = 5 × 7 + 1. So knowing that n is divisible by 4 (Statement I) or by 3 (Statement II) adds nothing, and options 1 to 3, which assume a statement is needed, are wrong. Remember the companion result: (x − 1)ⁿ divided by x leaves (−1)ⁿ, which does depend on whether n is even or odd; do not confuse the two.
