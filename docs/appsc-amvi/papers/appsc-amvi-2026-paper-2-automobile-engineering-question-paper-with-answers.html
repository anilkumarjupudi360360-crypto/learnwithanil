  1. Q1 · Thermodynamics & Heat Transfer · Thermodynamics

    A system is in thermodynamic equilibrium when

    1. It exchanges mass but not energy with its surroundings
    2. It exchanges both mass and energy with its surroundings
    3. Macroscopic variables like pressure, volume and temperature do not change with time
    4. It exchanges energy but not mass with its surroundings
    Show answer

    Answer: (3) Macroscopic variables like pressure, volume and temperature do not change with time

    A system is in thermodynamic equilibrium when it is at the same time in thermal, mechanical and chemical equilibrium, so its macroscopic properties such as pressure, volume and temperature are uniform and do not change with time, with no net internal flows. Whether a system exchanges mass, energy or both with its surroundings only tells us whether it is open or closed; it does not decide whether the system is in equilibrium.

  2. Q2 · Thermodynamics & Heat Transfer · Thermodynamics

    The Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics states that if two systems are each in thermal equilibrium with a third system, then

    1. They are in mechanical equilibrium
    2. They are in chemical equilibrium
    3. They are in thermal equilibrium with each other
    4. They are in a state of total thermodynamic equilibrium
    Show answer

    Answer: (3) They are in thermal equilibrium with each other

    The Zeroth Law says that if bodies A and B are each in thermal equilibrium with a third body C, then A and B are in thermal equilibrium with each other: there is no net heat flow when they are brought into contact, because they are at the same temperature. It is the basis of temperature measurement, the third body being the thermometer. It says nothing about mechanical or chemical equilibrium.

  3. Q3 · Thermodynamics & Heat Transfer · Thermodynamics

    Which of the following is not an example of a pure substance ?

    1. Water (H₂O)
    2. Nitrogen (N₂)
    3. Air (a mixture of gases like O₂, N₂, Ar)
    4. Helium (He)
    Show answer

    Answer: (3) Air (a mixture of gases like O₂, N₂, Ar)

    A pure substance has a fixed and uniform chemical composition throughout. Water, nitrogen and helium are single chemical species and qualify. Air is a mixture of nitrogen, oxygen, argon and other gases. It behaves like a pure substance only while it remains a gas of uniform composition; when it is partly liquefied, its components condense at different temperatures and the liquid and vapour have different compositions, so air is not a true pure substance.

  4. Q4 · Thermodynamics & Heat Transfer · Thermodynamics

    The Mollier diagram is a plot of

    1. Temperature and entropy
    2. Temperature and enthalpy
    3. Pressure and enthalpy
    4. Enthalpy and entropy
    Show answer

    Answer: (4) Enthalpy and entropy

    The Mollier diagram, devised by Richard Mollier, plots enthalpy (h) on the vertical axis against entropy (s) on the horizontal axis, usually for steam. On this h–s chart an isentropic expansion is a vertical line and the enthalpy drop can be read directly, which makes it convenient for nozzle and turbine calculations. The temperature–entropy plot is the T–s diagram, and the pressure–enthalpy (p–h) chart is mainly used for refrigerants.

  5. Q5 · IC Engines, Fuels & Emissions · Engine Fundamentals & Cycles

    For a fixed amount of heat supplied and a fixed compression ratio, the thermal efficiency of the air-standard cycle follows the order

    1. Otto > Diesel > Dual
    2. Otto > Dual > Diesel
    3. Diesel > Otto > Dual
    4. Dual > Otto > Diesel
    Show answer

    Answer: (2) Otto > Dual > Diesel

    For the same compression ratio and the same heat input, the Otto cycle adds all its heat at constant volume at the end of compression, so the gas then expands through the full ratio, rejects the least heat and is the most efficient. The Diesel cycle adds heat at constant pressure while the gas expands, so it rejects the most heat. The dual cycle adds part at constant volume and part at constant pressure and lies between: η Otto > η Dual > η Diesel.

  6. Q6 · IC Engines, Fuels & Emissions · Engine Fundamentals & Cycles

    The Stirling cycle consists of

    1. Two isothermal and two constant pressure processes
    2. Two isothermal and two constant volume processes
    3. Two isothermal and two adiabatic processes
    4. Two adiabatic processes and one constant volume processes
    Show answer

    Answer: (2) Two isothermal and two constant volume processes

    The Stirling cycle consists of isothermal compression, constant-volume heat addition, isothermal expansion and constant-volume heat rejection. With a perfect regenerator, which stores the heat rejected at constant volume and returns it later, its efficiency equals that of the Carnot cycle working between the same temperatures. Two isothermal and two constant-pressure processes make the Ericsson cycle, and two isothermal and two adiabatic processes make the Carnot cycle.

    The diagram factor is defined as the ratio of

    1. Brake power to indicated power
    2. Indicated work done to brake work done
    3. Area of the actual indicator diagram to the area of the theoretical indicator diagram
    4. Thermal efficiency to mechanical efficiency
    Show answer

    Answer: (3) Area of the actual indicator diagram to the area of the theoretical indicator diagram

    Diagram factor = area of the actual indicator diagram ÷ area of the theoretical (hypothetical) indicator diagram, which equals the ratio of the actual to the theoretical mean effective pressure. It allows for losses such as valve throttling, heat transfer and the rounding of the diagram's corners, and it is always less than 1. The ratio of brake power to indicated power (option 1) is the mechanical efficiency.

  8. Q8 · Engineering Mechanics & Machine Design · Theory of Machines

    The radial distance from the pitch circle to the root of the tooth is known as the

    1. Addendum
    2. Clearance
    3. Dedendum
    4. Whole depth
    Show answer

    Answer: (3) Dedendum

    In gear terminology the dedendum is the radial distance from the pitch circle to the root (dedendum) circle, while the addendum is the radial distance from the pitch circle to the tip (addendum) circle. The whole depth is addendum plus dedendum. Clearance is the radial gap between the tip of a tooth and the root of the mating gear, equal to the dedendum minus the addendum.

  9. Q9 · Engineering Mechanics & Machine Design · Theory of Machines

    What is the correct mathematical formula for the module of a gear ? (Where D is the pitch circle diameter and N is the number of teeth)

    1. D/N
    2. D/(2N)
    3. 2D/N
    4. 3D/N
    Show answer

    Answer: (1) D/N

    The module m is the pitch circle diameter divided by the number of teeth, m = D/N, usually expressed in millimetres. It is the reciprocal of the diametral pitch (N/D). The circular pitch is π times the module, p = πD/N = πm, and two gears can mesh only if they have the same module. For example, a gear with a pitch circle diameter of 100 mm and 50 teeth has a module of 2 mm.

  10. Q10 · Engineering Mechanics & Machine Design · Theory of Machines

    In a simple gear train, how is the driven gear's motion related to the driver gear if there is an odd number of gears ?

    1. Opposite direction
    2. Same direction
    3. Does not depends on gear ratio
    4. Depends on gear ratio
    Show answer

    Answer: (2) Same direction

    Each external mesh reverses the direction of rotation. With an odd number of gears in a simple train (for example driver, one idler and driven) there is an even number of meshes, so the driven gear turns in the same direction as the driver; with an even number of gears it turns the opposite way. Idlers change only the direction, not the speed ratio, which depends only on the teeth of the driver and the driven gear.
