  1. Q1 · English · Vocabulary

    Directions : Fill in the blanks in the following sentences by selecting the most appropriate option from the choices given below each. The intricate design ______ meticulous attention during manufacturing.

    1. calls for
    2. comes to
    3. gets over
    4. puts off
    Show answer

    Answer: (1) calls for

    "Calls for" is the right phrasal verb: "call for" means to require, need or demand. An intricate (very detailed, complicated) design naturally requires meticulous (very careful, precise) attention, so "The intricate design calls for meticulous attention during manufacturing" makes perfect sense. "Comes to" means to reach a total or to regain consciousness ("The bill comes to ₹500"), which does not fit. "Gets over" means to recover from something ("She got over her illness"), and "puts off" means to postpone or to discourage ("They put off the meeting"); neither can take "attention" in this sense. Another example of the rule: "This situation calls for quick action." Tip: a phrasal verb can change its meaning completely with the particle, so learn them in sets: call for (require), call off (cancel), call on (visit), call up (phone or summon). Trap: "come to" looks similar in form but refers to totals, decisions or waking up.

  2. Q2 · English · Grammar

    Directions : Fill in the blanks in the following sentences by selecting the most appropriate option from the choices given below each. Each of the buildings ______ unique design.

    1. have
    2. has
    3. are
    4. were
    Show answer

    Answer: (2) has

    "Has" is correct because "each" is singular and takes a singular verb. In "Each of the buildings has a unique design", the real subject is "Each" (each one), and "of the buildings" is only a phrase describing it, so the verb agrees with "Each", not with "buildings". "Have" is the plural form, "are" is plural and also gives no sense of possession, and "were" is plural past tense. The same rule applies to every, either, neither, one of, everyone and each one: "One of my friends lives in Cuttack"; "Neither of the answers is correct." The printed sentence leaves out the article "a" before "unique design", but that does not change the choice of verb: only "has" agrees with the subject. Trap: don't let the plural noun just before the verb ("buildings") pull the verb into the plural; this "proximity" error is a favourite trap in competitive exams.

  3. Q3 · English · Vocabulary

    Directions : Fill in the blanks in the following sentences by selecting the most appropriate option from the choices given below each. The mineral deposits lie ______, beyond the reach of conventional mining.

    1. subterranean
    2. subterraneously
    3. extraterrestrial
    4. insubordinately
    Show answer

    Answer: (2) subterraneously

    The key's answer is "subterraneously", meaning beneath the surface of the earth, underground. The idea behind it is that the blank tells us where or how the deposits lie, so an adverb is expected after the verb "lie", just as in "lie underground" or "lie deep". It also matches the clue "beyond the reach of conventional mining": ordinary mining cannot reach deposits buried that far down. "Extraterrestrial" means from outside the Earth (like meteorites), and "insubordinately" means disobediently, so neither makes sense here. The word comes from Latin sub (under) + terra (earth), the same root as terrain and territory; the adjective form is "subterranean" ("subterranean rivers", "a subterranean tunnel"). Note: "subterraneously" is a rare word, and the adjective "subterranean" can also be defended in this sentence, because "lie" can be followed by an adjective describing a state, as in "lie dormant", "lie hidden" or "lie idle". (Official key; some sources differ.)

  4. Q4 · English · Grammar

    Directions : Fill in the blanks in the following sentences by selecting the most appropriate option from the choices given below each. It was ______ honour to meet the author.

    1. a
    2. an
    3. the
    4. some
    Show answer

    Answer: (2) an

    "An" is correct because the choice between "a" and "an" depends on the first sound of the next word, not its first letter. In "honour" the h is silent, so the word begins with a vowel sound ("on-er"), and vowel sounds take "an": "It was an honour to meet the author." "A" is used before consonant sounds, so "a honour" is wrong. "The" would point to a particular, already-known honour, which does not suit the general sense of "It was an honour to ...", and "some" is used with uncountable or plural nouns and would change the meaning to "a certain amount of honour". Other silent-h words that take "an": an hour, an honest man, an heir, an honorarium. The reverse case: words that start with a vowel letter but a consonant sound take "a": a university, a European, a one-day match, a unique idea. Trap: "a hotel", "a historic event" and "a hospital" take "a" because their h is pronounced.

  5. Q5 · English · Vocabulary

    Directions : Fill in the blanks in the following sentences by selecting the most appropriate option from the choices given below each. The concert was so ______ that many people were moved to tears.

    1. chaotic
    2. touching
    3. dull
    4. loud
    Show answer

    Answer: (2) touching

    "Touching" fits because it means emotionally moving, stirring tender feelings such as sympathy or joy. The sentence uses the "so ... that" pattern, in which the second part shows the result of the first: the concert was so touching that people were moved to tears. "Chaotic" (disorderly, confused) might upset people but would not move them to tears; "dull" (boring) would leave them unmoved; and "loud" describes volume, not feeling. Synonyms of touching worth knowing: moving, poignant, heart-warming, stirring. Example: "The farewell speech of the retiring teacher was so touching that the whole class wept." Vocabulary tip: the adjective "touching" (emotionally moving) is different from the verb "touch" (make contact), and "touchy" means easily offended, a completely different word. Trap: in such sentences always look for the clue phrase, here "moved to tears", and pick the word whose meaning produces that result rather than one that merely describes a concert.

  6. Q6 · English · Vocabulary

    Directions : Fill in the blanks in the following sentences by selecting the most appropriate option from the choices given below each. The novel is known for its ______ style, which contrasts sharply with the author's earlier complex work.

    1. simple
    2. happy
    3. intricate
    4. shallow
    Show answer

    Answer: (1) simple

    "Simple" is correct because the sentence sets up a contrast: the novel's style "contrasts sharply with the author's earlier complex work", so the blank needs the opposite of "complex", which is "simple" (plain, easy to follow). "Intricate" means complicated or full of detail, the same as complex, so there would be no contrast at all. "Happy" describes mood or tone, not how complex a style is. "Shallow" means lacking depth and is a negative judgement, whereas the sentence presents the style as something the novel "is known for"; the natural opposite of a complex style is a simple or lucid one, not a shallow one. Contrast signals to watch for: unlike, whereas, while, but, although, in contrast to, contrasts with. Example: "Unlike his long speeches, his letters were short and simple." Antonym pairs useful for exams: complex/simple, intricate/plain, verbose/concise, obscure/lucid. Trap: picking "intricate" because it sounds literary; read the contrast clue first.

  7. Q7 · English · Vocabulary

    Directions : Fill in the blanks in the following sentences by selecting the most appropriate option from the choices given below each. She ______ her initial enthusiasm, giving her team a positive outlook.

    1. gave up
    2. held on to
    3. put up with
    4. got over
    Show answer

    Answer: (2) held on to

    "Held on to" is correct: to hold on to something means to keep it and not let it go. She kept her early enthusiasm, and that is why her team got a positive outlook: "She held on to her initial enthusiasm, giving her team a positive outlook." "Gave up" means abandoned or stopped ("she gave up smoking"), which would leave the team discouraged, not positive. "Got over" means recovered from something unpleasant ("got over the shock"), and one does not need to recover from enthusiasm. "Put up with" means tolerated or endured ("put up with the noise"), used for something annoying, not for a good feeling. Another example: "Despite the losses, the captain held on to his belief in the young players." Tip: the participle phrase "giving her team a positive outlook" shows the result, so choose the verb that produces a positive result. Trap: "got over" and "gave up" both suggest losing the feeling, the opposite of what the sentence needs.

  8. Q8 · English · Vocabulary

    Directions : Fill in the blanks in the following sentences by selecting the most appropriate option from the choices given below each. Her final exam results were ______ surprising, even to her parents.

    1. meticulously
    2. fast
    3. slowly
    4. hardly
    Show answer

    Answer: (4) hardly

    "Hardly" is the right word: it is an adverb of degree meaning "scarcely" or "almost not", and it can modify an adjective such as "surprising". "Her final exam results were hardly surprising" means the results were expected, since she had been doing well. "Meticulously" (very carefully) and "slowly" are adverbs of manner that describe how an action is done, so results cannot be "meticulously surprising" or "slowly surprising"; "fast" describes speed and is not used before an adjective like this. Remember that "hardly" already carries a negative sense, so don't add another negative: say "I can hardly believe it", not "I can't hardly believe it". Also don't confuse "hardly" (scarcely) with "hard" (with effort): "He worked hard" and "He hardly worked" have opposite meanings. Similar degree adverbs: barely, scarcely, rather, quite. Trap: "-ly" alone does not make a word fit; check whether the adverb can sensibly describe the adjective that follows.

  9. Q9 · English · Grammar

    Directions : Fill in the blanks in the following sentences by selecting the most appropriate option from the choices given below each. The pair of shoes ______ on sale at the store.

    1. is
    2. are
    3. be
    4. were
    Show answer

    Answer: (1) is

    "Is" is correct because the true subject is "pair", which is singular; "of shoes" only tells us what the pair consists of. So the verb must be singular and, since the sale is happening now, in the present tense: "The pair of shoes is on sale at the store." "Are" and "were" are plural forms, and "were" is also past tense; "be" is the base form and cannot follow a singular subject in a normal statement. The same rule works for other "a pair of" and "a set of" phrases: "A pair of scissors is on the table", "A set of books was gifted to the library", "A bouquet of flowers looks lovely." But nouns such as shoes, trousers, scissors and spectacles take a plural verb when used alone: "My shoes are new." Trap: the plural noun "shoes" sits right before the verb and tempts you to write "are"; always find the head noun of the subject first.

  10. Q10 · English · Grammar

    Directions : Fill in the blanks in the following sentences by selecting the most appropriate option from the choices given below each. They ______ the Asia Cup trophy in 1993.

    1. win
    2. has won
    3. won
    4. winning
    Show answer

    Answer: (3) won

    "Won" is correct: when a sentence names a definite past time, such as "in 1993", "yesterday", "last year" or "two days ago", the simple past tense is used: "They won the Asia Cup trophy in 1993." The present perfect "has won" links the past to the present and cannot be used with a specific past date; it is also wrong in number, because "They" needs "have", not "has". "Win" is present tense and clashes with 1993, and "winning" is a participle that needs a helping verb ("were winning"). Compare: "India has won the Asia Cup several times" (no time stated, so the present perfect is fine) with "India won the Asia Cup in 2023" (definite time, so simple past). Trap: never pair "has/have + past participle" with a finished time expression; "I have seen him yesterday" is a classic error-spotting mistake. Tip: words like already, ever, never, just and since usually go with the present perfect.
