  1. 2023 · Paper II · Q1(a) · 10 marks
    Ancient India · Stone Age and the Indus Valley (Harappan) Civilization

    Examine the survival and continuity of Harappan culture in the present times.

    Model answer · free sample

    Harappan continuityMaterial cultureProto-Hindu beliefsSubstratum of Indian civilization

    The Harappan Civilization (mature phase c. 2600–1900 BCE) declined as a city culture, but it did not vanish. Its farming, crafts, beliefs and customs passed through Late Harappan and Chalcolithic village cultures into Vedic and later Indian life. Much of this inheritance survives today in villages, temples and households, so the Harappan world forms the substratum of Indian civilization rather than a dead past.

    1. Town planning and technology

    • Planned settlements: grid-pattern streets, covered drains and soak pits at Mohenjodaro and Harappa anticipate the drainage and sanitation principles of modern town planning.
    • Water management: the reservoirs of Dholavira and the Great Bath foreshadow the stepwells and temple tanks of later India.
    • Weights and measures: cubical chert weights followed a binary system (1, 2, 4, 8, 16…); the 16-anna rupee, used until decimal coinage in 1957, follows the same logic.
    • Transport: terracotta models of bullock carts and boats closely resemble the carts and boats still used in Sindh, Rajasthan and Gujarat.

    2. Economy and crafts

    • Agriculture: the criss-cross furrowed field at Kalibangan resembles the mixed cropping still practised in Rajasthan.
    • Cotton: the Harappans were among the earliest people to grow cotton; cotton textiles remain a hallmark of Indian craft.
    • Crafts: carnelian bead-making (still practised at Khambhat), black-on-red pottery and the potter's wheel continue in village crafts.

    3. Religion and beliefs

    • Mother Goddess: numerous female figurines anticipate Shakti worship and village goddess cults.
    • Proto-Shiva: John Marshall read the 'Pashupati' seal, a horned figure in a yogic posture surrounded by animals, as proto-Shiva; linga–yoni stones point the same way.
    • Nature worship: the sacred pipal, the humped bull and the swastika remain objects of veneration.
    • Ritual: fire altars at Kalibangan and Lothal, and ritual bathing at the Great Bath, echo Vedic sacrifices and the Hindu emphasis on purificatory bathing.

    4. Social customs and daily life

    • Ornaments: the bronze 'dancing girl' wears many bangles; red pigment in the hair-parting of figurines at Nausharo resembles sindoor (J.M. Kenoyer's interpretation).
    • Leisure and homes: dice, toy carts, whistles and courtyard houses with rooms opening inward survive in village life.

    5. Limits of continuity

    • Lost elements: the script, standardised town planning and trade with Mesopotamia disappeared; cities returned only with the second urbanisation of the 6th century BCE.
    • Caveat: as the script is undeciphered, religious continuities are inferences from archaeology, not certainties.

    AP angle: the mother-goddess cult lives on in AP's village goddesses (gramadevatas) such as Gangamma and Poleramma, and bullock carts and courtyard houses remain common in rural Andhra.

    Thus Harappan culture survives less in monuments than in everyday practices, a striking example of continuity amid change in Indian civilization.

  2. 2023 · Paper II · Q1(b) · 10 marks
    Ancient India · Satavahanas, Sangam age and Guptas; foreign travellers

    Examine the socio-religious life and administration of early India with respect to the writings of Chinese travellers such as Fa-Hien, Hiuen-Tsang and I-Tsang.

    Model answer · free sample

    Chinese pilgrimsFa-Hien and Hiuen-TsangGupta and Harsha administrationBuddhist bias

    Chinese Buddhist pilgrims came to India to visit the sacred places of the Buddha and to collect texts. Fa-Hien's Fo-kuo-ki (early 5th century), Hiuen-Tsang's Si-yu-ki (7th century) and I-Tsing's record of Buddhist practice (late 7th century) are dated, first-hand outsider sources on Gupta and post-Gupta India, though coloured by their Buddhist outlook.

    1. Socio-religious life

    • Diet and customs (Fa-Hien): the people of Madhyadesha were prosperous; most avoided meat, liquor, onion and garlic, no pigs or fowls were kept, and cowries served as money in markets.
    • Untouchability: Fa-Hien saw Chandalas living outside towns and striking a piece of wood to announce their approach; Hiuen-Tsang found butchers, fishermen and scavengers living outside settlements, showing a hardening of caste.
    • Varna and learning (Hiuen-Tsang): he describes the four varnas and Brahmana vegetarianism, and Nalanda as a great centre with thousands of students, where he himself studied under Silabhadra.
    • Religion: Fa-Hien found Buddhism flourishing alongside Brahmanism; by Hiuen-Tsang's time Buddhism was declining in many regions. Harsha's quinquennial assembly at Prayag honoured the Buddha, Surya and Shiva.
    • Monastic life (I-Tsing): after about ten years at Nalanda, he described the vinaya discipline, the curriculum, Sanskrit grammar, medicine, diet and the hygiene of monks.

    2. Administration

    • Gupta mildness (Fa-Hien): no registration of households; crimes were punished mostly by fines, with the cutting off of the right hand only for repeated rebellion; free hospitals and rest-houses existed at Pataliputra.
    • Harsha's revenue (Hiuen-Tsang): taxation was light, about one-sixth of the produce; revenue from crown lands was divided into four parts: for state affairs, for public servants, for scholars and for religious gifts.
    • Officials and labour: officials were paid by assignments of land, and forced labour was limited and paid.
    • Law and order: punishments were harsher than in Gupta times (mutilation and ordeals by water, fire, weighing and poison), and roads were unsafe; Hiuen-Tsang himself was robbed.

    AP angle: Hiuen-Tsang visited Andhra (around Vengi) and Dhanakataka (the Amaravati region) and found many Buddhist monasteries, most of them deserted, evidence of Buddhism's decline in its old Andhra heartland.

    3. Value and limitations

    • Value: precise dates and first-hand detail fill gaps in Indian texts; Hiuen-Tsang's descriptions later helped archaeologists identify Buddhist sites.
    • Limitations: a Buddhist bias, little political history, generalisations from short stays, and problems with Indian languages and names.

    Read with inscriptions and Bana's Harshacharita, these accounts remain indispensable for reconstructing the society and governance of early India.
