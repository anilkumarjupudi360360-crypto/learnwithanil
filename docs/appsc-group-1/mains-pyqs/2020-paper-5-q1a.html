APPSC Group-1 Mains 2020 · Paper V · Q1(a)

Question · Q1(a) · 10 marks

What is "life expectancy at birth"? Data shows that the global average life expectancy has more than doubled since 1900, and is now above 70 years. Describe three to five factors which have contributed to improvement in life expectancy.

Syllabus: Biotechnology and health · Health and diseases
Model answer · free sample

Life expectancy at birthMortality declineHealth transitionPublic health

Life expectancy at birth is the average number of years a newborn would live if the age-specific death rates of a given period stayed unchanged throughout its life. It summarises the health of a whole population rather than predicting any one person's lifespan. Globally it has risen from about 32 years in 1900 to 73.3 years in 2024 (UN World Population Prospects 2024); India's rose from about 32 years in 1951 to a little over 70 years today.

1. Why it matters as an indicator

  • Captures deaths at every age: a death in infancy removes many potential years, so falling child mortality lifts the average far more than gains in old age.
  • Measure of development: it is one of the three dimensions of the UNDP's Human Development Index and reflects nutrition, sanitation and health care together.

2. Factors behind the improvement

  • Vaccines and control of infections: smallpox was declared eradicated in 1980 and India became polio-free in 2014; the Universal Immunisation Programme (1985) and Mission Indradhanush protect children from measles, diphtheria, tetanus and severe forms of TB.
  • Clean water and sanitation: sewers and chlorinated water cut cholera and typhoid in 19th-century Europe; in India, Swachh Bharat (2014) and the Jal Jeevan Mission (tap water in about 82% of rural homes by July 2026) play the same role.
  • Medicines and medical technology: antibiotics after penicillin (1928), oral rehydration salts for diarrhoea, anti-malarials and TB drugs, and later treatment for heart disease, diabetes and cancer.
  • Better nutrition and food security: the Green Revolution, the public distribution system, the National Food Security Act (2013) and mid-day meals prevented famines and reduced child undernutrition.
  • Mother-and-child care and wider development: institutional deliveries (about 89% of births, NFHS-5) and newborn care have brought India's infant mortality to 24 per 1,000 and maternal mortality to 87 per lakh live births (SRS); income growth, female literacy and smaller families reinforce these gains.

3. Challenges that remain

  • Quality of years: non-communicable diseases now cause about two-thirds of deaths in India; air pollution and drug resistance threaten healthy years.
  • Inequality: infant mortality ranges from 8 in Kerala to 36 in Chhattisgarh (SRS 2024), and rural rates exceed urban ones.
  • Shocks: COVID-19 cut global life expectancy in 2020–21; it has returned to pre-pandemic levels in nearly all countries since 2022 (UN, 2024).
  • Ageing: more elderly people need pensions, geriatric care and long-term support.

AP angle: Andhra Pradesh's maternal mortality ratio of 30 (2021–23) is the joint lowest in India with Kerala; Dr NTR Vaidya Seva gives cashless hospital care, and a Universal Health Policy approved in 2025 aims to widen it.

The doubling of life expectancy owes more to public health (vaccines, water, food and safe births) than to hospitals alone. The next goal is to add healthy years, through prevention of chronic disease, clean air and universal health coverage.
