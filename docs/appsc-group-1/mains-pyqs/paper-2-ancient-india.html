Stone Age and the Indus Valley (Harappan) Civilization · asked 3 times

  1. 2025 · Paper II · Q1(a) · 10 marks

    Discuss the origin and expansion of the Indus Valley Civilization.

    Model answer · free sample

    Harappan urbanismMehrgarhIndigenous originSpatial expansion

    The Indus Valley or Harappan Civilization was South Asia's first urban culture, with a mature phase of about 2600–1900 BCE. It came to light when Daya Ram Sahni excavated Harappa (1921) and R.D. Banerji excavated Mohenjo-daro (1922). Planned cities, standard weights, seals and an undeciphered script show a highly organised society. Its origin and spread are best understood through its Early, Mature and Late Harappan phases.

    1. Origin: two views

    • Diffusionist view: Mortimer Wheeler and Gordon Childe argued that the idea of city life came from Mesopotamia, pointing to trade contacts and some shared traits.
    • Indigenous evolution (now accepted): excavations at Mehrgarh in Baluchistan (J.-F. Jarrige) show farming villages from about 7000 BCE, with wheat, barley, cattle and mud-brick houses, growing step by step towards urban life.
    • Early Harappan phase (c. 3200–2600 BCE): sites such as Amri, Kot Diji, Kalibangan and Rehman Dheri already had fortifications, wheel-made pottery and planned layouts, the direct ancestors of the mature cities.
    • Distinctiveness: the script, seals, grid planning and covered drains have no Mesopotamian model, and the Rakhigarhi ancient-DNA study (2019) traced Harappan ancestry largely to local populations.

    2. Expansion and extent

    • Area: the civilization covered about 1.3 million sq km, larger than ancient Egypt or Mesopotamia.
    • Core along rivers: major cities followed the Indus and the Ghaggar-Hakra — Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, Ganweriwala, Rakhigarhi, Kalibangan and Banawali.
    • Coastal Gujarat: Lothal (with its dockyard), Dholavira (with its water reservoirs) and Surkotada linked the civilization to sea trade.
    • Frontiers: Manda in the north, Daimabad in the south, Sutkagen-dor in the west and Alamgirpur in the east; Shortughai in Afghanistan was a trading outpost.

    3. Drivers of expansion

    • Fertile floodplains: farming of wheat and barley on river floodplains produced a surplus to feed cities, craftsmen and traders.
    • Trade and raw materials: copper from Khetri, lapis lazuli from Badakhshan, and shell and carnelian from Gujarat; Mesopotamian texts call the region "Meluhha".
    • Standardisation: bricks in the ratio 1:2:4, uniform weights and seals sustained a common economic system across distant regions.
    • Later shift: after about 1900 BCE, as rivers changed course and trade declined (the causes are debated), Late Harappan settlements moved east into the Ganga–Yamuna doab and south into Gujarat and Maharashtra.

    AP angle: Andhra lay outside the Harappan zone; it was then home to Neolithic farming and cattle-herding communities of the southern Deccan, with remains at sites such as Nagarjunakonda.

    The Harappan Civilization was thus an indigenous growth that expanded along rivers and trade routes. Ongoing work at Rakhigarhi and Dholavira (a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2021) keeps refining this picture.

  2. 2023 · Paper II · Q1(a) · 10 marks

    Examine the survival and continuity of Harappan culture in the present times.

    Model answer · free sample

    Harappan continuityMaterial cultureProto-Hindu beliefsSubstratum of Indian civilization

    The Harappan Civilization (mature phase c. 2600–1900 BCE) declined as a city culture, but it did not vanish. Its farming, crafts, beliefs and customs passed through Late Harappan and Chalcolithic village cultures into Vedic and later Indian life. Much of this inheritance survives today in villages, temples and households, so the Harappan world forms the substratum of Indian civilization rather than a dead past.

    1. Town planning and technology

    • Planned settlements: grid-pattern streets, covered drains and soak pits at Mohenjodaro and Harappa anticipate the drainage and sanitation principles of modern town planning.
    • Water management: the reservoirs of Dholavira and the Great Bath foreshadow the stepwells and temple tanks of later India.
    • Weights and measures: cubical chert weights followed a binary system (1, 2, 4, 8, 16…); the 16-anna rupee, used until decimal coinage in 1957, follows the same logic.
    • Transport: terracotta models of bullock carts and boats closely resemble the carts and boats still used in Sindh, Rajasthan and Gujarat.

    2. Economy and crafts

    • Agriculture: the criss-cross furrowed field at Kalibangan resembles the mixed cropping still practised in Rajasthan.
    • Cotton: the Harappans were among the earliest people to grow cotton; cotton textiles remain a hallmark of Indian craft.
    • Crafts: carnelian bead-making (still practised at Khambhat), black-on-red pottery and the potter's wheel continue in village crafts.

    3. Religion and beliefs

    • Mother Goddess: numerous female figurines anticipate Shakti worship and village goddess cults.
    • Proto-Shiva: John Marshall read the 'Pashupati' seal, a horned figure in a yogic posture surrounded by animals, as proto-Shiva; linga–yoni stones point the same way.
    • Nature worship: the sacred pipal, the humped bull and the swastika remain objects of veneration.
    • Ritual: fire altars at Kalibangan and Lothal, and ritual bathing at the Great Bath, echo Vedic sacrifices and the Hindu emphasis on purificatory bathing.

    4. Social customs and daily life

    • Ornaments: the bronze 'dancing girl' wears many bangles; red pigment in the hair-parting of figurines at Nausharo resembles sindoor (J.M. Kenoyer's interpretation).
    • Leisure and homes: dice, toy carts, whistles and courtyard houses with rooms opening inward survive in village life.

    5. Limits of continuity

    • Lost elements: the script, standardised town planning and trade with Mesopotamia disappeared; cities returned only with the second urbanisation of the 6th century BCE.
    • Caveat: as the script is undeciphered, religious continuities are inferences from archaeology, not certainties.

    AP angle: the mother-goddess cult lives on in AP's village goddesses (gramadevatas) such as Gangamma and Poleramma, and bullock carts and courtyard houses remain common in rural Andhra.

    Thus Harappan culture survives less in monuments than in everyday practices, a striking example of continuity amid change in Indian civilization.

  3. 2020 · Paper II · Q1(a) · 10 marks

    Critically explain the salient features of urban planning of Harappan Civilization.

    Model answer · free sample

    Town planningCitadel and lower townDrainage systemStandardisation

    The Harappan Civilization (mature phase c. 2600–1900 BCE) was India's first urban culture, the Bronze Age "first urbanisation". Excavations at Harappa (Daya Ram Sahni, 1921), Mohenjo-daro (R.D. Banerji, 1922), Kalibangan, Lothal, Dholavira and Rakhigarhi reveal a remarkable degree of civic planning: planned streets, standard bricks, covered drains and public buildings, spread over a vast area, yet without any clearly identified palace or temple.

    1. Layout of the city

    • Grid pattern: main streets ran north–south and east–west, crossing at right angles and dividing the town into rectangular blocks; narrow lanes led into the residential quarters.
    • Two-part division: a raised, fortified citadel on the west held public buildings, while a larger lower town on the east housed the people. Dholavira had a three-fold division into citadel, middle town and lower town.
    • Zoning of activities: bead-making workshops at Lothal and Chanhudaro and circular working floors near the granaries of Harappa suggest planned use of space.

    2. Buildings and materials

    • Standardised bricks: burnt bricks in the uniform ratio 1:2:4 were used across a huge area, showing common standards of measurement; Kalibangan used mud bricks and Dholavira mainly stone.
    • Houses: one or two storeys built around courtyards, with wells, bathrooms and kitchens; doors opened into side lanes rather than main streets, ensuring privacy.
    • Public structures: the Great Bath (about 12 m × 7 m × 2.4 m) at Mohenjo-daro, made watertight with a layer of bitumen; granaries at Harappa; the dockyard at Lothal; and the stone reservoirs of Dholavira.

    3. Sanitation and water management

    • Covered drainage: house drains joined brick-lined street drains with manholes and soak pits, among the most advanced systems of the Bronze Age world.
    • Water supply: Mohenjo-daro had several hundred wells, while Dholavira stored rain and stream water in a chain of rock-cut reservoirs in the dry Kutch region.
    • Civic discipline: regular cleaning of drains and disposal of refuse imply some form of municipal authority.

    4. A critical view

    • Uniformity exaggerated: Lothal had a single enclosing wall, Kalibangan fortified both parts and Banawali's streets were not on a grid, so there was no single template.
    • Debated functions: whether the "granary" was a granary or an assembly hall, and the "dockyard" a dock or a water tank, is still discussed.
    • Decline: in the late phase, houses at Mohenjo-daro encroached on streets and building quality fell, showing weakening civic control.
    • Unknown authority: with no palaces or royal tombs, it is unclear whether priests, merchants or a council planned and enforced civic rules.

    Harappan planning thus combined standardisation, sanitation and zoning on a scale unmatched in its age, even if it was less uniform than once believed. Dholavira's inscription as a UNESCO World Heritage Site (2021) shows its global value, and its water harvesting and drainage still offer lessons for planned cities such as Amaravati.

Vedic age, Buddhism and Jainism · asked 2 times

  1. 2025 · Paper II · Q1(b) · 10 marks

    Briefly analyze the teachings of Buddha and Mahavira.

    Model answer · free sample

    Shramana traditionFour Noble TruthsTriratnaAhimsa

    In the 6th–5th centuries BCE, the Gangetic valley saw the growth of cities, trade and a shramana (ascetic) reaction against costly Vedic sacrifices, ritualism and Brahmanical dominance. Gautama Buddha and Vardhamana Mahavira, the 24th Jain Tirthankara, gave this protest its most lasting form. Both offered liberation through ethical conduct rather than ritual, and both preached in the people's languages, Pali and Ardhamagadhi.

    1. Teachings of Buddha

    • Four Noble Truths: life is full of suffering (dukkha); desire (tanha) is its cause; suffering can end; and the way to end it is the Eightfold Path.
    • Ashtangika Marga (Middle Path): right view, intention, speech, action, livelihood, effort, mindfulness and concentration, avoiding both luxury and harsh austerity.
    • Philosophy: dependent origination (pratityasamutpada), impermanence (anicca) and no permanent soul (anatta); karma decides rebirth, and the goal is nirvana.
    • Social message: silent on God; rejected Vedic authority, animal sacrifice and caste; opened the sangha to all, including women.

    2. Teachings of Mahavira

    • Triratna: right faith, right knowledge and right conduct lead to liberation (kaivalya) from the cycle of birth.
    • Five vows: ahimsa, satya, asteya and aparigraha came from Parshvanatha, and Mahavira added brahmacharya; monks keep them strictly as mahavratas, lay followers as milder anuvratas.
    • Soul in everything: even stones, plants and water have a soul (jiva); hence extreme non-violence, fasting and austerity.
    • Anekantavada and syadvada: truth has many sides and can be stated only conditionally, a philosophy of tolerance.
    • Attitude to authority: rejected the Vedas and a creator God, but criticised varna less sharply than Buddhism.

    3. Analysis: common ground and differences

    • Common ground: both were Kshatriya teachers who rejected ritualism, Vedic authority and priestly monopoly, and stressed karma, rebirth and moral conduct.
    • Differences: Buddha's Middle Path was practical, while Mahavira demanded rigorous austerity; Buddhism denied a permanent soul, Jainism saw souls everywhere.
    • Spread: Buddhism's missionary sangha and royal patronage (Ashoka) made it a world religion; Jainism's rigour kept it smaller but enduring among merchants.
    • Social appeal: both attracted the new merchant class, and their ethics of non-violence suited trade and settled farming.

    AP angle: Buddhism flourished at Amaravati, Nagarjunakonda and Bhattiprolu, and Acharya Nagarjuna developed the Madhyamika philosophy; Jain remains survive at Ramatheertham (Vizianagaram) and Danavulapadu (YSR Kadapa).

    Their message of ahimsa, tolerance and self-discipline inspired Ashoka's dhamma and Gandhi's satyagraha, and it remains relevant in an age of conflict and excessive consumption.

  2. 2020 · Paper II · Q1(b) · 10 marks

    Critically explain the causes of the emergence of Buddhism in ancient India.

    Model answer · free sample

    Heterodox sectsVedic ritualismSecond urbanisationMiddle Path

    Buddhism arose in the 6th–5th century BCE in the middle Gangetic plain, founded by Gautama Buddha of the Sakya clan. It was one of several heterodox sects, along with Jainism, the Ajivikas and the Charvakas, that challenged Vedic orthodoxy. Its emergence reflected deep religious, social, economic and political changes in the age of the second urbanisation, when iron, towns, trade and large states transformed north India.

    1. Religious causes

    • Ritualism: later Vedic religion was dominated by costly yajnas and animal sacrifices conducted by priests, which common people could neither afford nor understand.
    • Language barrier: the Vedic texts were in Sanskrit, beyond the reach of ordinary people, whereas the Buddha preached in Pali, the people's language.
    • Intellectual ferment: Upanishadic thinkers had already questioned rituals and debated karma, rebirth and liberation, creating a climate for new teachers.

    2. Social causes

    • Rigid varna order: Brahmanas claimed privileges and exemptions, and Kshatriyas resented their claim to supremacy; both the Buddha and Mahavira were Kshatriyas.
    • Rising Vaishyas: merchants who had grown rich wanted a social status matching their wealth, which the varna order denied them.
    • An open order: the Sangha admitted people regardless of birth, and later women too, beginning with Mahaprajapati Gotami.

    3. Economic causes

    • Iron and the plough: iron tools cleared the Gangetic forests and plough agriculture made cattle precious; sacrificial killing of cattle clashed with farmers' needs, so ahimsa appealed to them.
    • Towns, trade and money: punch-marked coins and towns such as Rajagriha, Shravasti and Kaushambi created rich merchants (setthis such as Anathapindika), who disliked the Dharmasutras' disapproval of money-lending and patronised the new faith.
    • Reaction to new wealth: growing luxury produced a longing for a simple, austere life, which the monastic ideal offered.

    4. Political causes

    • Rise of Mahajanapadas: large kingdoms such as Magadha and Kosala emerged; kings like Bimbisara, Ajatashatru and Prasenajit patronised the Buddha.
    • Republican traditions: the Buddha came from the Sakya gana, and the Sangha's democratic procedures echoed the practices of the ganas.

    Critical view: Buddhism was not merely a revolt. It shared ideas of karma and rebirth with the Upanishads, and its success owed as much to the Buddha's personality, the simple Middle Path and the organised Sangha as to social discontent.

    AP angle: Buddhism took deep roots in Andhra at Amaravati, Nagarjunakonda, Bhattiprolu, Salihundam, Thotlakonda and Sankaram, and Acharya Nagarjuna developed the Madhyamaka philosophy here.

    Buddhism was thus the product of a changing society: it offered an ethical and egalitarian way of life to farmers, merchants and the lower orders, and its message of non-violence remains relevant today.

Satavahanas, Sangam age and Guptas; foreign travellers · asked 1 time

  1. 2023 · Paper II · Q1(b) · 10 marks

    Examine the socio-religious life and administration of early India with respect to the writings of Chinese travellers such as Fa-Hien, Hiuen-Tsang and I-Tsang.

    Model answer · free sample

    Chinese pilgrimsFa-Hien and Hiuen-TsangGupta and Harsha administrationBuddhist bias

    Chinese Buddhist pilgrims came to India to visit the sacred places of the Buddha and to collect texts. Fa-Hien's Fo-kuo-ki (early 5th century), Hiuen-Tsang's Si-yu-ki (7th century) and I-Tsing's record of Buddhist practice (late 7th century) are dated, first-hand outsider sources on Gupta and post-Gupta India, though coloured by their Buddhist outlook.

    1. Socio-religious life

    • Diet and customs (Fa-Hien): the people of Madhyadesha were prosperous; most avoided meat, liquor, onion and garlic, no pigs or fowls were kept, and cowries served as money in markets.
    • Untouchability: Fa-Hien saw Chandalas living outside towns and striking a piece of wood to announce their approach; Hiuen-Tsang found butchers, fishermen and scavengers living outside settlements, showing a hardening of caste.
    • Varna and learning (Hiuen-Tsang): he describes the four varnas and Brahmana vegetarianism, and Nalanda as a great centre with thousands of students, where he himself studied under Silabhadra.
    • Religion: Fa-Hien found Buddhism flourishing alongside Brahmanism; by Hiuen-Tsang's time Buddhism was declining in many regions. Harsha's quinquennial assembly at Prayag honoured the Buddha, Surya and Shiva.
    • Monastic life (I-Tsing): after about ten years at Nalanda, he described the vinaya discipline, the curriculum, Sanskrit grammar, medicine, diet and the hygiene of monks.

    2. Administration

    • Gupta mildness (Fa-Hien): no registration of households; crimes were punished mostly by fines, with the cutting off of the right hand only for repeated rebellion; free hospitals and rest-houses existed at Pataliputra.
    • Harsha's revenue (Hiuen-Tsang): taxation was light, about one-sixth of the produce; revenue from crown lands was divided into four parts: for state affairs, for public servants, for scholars and for religious gifts.
    • Officials and labour: officials were paid by assignments of land, and forced labour was limited and paid.
    • Law and order: punishments were harsher than in Gupta times (mutilation and ordeals by water, fire, weighing and poison), and roads were unsafe; Hiuen-Tsang himself was robbed.

    AP angle: Hiuen-Tsang visited Andhra (around Vengi) and Dhanakataka (the Amaravati region) and found many Buddhist monasteries, most of them deserted, evidence of Buddhism's decline in its old Andhra heartland.

    3. Value and limitations

    • Value: precise dates and first-hand detail fill gaps in Indian texts; Hiuen-Tsang's descriptions later helped archaeologists identify Buddhist sites.
    • Limitations: a Buddhist bias, little political history, generalisations from short stays, and problems with Indian languages and names.

    Read with inscriptions and Bana's Harshacharita, these accounts remain indispensable for reconstructing the society and governance of early India.
