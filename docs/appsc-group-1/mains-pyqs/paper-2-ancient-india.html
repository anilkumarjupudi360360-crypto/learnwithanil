Stone Age and the Indus Valley (Harappan) Civilization · asked 3 times

  1. 2025 · Paper II · Q1(a) · 10 marks

    Discuss the origin and expansion of the Indus Valley Civilization.

    Model answer · free sample

    The Indus Valley or Harappan Civilization (mature phase c. 2600–1900 BCE) was India's first urban culture. It came to light when Daya Ram Sahni excavated Harappa (1921) and R.D. Banerji Mohenjo-daro (1922).

    Origin: two views

    • Diffusionist view: Mortimer Wheeler and Gordon Childe traced the idea of city life to Mesopotamia.
    • Indigenous evolution (now accepted): excavations at Mehrgarh in Baluchistan (J.-F. Jarrige; from c. 7000 BCE) show unbroken growth from Neolithic farming villages to the Early Harappan phase (c. 3200–2600 BCE) at Amri, Kot Diji, Kalibangan and Rehman Dheri, and then to the mature cities.
    • Its script, seals and grid planning are unique, and the Rakhigarhi ancient-DNA study (2019) traced Harappan ancestry largely to local populations.

    Expansion

    • Spread over about 1.3 million sq km — from Manda (Jammu) in the north to Daimabad (Maharashtra) in the south, and from Sutkagen-dor (Makran coast) in the west to Alamgirpur (western UP) in the east; Shortughai in Afghanistan was a trading outpost.
    • Settlements followed the Indus and Ghaggar-Hakra rivers (Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, Ganweriwala, Rakhigarhi, Kalibangan, Banawali) and the Gujarat coast (Lothal, Dholavira, Surkotada).
    • Drivers: fertile floodplains, trade with Mesopotamia ("Meluhha") and raw materials — Khetri copper, Badakhshan lapis lazuli, Gujarat shell and carnelian.
    • After c. 1900 BCE the Late Harappan phase shifted east into the Ganga–Yamuna doab and south into Gujarat and Maharashtra.

    Andhra lay outside the Harappan zone; it was then home to Neolithic farming and cattle-herding communities of the southern Deccan.

    The civilization was thus an indigenous growth that expanded along rivers and trade networks; Rakhigarhi and Dholavira (UNESCO World Heritage Site, 2021) keep refining this picture.

  2. 2023 · Paper II · Q1(a) · 10 marks

    Examine the survival and continuity of Harappan culture in the present times.

    Model answer · free sample

    The Harappan Civilization (mature phase c. 2600–1900 BCE) declined, but it did not vanish. Through Late Harappan and Chalcolithic cultures, many of its material practices and religious ideas passed into later Indian life and survive even today.

    Material life and technology

    • Grid-planned towns, covered drains and burnt bricks in a standard 1:2:4 ratio anticipate modern urban planning and sanitation.
    • Weights followed a binary system (1, 2, 4, 8, 16…); the 16-anna rupee survived until decimal coinage in 1957.
    • Bullock carts and boats seen in terracotta models are still used in Sindh, Rajasthan and Gujarat.
    • The criss-cross furrowed field at Kalibangan resembles the mixed cropping still practised in Rajasthan; cotton cultivation, carnelian bead-making (Khambhat) and black-on-red pottery continue.

    Religion and social customs

    • Mother Goddess figurines anticipate Shakti worship; the 'Pashupati' seal in a yogic posture is read as proto-Shiva; linga-yoni stones, the sacred pipal and the humped bull remain objects of worship.
    • The swastika, fire altars (Kalibangan, Lothal) and ritual bathing (Great Bath) echo Vedic-Hindu rituals and temple tanks.
    • Bangles (the bronze 'dancing girl'), red pigment in the hair-parting of figurines at Nausharo (like sindoor), dice games, toys and courtyard houses persist.

    Andhra angle

    The mother-goddess cult lives on in AP's village goddesses (gramadevatas) such as Gangamma and Poleramma, and bullock carts and courtyard houses remain common in rural Andhra.

    Caveat

    As the script is undeciphered, religious continuities are inferences drawn from archaeology, not certainties.

    Thus, Harappan culture forms the substratum of Indian civilization, a striking example of continuity amid change.

  3. 2020 · Paper II · Q1(a) · 10 marks

    Critically explain the salient features of urban planning of Harappan Civilization.

    Model answer · free sample

    The Harappan Civilization (mature phase c. 2600–1900 BCE) was India's first urban culture. Harappa (excavated by Daya Ram Sahni, 1921), Mohenjo-daro (R.D. Banerji, 1922), Kalibangan, Lothal, Dholavira and Rakhigarhi show a remarkable degree of civic planning.

    Salient features

    • Grid layout: main streets ran north–south and east–west, crossing at right angles and dividing the town into rectangular blocks.
    • Two-part division: a raised, fortified citadel on the west for public buildings and a larger lower town on the east for residences; Dholavira had a three-fold division.
    • Standardised burnt bricks in the ratio 1:2:4; houses with courtyards, wells and bathrooms, with doors opening into side lanes rather than main streets.
    • Covered drainage: house drains joined brick-lined street drains with manholes and soak pits, among the most advanced of the Bronze Age world.
    • Public structures: the Great Bath (about 12 m × 7 m × 2.4 m) at Mohenjo-daro, granaries at Harappa, the dockyard at Lothal and the stone reservoirs of Dholavira.

    A critical view

    • Uniformity is often exaggerated: Lothal had a single enclosing wall, Kalibangan fortified both parts, and Banawali's streets did not follow a strict grid.
    • The functions of several structures, such as the "granary" and the "dockyard", are still debated.
    • In the late phase, planning declined at Mohenjo-daro: houses encroached on streets and building quality fell.
    • No palaces or temples have been found, so who planned and enforced civic rules remains unknown.

    Dholavira's inscription as a UNESCO World Heritage Site (2021) shows the global value of this legacy. Its zoning, drainage and water harvesting still offer lessons for planned cities such as Amaravati.

Vedic age, Buddhism and Jainism · asked 2 times

  1. 2025 · Paper II · Q1(b) · 10 marks

    Briefly analyze the teachings of Buddha and Mahavira.

    Model answer · free sample

    In the 6th–5th centuries BCE, Gautama Buddha and Vardhamana Mahavira (the 24th Jain Tirthankara) led the shramana reaction in the Gangetic valley against costly Vedic sacrifices, ritualism and Brahmanical dominance.

    Teachings of Buddha

    • Four Noble Truths: life is full of suffering (dukkha); desire (tanha) is its cause; suffering can end; the way is the Eightfold Path.
    • Ashtangika Marga: right view, intention, speech, action, livelihood, effort, mindfulness and concentration — a Middle Path between luxury and harsh austerity.
    • Philosophy: dependent origination (pratityasamutpada), impermanence (anicca) and no permanent soul (anatta); karma and rebirth; the goal is nirvana.
    • Social message: silent on God; rejected Vedic authority, sacrifices and caste; stressed ahimsa, moral conduct and a sangha open to all, including women.

    Teachings of Mahavira

    • Triratna: right faith, right knowledge and right conduct lead to liberation (kaivalya).
    • Five vows (mahavratas): ahimsa, satya, asteya and aparigraha of Parshvanatha, with brahmacharya added by Mahavira.
    • Everything, even stones and water, has a soul (jiva); hence extreme non-violence, fasting and austerity.
    • Anekantavada and syadvada: truth has many sides and can be stated only conditionally.
    • Rejected the Vedas and a creator God, but did not attack varna as sharply as Buddhism.

    Analysis

    Both preached in people's languages (Pali, Ardhamagadhi), rejected ritualism and offered salvation through conduct. Buddhism's Middle Path and missionary sangha made it a world religion; Jainism's rigour kept it smaller but enduring among traders.

    In Andhra, Buddhism flourished at Amaravati, Nagarjunakonda and Bhattiprolu, and Acharya Nagarjuna developed the Madhyamika philosophy; Jain remains survive at Ramatheertham (Vizianagaram) and Danavulapadu (YSR Kadapa).

    Their message of non-violence, tolerance and ethical living inspired Ashoka and Gandhi and remains relevant today.

  2. 2020 · Paper II · Q1(b) · 10 marks

    Critically explain the causes of the emergence of Buddhism in ancient India.

    Model answer · free sample

    Buddhism arose in the 6th–5th century BCE in the middle Gangetic plain, founded by Gautama Buddha of the Sakya clan. It was one of several heterodox sects (Jainism, Ajivikas, Charvakas) that challenged Vedic orthodoxy.

    Religious causes

    • Later Vedic religion had become ritualistic; costly yajnas and animal sacrifices were controlled by priests, and Sanskrit texts were beyond the reach of common people.
    • Upanishadic thinkers had already questioned rituals, creating a climate for new ideas on karma and liberation.

    Social causes

    • The rigid varna order gave Brahmanas privileges; Kshatriyas resented their claims to supremacy (both Buddha and Mahavira were Kshatriyas).
    • Vaishyas, newly rich from trade, and Shudras sought a faith that did not rank people by birth.

    Economic causes

    • Iron tools and plough agriculture in the Gangetic plain made cattle precious; sacrificial killing of cattle clashed with farmers' needs, so ahimsa appealed to them.
    • Growth of towns, trade and punch-marked coins created rich merchants (setthis such as Anathapindika), who disliked the Dharmasutras' disapproval of money-lending and patronised the new faith.

    Political causes

    • The rise of Mahajanapadas such as Magadha and Kosala; kings like Bimbisara, Ajatashatru and Prasenajit patronised the Buddha.

    Critical view: Buddhism was not merely a revolt. It shared ideas of karma and rebirth with the Upanishads, and its success owed much to its simple Middle Path, preaching in Pali (the people's language) and the organised Sangha.

    AP angle: Buddhism took deep roots in Andhra, at Amaravati, Nagarjunakonda, Bhattiprolu, Salihundam and Thotlakonda; Acharya Nagarjuna developed the Madhyamaka philosophy here.

    Buddhism was thus the product of a changing society, offering an ethical and egalitarian way of life that remains relevant today.

Satavahanas, Sangam age and Guptas; foreign travellers · asked 1 time

  1. 2023 · Paper II · Q1(b) · 10 marks

    Examine the socio-religious life and administration of early India with respect to the writings of Chinese travellers such as Fa-Hien, Hiuen-Tsang and I-Tsang.

    Model answer · free sample

    Chinese Buddhist pilgrims came to India to visit sacred places and collect texts. Their accounts, Fa-Hien's Fo-kuo-ki, Hiuen-Tsang's Si-yu-ki and I-Tsing's record of Buddhist practice, are valuable dated outsider sources, though coloured by their Buddhist outlook.

    Fa-Hien (early 5th century CE, reign of Chandragupta II)

    • Society: people of Madhyadesha prosperous; most avoided meat, liquor, onion and garlic; Chandalas lived outside towns and struck a piece of wood to announce their approach.
    • Administration: mild government; no registration of households; crimes punished mostly by fines, mutilation only for repeated rebellion; free hospitals and rest-houses at Pataliputra; Buddhism flourishing alongside Brahmanism.

    Hiuen-Tsang (c. 630–644 CE, reign of Harsha)

    • Society: four varnas; untouchables lived outside settlements; vegetarianism among Brahmanas; Nalanda flourished with thousands of students, but Buddhism was declining in many regions.
    • Administration: light taxation, about one-sixth of produce; officials paid by land assignments; forced labour limited and paid; punishments harsh (mutilation, ordeals); roads unsafe, as he himself was robbed; Harsha's Kanauj assembly and quinquennial gathering at Prayag showed patronage to all sects.
    • Andhra: he visited Dhanakataka (Amaravati region) and Vengi and found many Buddhist monasteries deserted, showing Buddhism's decline there.

    I-Tsing (late 7th century CE)

    • Studied about ten years at Nalanda; described monastic discipline (vinaya), the curriculum, Sanskrit grammar studies, medicine, diet and hygiene of monks.

    Assessment

    They give rare first-hand pictures of society and governance, but ignore political history, over-emphasise Buddhism and generalise from short stays.

    Read with inscriptions and Indian texts, these accounts remain indispensable for reconstructing early Indian society.
