How the Constitution was made and its main features · asked 2 times

  1. 2020 · Paper III · Q1(a) · 10 marks

    How was the Constituent Assembly formed that framed the Indian constitution? Critically evaluate the Constitution of India as framed by the Constituent Assembly.

    Model answer · free sample

    Constituent AssemblyCabinet Mission PlanBag of borrowingsConstitutionalism

    The idea of a Constituent Assembly was first put forward by M.N. Roy (1934); the Congress officially demanded one in 1935, and the British accepted the demand in principle in the August Offer (1940). The Assembly was finally constituted in November 1946 under the Cabinet Mission Plan. In just under three years it framed the Constitution, adopted on 26 November 1949, whose merits and flaws have been debated ever since.

    1. Formation of the Constituent Assembly

    • Strength: a total of 389 seats: 296 for British India (292 from the eleven governors' provinces and 4 from the chief commissioners' provinces) and 93 for the princely states.
    • Allocation: seats were shared among the provinces and princely states roughly in proportion to population (about one seat per ten lakh people) and divided among the General, Muslim and Sikh communities.
    • Indirect election: members of the provincial assemblies elected the representatives by proportional representation through the single transferable vote in July–August 1946. The Congress won 208 seats, the Muslim League 73 and small groups and independents 15; princely-state members were to be nominated by the rulers.
    • Working: it first met on 9 December 1946 (the League boycotted it), elected Dr Rajendra Prasad President and set up the Drafting Committee under Dr B.R. Ambedkar on 29 August 1947. After Partition its strength fell to 299.
    • Andhra members: Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar (Drafting Committee), N.G. Ranga, Durgabai Deshmukh and Pattabhi Sitaramayya.

    2. Strengths of the Constitution as framed

    • Democratic content: universal adult franchise from the very first general election, a bold step for a poor and largely non-literate new nation.
    • Rights with remedies: justiciable Fundamental Rights backed by Article 32, which Ambedkar called the "heart and soul" of the Constitution, and Directive Principles to guide a welfare state.
    • Social revolution: abolition of untouchability (Art 17) and safeguards for SCs, STs and minorities; Granville Austin called it "first and foremost a social document".
    • Balanced institutions: parliamentary government, an independent judiciary and a federation with a strong Centre suited to a newly partitioned country.

    3. Criticisms and the counter-view

    These criticisms are only partly valid. Decisions were taken by consensus and accommodation rather than by majority votes, and the drafts were openly debated clause by clause.

    In conclusion, the Assembly's legitimacy rests less on how it was elected than on what it produced: a democratic, rights-based Constitution that has endured for over 75 years. As Ambedkar warned, its success depends on those who work it, that is, on constitutional morality.

  2. 2023 · Paper III · Q1(a) · 10 marks

    Examine the scope of 'reasonable restrictions' and the 'test of reasonableness' in connection with Article 19 of the Indian Constitution.

    Model answer · free sample

    Article 19Reasonable restrictionsTest of reasonablenessProportionality

    Article 19(1) guarantees six freedoms to citizens: speech and expression, peaceful assembly, association, movement, residence, and profession, trade or business (the right to property was deleted by the 44th Amendment, 1978). These freedoms are not absolute. Clauses (2) to (6) allow the State to impose "reasonable restrictions" by law on specified grounds, and the courts, applying the test of reasonableness, decide whether a restriction is valid. The aim is to balance individual liberty with social control.

    1. Scope of reasonable restrictions

    • Only on listed grounds: each freedom has its own grounds (see table). In Kaushal Kishore (2023) a Constitution Bench held the grounds in Article 19(2) to be exhaustive, so no new ground can be added by invoking other rights.
    • Only by law: a restriction must be imposed by a valid law, not by an executive instruction (Bijoe Emmanuel, 1986, the national anthem case).
    • Grounds widened by amendment: after Romesh Thappar (1950), the First Amendment (1951) added public order, friendly relations with foreign States and incitement to an offence, and inserted the word "reasonable"; the 16th Amendment (1963) added sovereignty and integrity of India.
    • Special situations: Article 33 lets Parliament restrict the rights of the armed forces, and Article 358 suspends Article 19 during an Emergency declared on the ground of war or external aggression.

    2. The test of reasonableness

    • No fixed standard (V.G. Row, 1952): the court weighs the nature of the right, the purpose of the restriction, the extent and urgency of the evil, proportionality and the prevailing conditions.
    • Not arbitrary or excessive (Chintaman Rao, 1950): a restriction that goes beyond what its object requires is unreasonable.
    • Substance and procedure: both the law and the procedure it lays down must be reasonable; Maneka Gandhi (1978) read Articles 14, 19 and 21 together.
    • Clarity: Shreya Singhal (2015) struck down Section 66A of the IT Act as vague and overbroad, separating discussion and advocacy, which are protected, from incitement.

    3. Proportionality: the modern test

    • Privacy: Puttaswamy (2017) adopted the test for the right to privacy, and the Aadhaar judgment (2018) applied it to uphold the scheme with limits.
    • Digital age: Anuradha Bhasin (2020) applied proportionality to internet shutdowns: they must be temporary, necessary, published and periodically reviewed.

    AP angle: in the protective spirit of Article 19(5), the AP Scheduled Areas Land Transfer Regulation (1 of 1959, amended 1970) bars the transfer of land in Scheduled Areas to non-tribals; in Samatha (1997) the Supreme Court held leases of such land to private mining firms void.

    Reasonableness is thus a judicially tested balance: freedom remains the rule and restriction the exception, and proportionality ensures that the State uses the least intrusive means to protect the public interest.

Emergency powers and changing (amending) the Constitution · asked 1 time

  1. 2025 · Paper III · Q1(a) · 10 marks

    What are the emergency functions of the Union Government of India? Describe how they impact the legislative powers of Parliament and State Legislatures.

    Model answer · free sample

    Emergency provisionsArticle 352Article 356Federal to unitary shift

    Part XVIII of the Constitution (Articles 352–360), drawn largely from the Government of India Act 1935, arms the Union with three kinds of emergency powers. They rest on the Union's duty under Article 355 to protect every State against external aggression and internal disturbance. During an emergency the federal system can turn almost unitary without any formal amendment.

    1. Emergency functions of the Union

    • National Emergency (Art 352): proclaimed on war, external aggression or armed rebellion, only on the Union Cabinet's written advice (44th Amendment, 1978, which also replaced "internal disturbance"). It was proclaimed in 1962, 1971 and 1975.
    • President's Rule (Art 356): imposed when the constitutional machinery of a State fails, or when a State ignores Union directions (Art 365).
    • Financial Emergency (Art 360): when the financial stability or credit of India or any part of it is threatened; it has never been invoked.
    • Executive control: during a National Emergency the Union can direct a State on any matter (Art 353) and the President can modify the sharing of revenues (Art 354).

    2. Impact on the legislative powers of Parliament

    • State List opens up (Art 250): Parliament can make laws on any State subject; such laws lapse six months after the emergency ends.
    • Longer life of the House: Parliament can extend the Lok Sabha's term by one year at a time (Art 83(2)), but not beyond six months after the emergency ends.
    • Rights-restricting laws: Art 19 is suspended during war or external aggression (Art 358), so Parliament can make laws that would otherwise violate it; enforcement of other rights, except Art 20 and 21, may be suspended (Art 359).
    • Ordinances: the President can issue ordinances even on State subjects when Parliament is not in session.

    3. Impact on State Legislatures

    • Overridden, not suspended (Art 352): State Legislatures keep functioning, but their laws give way to Parliament's in case of conflict; an Assembly's term can also be extended (Art 172).
    • Suspended or dissolved (Art 356–357): under President's Rule, Parliament legislates for the State, passes its budget or delegates the power to the President; such laws continue until the State Legislature changes them.
    • Money Bills reserved (Art 360): in a Financial Emergency, State Money and Financial Bills may be reserved for the President.

    AP angle: Andhra Pradesh came under President's Rule in 1973, during the Jai Andhra agitation, and again in 2014 on the eve of bifurcation.

    Safeguards: the 44th Amendment let the Lok Sabha end a National Emergency, and S.R. Bommai (1994) made President's Rule subject to judicial review and the floor test.

    Emergency powers are a safety valve for national unity, but, as Dr Ambedkar hoped, Art 356 should remain a "dead letter"; the Sarkaria Commission's advice to use it only as a last resort keeps federalism intact.

Jobs and duties of the Union and State governments · asked 1 time

  1. 2025 · Paper III · Q1(b) · 10 marks

    The Indian Constitution favours a federal type of government. Considering this, what duties does the Constitution place on State Governments to ensure the smooth functioning of the nation?

    Model answer · free sample

    Cooperative federalismArticles 256–257Union directionsArticle 365

    The Constitution calls India a "Union of States" (Art 1). The Seventh Schedule gives States wide autonomy, yet the framers also placed definite constitutional duties on State Governments, so that 28 States act as parts of one nation rather than as rival units. Granville Austin called this blend of autonomy and obligation cooperative federalism; these duties are its legal backbone.

    1. Administrative duties towards the Union

    • Compliance with Union laws (Art 256): a State must use its executive power to ensure that laws made by Parliament are obeyed; the Union may give directions for this. States' police and revenue staff enforce central laws such as the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita and the National Food Security Act.
    • Not impeding the Union (Art 257(1)): State executive power must not obstruct the Union's executive power.
    • Communications and railways (Art 257(2)–(3)): when directed, States must maintain means of communication of national or military importance and protect railways in their territory; the Union pays any extra cost (Art 257(4)).
    • Election duty (Art 324(6)): the Governor must make staff available to the Election Commission, which is why State officials conduct Lok Sabha polls.

    2. Duties towards other States and the nation

    • Full faith and credit (Art 261): public acts, records and judicial proceedings of the Union and every State must be recognised throughout India.
    • Inter-State cooperation: States implement river-water tribunal awards (Art 262) and work through the Inter-State Council (Art 263, set up in 1990) and the Zonal Councils.
    • Fiscal discipline (Art 293): a State indebted to the Union needs its consent to borrow; States have Fiscal Responsibility laws, such as the AP FRBM Act 2005.
    • Shared decisions (Art 279A): States sit in the GST Council to keep one national market.

    3. Social and democratic duties

    • Directive Principles (Art 37): "fundamental in the governance" of the country, for example panchayats (Art 40), public health (Art 47) and environment (Art 48A).
    • Weaker sections: promote the interests of SCs and STs (Art 46) and follow Union directions on schemes for STs (Art 339(2)).
    • Local democracy (Parts IX and IXA): hold panchayat and municipal elections through a State Election Commission and set up a State Finance Commission every five years.

    In return, the Union must protect every State against external aggression and internal disturbance (Art 355).

    AP angle: Andhra Pradesh executes the Polavaram project, a national project under Section 90 of the AP Reorganisation Act 2014, on the Union's behalf; its State Disaster Management Authority works with the NDRF during cyclones, and it sits in the Southern Zonal Council.

    The Sarkaria (1988) and Punchhi (2010) Commissions stressed consultation over coercion: when States perform these duties willingly and the Union respects their autonomy, national unity and State autonomy reinforce each other.
