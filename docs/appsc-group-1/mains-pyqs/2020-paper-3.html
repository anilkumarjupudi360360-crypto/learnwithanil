  1. 2020 · Paper III · Q1(a) · 10 marks
    The Constitution and our rights · How the Constitution was made and its main features

    How was the Constituent Assembly formed that framed the Indian constitution? Critically evaluate the Constitution of India as framed by the Constituent Assembly.

    Model answer · free sample

    First proposed by M.N. Roy (1934) and accepted in principle in the August Offer (1940), the Constituent Assembly was constituted in November 1946 under the Cabinet Mission Plan.

    Formation

    • Total strength 389: 296 seats for British India (292 provinces and 4 chief commissioners' provinces) and 93 for princely states.
    • Seats were allotted roughly one per ten lakh population and shared among General, Muslim and Sikh communities.
    • Provincial assemblies elected members by single transferable vote (July–August 1946); princely-state members were to be nominated by the rulers.
    • It first met on 9 December 1946, elected Dr Rajendra Prasad President and set up the Drafting Committee under Dr B.R. Ambedkar (29 August 1947). After Partition its strength fell to 299. Andhra members included Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar (Drafting Committee), N.G. Ranga, Durgabai Deshmukh and Pattabhi Sitaramayya.

    Critical evaluation

    Critics argued that the Assembly was not directly elected on adult franchise and not sovereign, being a British creation; that it was dominated by the Congress and by lawyers (Ivor Jennings called the Constitution a "lawyers' paradise"); and that the Constitution was a "bag of borrowings", largely a copy of the Government of India Act, 1935, and un-Gandhian in neglecting village panchayats.

    These criticisms are only partly valid. The Congress was itself a broad coalition, non-Congress figures like Dr Ambedkar were included, decisions were taken by consensus and accommodation (Granville Austin), and borrowed provisions were adapted to Indian needs. The Constitution gave universal adult franchise from the start, justiciable Fundamental Rights, an independent judiciary and social justice (abolition of untouchability, Art 17).

    Its legitimacy rests less on how the Assembly was elected than on its democratic content and its durability for over 75 years.

  2. 2020 · Paper III · Q1(b) · 10 marks
    Courts and judicial review · Judicial review: courts checking laws and orders

    Define Judicial Review with special reference to the classification given by Justice Syed Shah Mohamed Quadri. Describe the importance and scope of Judicial Review.

    Model answer · free sample

    Judicial review is the power of the courts to examine the constitutionality of laws and executive orders of the Centre and the states and to declare them void if they violate the Constitution. Rooted in Marbury v. Madison (USA, 1803), it rests in India on Articles 13, 32, 226, 131–136 and 245–246, and is part of the basic structure (L. Chandra Kumar, 1997).

    Justice Syed Shah Mohamed Quadri's classification

    • Judicial review of constitutional amendments: Kesavananda Bharati (1973); the NJAC case (2015) struck down the 99th Amendment.
    • Judicial review of legislation of Parliament and State Legislatures and of subordinate legislation: Shreya Singhal (2015) struck down Section 66A of the IT Act.
    • Judicial review of administrative action of the Union, the states and authorities under the State: Maneka Gandhi (1978); S.R. Bommai (1994) on Article 356.

    Importance: It upholds the supremacy of the Constitution and the rule of law, protects Fundamental Rights, maintains the federal balance between the Centre and the states, and keeps checks and balances among the three organs.

    Scope: A law or order can be struck down if it infringes Fundamental Rights (Part III), is outside the competence of the authority that framed it, or violates any other constitutional provision. Even Ninth Schedule laws placed after 24 April 1973 are reviewable (I.R. Coelho, 2007). Courts, however, show restraint on policy and political questions.

    Andhra Pradesh example: In E.V. Chinnaiah v. State of AP (2004) the Supreme Court struck down AP's law sub-categorising Scheduled Castes; a seven-judge bench overruled it in State of Punjab v. Davinder Singh (2024), permitting sub-classification.

    Judicial review guards constitutionalism, but must respect the separation of powers.
