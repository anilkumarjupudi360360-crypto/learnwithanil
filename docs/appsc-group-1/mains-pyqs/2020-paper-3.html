  1. 2020 · Paper III · Q1(a) · 10 marks
    The Constitution and our rights · How the Constitution was made and its main features

    How was the Constituent Assembly formed that framed the Indian constitution? Critically evaluate the Constitution of India as framed by the Constituent Assembly.

    Model answer · free sample

    Constituent AssemblyCabinet Mission PlanBag of borrowingsConstitutionalism

    The idea of a Constituent Assembly was first put forward by M.N. Roy (1934); the Congress officially demanded one in 1935, and the British accepted the demand in principle in the August Offer (1940). The Assembly was finally constituted in November 1946 under the Cabinet Mission Plan. In just under three years it framed the Constitution, adopted on 26 November 1949, whose merits and flaws have been debated ever since.

    1. Formation of the Constituent Assembly

    • Strength: a total of 389 seats: 296 for British India (292 from the eleven governors' provinces and 4 from the chief commissioners' provinces) and 93 for the princely states.
    • Allocation: seats were shared among the provinces and princely states roughly in proportion to population (about one seat per ten lakh people) and divided among the General, Muslim and Sikh communities.
    • Indirect election: members of the provincial assemblies elected the representatives by proportional representation through the single transferable vote in July–August 1946. The Congress won 208 seats, the Muslim League 73 and small groups and independents 15; princely-state members were to be nominated by the rulers.
    • Working: it first met on 9 December 1946 (the League boycotted it), elected Dr Rajendra Prasad President and set up the Drafting Committee under Dr B.R. Ambedkar on 29 August 1947. After Partition its strength fell to 299.
    • Andhra members: Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar (Drafting Committee), N.G. Ranga, Durgabai Deshmukh and Pattabhi Sitaramayya.

    2. Strengths of the Constitution as framed

    • Democratic content: universal adult franchise from the very first general election, a bold step for a poor and largely non-literate new nation.
    • Rights with remedies: justiciable Fundamental Rights backed by Article 32, which Ambedkar called the "heart and soul" of the Constitution, and Directive Principles to guide a welfare state.
    • Social revolution: abolition of untouchability (Art 17) and safeguards for SCs, STs and minorities; Granville Austin called it "first and foremost a social document".
    • Balanced institutions: parliamentary government, an independent judiciary and a federation with a strong Centre suited to a newly partitioned country.

    3. Criticisms and the counter-view

    These criticisms are only partly valid. Decisions were taken by consensus and accommodation rather than by majority votes, and the drafts were openly debated clause by clause.

    In conclusion, the Assembly's legitimacy rests less on how it was elected than on what it produced: a democratic, rights-based Constitution that has endured for over 75 years. As Ambedkar warned, its success depends on those who work it, that is, on constitutional morality.

  2. 2020 · Paper III · Q1(b) · 10 marks
    Courts and judicial review · Judicial review: courts checking laws and orders

    Define Judicial Review with special reference to the classification given by Justice Syed Shah Mohamed Quadri. Describe the importance and scope of Judicial Review.

    Model answer · free sample

    Judicial reviewBasic structureArticle 13Separation of powers

    Judicial review is the power of the courts to examine the constitutionality of legislative enactments and executive orders of both the Centre and the States, and to declare them void if they violate the Constitution. Born in Marbury v. Madison (USA, 1803), it rests in India on Articles 13, 32 and 226 (with Arts 131–136 and 245–246). It is part of the basic structure of the Constitution (L. Chandra Kumar, 1997), so even Parliament cannot take it away.

    1. Justice S.S.M. Quadri's classification

    Justice Syed Shah Mohamed Quadri classified judicial review into three categories:

    • Constitutional amendments: Parliament's amending power is wide but not unlimited; amendments that destroy core features such as judicial independence or free elections can be struck down.
    • Legislation: laws are tested against Part III and the division of powers; even Ninth Schedule laws placed there after 24 April 1973 are reviewable (I.R. Coelho, 2007).
    • Administrative action: executive decisions are tested for illegality, irrationality, procedural impropriety and, increasingly, proportionality.

    2. Scope of judicial review

    • Three grounds: a law or order can be struck down if it infringes Fundamental Rights, lies outside the competence of the authority that framed it, or is repugnant to any other constitutional provision.
    • Judicial doctrines: severability, eclipse, pith and substance, harmonious construction and colourable legislation guide the courts.
    • Narrower than in the USA: Article 21 uses "procedure established by law", not American "due process"; Maneka Gandhi (1978) widened it by requiring procedure to be "fair, just and reasonable".
    • Limits: courts show restraint on economic policy (BALCO, 2002) and political questions.

    3. Importance of judicial review

    • Supremacy of the Constitution: every organ must act within constitutional limits, upholding the rule of law.
    • Guardian of rights: it makes Fundamental Rights enforceable against the State.
    • Federal balance: it settles Centre–State disputes (Art 131) and checked the misuse of Article 356 (S.R. Bommai, 1994).
    • Checks and balances: it prevents a legislative majority from overriding minority rights.

    AP angle: In E.V. Chinnaiah v. State of AP (2004) the Supreme Court struck down AP's law sub-categorising Scheduled Castes; a seven-judge bench overruled it in State of Punjab v. Davinder Singh (2024), permitting sub-classification.

    In conclusion, judicial review keeps every organ within constitutional limits; exercised with judicial restraint and respect for the separation of powers, it strengthens democracy rather than rivalling it.
