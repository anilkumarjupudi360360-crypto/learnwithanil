Why farming and factories grow slowly (and what is being done) · asked 3 times

  1. 2025 · Paper IV · Q1(b) · 10 marks

    Discuss how infrastructure bottlenecks hinder the growth of the Indian Manufacturing sector. What steps can be taken to address them ?

    Model answer · free sample

    ManufacturingInfrastructure bottlenecksLogistics costPM Gati Shakti

    Manufacturing contributes only about 17% of India's GVA, well short of the 25% target of the National Manufacturing Policy, 2011. Factories compete on cost and time, and both depend on infrastructure: power, transport, ports, land and water. Costly and unreliable infrastructure therefore acts as a hidden tax that keeps Indian firms small and less competitive than rivals in East and South-East Asia.

    1. How infrastructure bottlenecks hinder manufacturing

    • High logistics cost: estimated at 7.8–8.9% of GDP (NCAER–DPIIT, for 2021-22); freight is road-heavy and railways carry only about 27% of it, raising costs for bulk goods such as steel and cement.
    • Power: industrial tariffs carry cross-subsidies for farm and household users; DISCOM losses and interruptions push firms to costly captive power and diesel back-up.
    • Ports and last-mile links: congestion, weak hinterland rail and road links and slow multimodal transfer raise turnaround time and inventory costs, hurting time-sensitive exports.
    • Land: difficult acquisition, fragmented holdings and few plug-and-play industrial parks delay projects for years.
    • Other gaps: water scarcity, urban congestion, and weak testing, certification and R&D facilities for MSMEs.

    2. Impact on the sector

    • Small scale: high costs keep most firms small and informal, unable to reap economies of scale.
    • Weak exports: costs and delays limit India's integration into global value chains.
    • Low investment: uncertainty over land and power deters private capex and FDI in labour-intensive sectors such as textiles and footwear.
    • Lost jobs: labour-intensive factories need cheap logistics and reliable power; where these are lacking, investment goes to competitors such as Vietnam and Bangladesh, a lost opportunity for India's young workforce.

    3. Steps to address them

    • States' role: land and water are State subjects and power is distributed by State DISCOMs, so single-window clearances, land banks and DISCOM reforms are essential.
    • Regulatory ease: decriminalisation under the Jan Vishwas Act, 2023 and faster dispute resolution remove non-physical bottlenecks.

    AP angle: Andhra Pradesh hosts two of the new industrial smart cities, Orvakal and Kopparthy; the Visakhapatnam–Chennai and Chennai–Bengaluru industrial corridors and new ports at Ramayapatnam, Machilipatnam and Mulapeta aim to cut logistics costs for its industries.

    Cheap and reliable power, seamless multimodal logistics and ready industrial land, planned jointly by the Centre and the States, can lift manufacturing's share and make India a global manufacturing hub.

  2. 2020 · Paper IV · Q1(a) · 10 marks

    State some of the notable initiatives taken by the government recently to boost the growth of manufacturing sector in India.

    Model answer · free sample

    Make in IndiaPLI schemesEase of doing businessIndustrial corridors

    Manufacturing's share in India's GVA has stayed at roughly 15–17% for three decades, well below the 25% target of the National Manufacturing Policy, 2011. A weak factory sector means too few jobs for workers leaving farms and a large import bill for electronics and machinery. Since Make in India (September 2014), the government has combined a better investment climate, output-linked incentives and logistics reform to make India a manufacturing hub linked to global value chains.

    1. Improving the investment climate

    • Make in India (2014): a campaign covering 25 focus sectors; FDI was liberalised, with the automatic route allowed up to 100% in most sectors and up to 74% in defence (2020).
    • Corporate tax cut (2019): the rate fell to 22% for existing companies and 15% for new manufacturing units set up by March 2024, bringing India in line with competing Asian economies.
    • Regulatory reform: the National Single Window System (2021) for approvals, the Jan Vishwas Act, 2023 (minor offences decriminalised), and the four Labour Codes, in force from 21 November 2025, which replace 29 central labour laws.
    • Competitive federalism: State rankings under the Business Reform Action Plan push States to compete for investors.

    2. Incentives for scale and technology

    • Production Linked Incentive (PLI) schemes: 14 sectors with an outlay of ₹1.97 lakh crore, including mobiles, pharma, auto, batteries, solar modules, textiles and food. Because support is tied to actual sales, firms are rewarded only when they produce more in India.
    • Semiconductors and electronics: the India Semiconductor Mission (₹76,000 crore, 2021) for fabs and assembly-testing units, and the Electronics Component Manufacturing Scheme (2025) to deepen the supply chain.
    • National Manufacturing Mission (Union Budget 2025-26): policy support and a roadmap for small, medium and large industry.
    • PM MITRA parks (2021): seven integrated textile parks to give the textile chain scale from fibre to fashion.

    3. Infrastructure and logistics

    • PM Gati Shakti (2021): a GIS-based master plan that coordinates roads, railways, ports and gas lines; the National Logistics Policy (2022) aims to cut logistics costs.
    • Dedicated Freight Corridors move cargo faster between the hinterland and ports.
    • Industrial smart cities: 12 new ones under the National Industrial Corridor Programme (August 2024), with ready plots.

    4. Andhra Pradesh angle

    • Two of these 12 nodes, Orvakal (Kurnool) and Kopparthy (YSR Kadapa), are in AP.
    • The electronics cluster at Sri City, Kia's plant at Penukonda and the State's Industrial Development Policy 4.0 (2024-29) complement the national effort.

    The results are visible: smartphones are now among India's largest exports. Raising manufacturing's share further, for Viksit Bharat@2047, needs better skills, more R&D, lower logistics costs and MSMEs linked to global value chains.

  3. 2020 · Paper IV · Q1(b) · 10 marks

    Highlight some of the major factors leading to low agricultural productivity in India.

    Model answer · free sample

    Agricultural productivityFragmented holdingsRain-fed farmingInput and market constraints

    Agriculture employs about 46% of India's workers (PLFS 2023-24) but produces less than one-fifth of GVA, which shows low productivity per hectare and per worker. Yields of rice, wheat and pulses are well below those of China and the world leaders. The Green Revolution raised yields mainly in irrigated pockets, while large dryland areas lagged. The causes are structural, technological and institutional, and they reinforce one another.

    1. Structural factors

    • Small, fragmented holdings: the average holding is 1.08 ha, and 86% of holdings are small or marginal (Agriculture Census 2015-16). Tiny, scattered plots make tractors, harvesters and borewells uneconomical.
    • Population pressure: too many people depend on the land, so disguised unemployment keeps output per worker low.
    • Insecure tenancy: oral leases leave tenants without bank credit, crop insurance or input subsidies, and without any incentive to invest in land improvement.
    • Low capital formation: public investment in irrigation, drainage and rural infrastructure has lagged behind spending on input subsidies.

    2. Technological factors

    • Dependence on the monsoon: nearly half of the net sown area is rain-fed, so a delayed or weak monsoon lowers yields across the dryland belt.
    • Poor inputs: low seed replacement rates, and urea-biased fertiliser use (driven by the subsidy) that is far from the recommended 4:2:1 NPK balance and degrades soil health.
    • Weak research and extension: low spending on agricultural R&D and too few extension workers, so new varieties and practices spread slowly.
    • Low mechanisation on small farms, and little use of precision or protected cultivation.

    3. Institutional and market factors

    • Inadequate institutional credit: small and tenant farmers still depend on moneylenders at high interest.
    • Post-harvest losses and volatile prices: poor storage and cold chains, and price crashes that discourage investment in better inputs.
    • Climate change: heat waves, erratic rain and new pests reduce and destabilise yields.

    4. Andhra Pradesh angle

    The Godavari–Krishna deltas have some of India's highest paddy yields, but rain-fed Rayalaseema, especially drought-prone Anantapur, has low yields. Many delta farmers are tenants, so the AP Crop Cultivators Rights Act, 2019 gives them cards to obtain credit.

    Way forward: micro-irrigation (PMKSY, "more crop per drop"), Soil Health Cards, quality seeds, digital agriculture (AgriStack), FPOs and climate-resilient varieties can raise productivity sustainably, a second Green Revolution that is also ecologically sound.

Growth record, reforms and India's growth outlook · asked 1 time

  1. 2023 · Paper IV · Q1(a) · 10 marks

    Discuss the impact of 'New Age' reforms on the Indian Economy in the last eight years. Examine the scope of growth potential for India in this decade (2023 – 30).

    Model answer · free sample

    New Age reformsPublic goodsFormalisationPotential growth

    The Economic Survey 2022-23 used the term 'New Age' reforms for the changes made since 2014, which shifted policy from one-time liberalisation to building public goods, formalising the economy and trust-based governance. The Survey compared them with the 1998-2002 reforms that paid off in the 2003-08 boom, and argued that India's medium-term potential growth would rise as their benefits matured.

    1. Impact of the reforms

    • Digital public infrastructure: JAM (Jan Dhan-Aadhaar-Mobile), Direct Benefit Transfer and UPI (2016) cut leakages and brought millions into formal finance; UPI crossed 20 billion transactions a month in 2025.
    • Tax reform: GST (2017) unified the national market, and the 2019 cut in corporate tax to 22% (15% for new manufacturing units) made India competitive with its Asian peers.
    • Balance-sheet repair: the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (2016) and the 4R strategy (recognition, resolution, recapitalisation, reform) cut banks' gross NPAs from 11.2% (March 2018) to about 2.3% (March 2025), reviving credit growth.
    • Macro stability: flexible inflation targeting (2016) and a fiscal glide path helped India absorb COVID-19 and the 2022 commodity shock without a crisis.
    • Investment and ease of business: a public capex push, PM Gati Shakti (2021), PLI schemes (2020), the Jan Vishwas Act 2023 and four Labour Codes (in force from November 2025).

    2. Growth potential, 2023-30

    The Survey expected average real growth of about 6.5% in the medium term and said potential growth could rise to 7-8% as the reforms pay off. Outcomes so far beat this baseline: 7.4% in 2025-26 (NSO first advance estimate) and an RBI projection of 7.1% for 2026-27 (October 2026).

    • Demographic dividend: a young workforce, if skilled and employed, can lift savings, investment and consumption through the decade.
    • Infrastructure and logistics: record public capex (₹12.2 lakh crore in 2026-27) and Gati Shakti lower logistics costs and crowd in private investment.
    • Global shifts: China-plus-one supply chains, services exports and the digital economy widen the base of growth.
    • Constraints: weak private investment, low female labour force participation (41.7%, PLFS 2023-24), skill gaps, global protectionism and climate risks.

    AP angle: Andhra Pradesh's Swarna Andhra@2047 vision likewise counts on ports, industrial corridors, the Amaravati capital and technology-driven governance for high growth.

    Sustaining 7-8% growth needs second-generation reforms in land, labour, power and skills, and stronger state capacity: the path to Viksit Bharat@2047.

Trade gap, balance of payments and the falling rupee · asked 2 times

  1. 2025 · Paper IV · Q1(a) · 10 marks

    Analyse the factors contributing to India's Current Account Deficit (CAD) and unfavourable Balance of Payments (BoP) and discuss their implications for the Indian economy.

    Model answer · free sample

    Current Account DeficitBalance of PaymentsTrade deficitRupee depreciation

    The Balance of Payments (BoP) records all of India's transactions with the rest of the world. Its current account covers trade in goods and services, income and transfers; a Current Account Deficit (CAD) arises when payments exceed receipts and must be financed by capital inflows or by drawing down reserves. India's CAD was about 0.6% of GDP in 2025-26 (US$25.2 billion, RBI), but a much wider goods deficit and volatile capital flows kept the BoP under pressure.

    1. Factors contributing to the CAD

    • Large merchandise trade deficit: India imports over 85% of its crude oil, besides gold, electronics, coal and fertilisers; the goods deficit (BoP basis) widened to US$337.3 billion in 2025-26 from US$286.9 billion in 2024-25 (RBI).
    • Narrow export base: weak manufacturing competitiveness, a small role in global value chains and a trade deficit of about US$99 billion with China (2024-25).
    • External shocks: volatile crude prices (West Asia conflict, 2026) and US tariff actions in 2025 hurt exports such as shrimp, textiles and gems.
    • Primary income outflows: interest on external debt, and dividends and profits repatriated by foreign firms.

    2. Why the BoP turned unfavourable

    • Weak capital inflows: in 2025-26, net FPI outflows of US$16.4 billion and net FDI of only US$6.9 billion left the CAD unfinanced, so foreign-exchange reserves fell by US$23.6 billion on a BoP basis (RBI).
    • Rupee depreciation: the rupee breached ₹90 per US dollar in December 2025.
    • Cushions: a large services surplus (US$216.6 billion in 2025-26, from IT and Global Capability Centres) and record remittances (about US$135 billion in 2024-25) keep the CAD far smaller than the goods deficit.

    3. Implications for the Indian economy

    • Imported inflation: a weaker rupee and costlier crude raise fuel and input costs; rising inflation led the RBI to raise the repo rate to 5.50% in October 2026.
    • Reserves and liquidity: RBI's dollar sales to steady the rupee use up reserves and tighten domestic liquidity; external borrowing becomes costlier to service.
    • Sustainability: a CAD below about 2.5% of GDP is considered manageable; the 4.8% CAD of 2012-13 left India exposed in the 2013 taper tantrum, and the 1991 crisis followed a collapse of reserves.
    • Twin deficits: a high fiscal deficit adds to import demand and widens the CAD.

    AP angle: AP's merchandise exports were ₹1.59 lakh crore in 2025-26 (DGCI&S data), with marine products at 18%, so US tariffs on shrimp directly hurt the State's aquaculture farmers.

    Export diversification through FTAs (India–UK CETA, 2025; India–EFTA TEPA), PLI-led manufacturing and an energy transition that cuts oil imports can make India's external sector resilient.

  2. 2023 · Paper IV · Q1(b) · 10 marks

    Is the exchange rate of the Indian Rupee fully determined by the market ? Explain the effects of Rupee Depreciation on the Indian Economy. Discuss the role of the RBI in defending the falling rupee.

    Model answer · free sample

    Managed floatRupee depreciationImported inflationForex intervention

    India follows a managed float. After the dual-rate LERMS of March 1992, the rupee became market-determined in March 1993; the current account was made fully convertible in August 1994, while the capital account remains only partly convertible, as the Tarapore Committees (1997, 2006) advised a cautious path. So the market sets the rate, but the RBI smooths sharp moves.

    1. Is the rupee fully market-determined?

    • In principle, yes: there is no official target or band; the rate reflects trade flows, capital flows (FDI, FPI, ECBs), remittances and the interest-rate gap with the US.
    • In practice, managed: the RBI intervenes in spot, forward and offshore (NDF) markets to curb excessive volatility, though it does not defend any particular level.
    • Other levers: capital-account controls, ECB and FPI limits and large reserves (about $700 billion) also shape the rate. The RBI watches the 40-currency REER to judge competitiveness.
    • Verdict: market-determined in principle, managed in practice.

    2. Effects of depreciation (from about ₹60 per dollar in 2014 to beyond ₹90 in December 2025)

    • Costlier imports: India imports over 85% of its crude oil, besides edible oils, fertilisers and electronics, so imported inflation and subsidy bills rise.
    • Debt and capital: unhedged firms' foreign borrowings cost more to service, and FPI outflows can become self-reinforcing.
    • Gains: IT services, textiles, pharma and marine exports become more competitive, and remittances (India is the world's largest recipient) are worth more in rupees.
    • Limits: the high import content of exports such as electronics and gems, and the J-curve effect, delay any improvement in the trade balance.

    3. Role of the RBI in defending the rupee

    • Direct intervention: selling dollars from reserves in spot and forward markets, and buy-sell swaps to manage the resulting rupee liquidity.
    • Interest rates: the repo rate was raised by 250 bps in 2022-23 and again to 5.50% in October 2026; besides fighting inflation, this narrows the rate gap that drives outflows.
    • Attracting inflows: in 2013 a special FCNR(B) swap window drew about $34 billion; in July 2022 the RBI relaxed interest caps on FCNR(B) and NRE deposits, raised the ECB limit and eased FPI norms.
    • Less dollar dependence: rupee trade settlement through Special Rupee Vostro Accounts (2022) and a local-currency arrangement with the UAE (2023).

    AP angle: a weaker rupee helps Andhra Pradesh's shrimp and rice exporters but raises costs for Visakhapatnam's crude-importing refinery and pharma units that import raw materials.

    The RBI should smooth volatility, not defend a level; lasting rupee stability needs a lower current account deficit, stronger exports and steady capital inflows.
