Why farming and factories grow slowly (and what is being done) · asked 3 times

  1. 2025 · Paper IV · Q1(b) · 10 marks

    Discuss how infrastructure bottlenecks hinder the growth of the Indian Manufacturing sector. What steps can be taken to address them ?

    Model answer · free sample

    Manufacturing contributes only about 17% of India's GVA, well short of the 25% target of the National Manufacturing Policy, 2011. Costly and inadequate infrastructure is a key reason Indian factories struggle to scale up and compete globally.

    How infrastructure bottlenecks hinder manufacturing

    • High logistics cost: estimated at 7.8-8.9% of GDP (NCAER-DPIIT, for 2021-22); freight is road-heavy and railways carry only about 27% of it.
    • Power: industrial tariffs carry cross-subsidies for farm and household users; DISCOM losses and interruptions push firms to costly captive power.
    • Ports and last-mile links: congestion, weak hinterland connectivity and slow multimodal transfer raise turnaround time and inventory costs.
    • Land: difficult acquisition, fragmented holdings and few plug-and-play industrial parks delay projects.
    • Others: water scarcity, urban congestion, and weak testing and R&D facilities for MSMEs.

    Steps to address them

    • PM Gati Shakti National Master Plan (2021) and the National Logistics Policy (2022) for integrated, GIS-based planning and digitised logistics (ULIP).
    • Dedicated Freight Corridors, Bharatmala, Sagarmala and multimodal logistics parks to shift freight to rail and coastal shipping.
    • 12 new industrial smart cities under the National Industrial Corridor programme (August 2024), including Orvakal and Kopparthy in AP.
    • Sustained public capex (₹12.2 lakh crore in Union Budget 2026-27), power-distribution reforms (RDSS), PLI schemes and the National Manufacturing Mission (Budget 2025-26).

    AP angle: the Visakhapatnam-Chennai and Chennai-Bengaluru industrial corridors, with new ports at Ramayapatnam, Machilipatnam and Mulapeta, aim to cut logistics costs for AP's industries.

    Cheap, reliable power, seamless multimodal logistics and ready industrial land, planned jointly by the Centre and States, can make India a global manufacturing hub.

  2. 2020 · Paper IV · Q1(a) · 10 marks

    State some of the notable initiatives taken by the government recently to boost the growth of manufacturing sector in India.

    Model answer · free sample

    Manufacturing's share in India's GVA has stayed at roughly 15–17% for three decades, below the 25% target of the National Manufacturing Policy 2011. Since 2014 the government has tried to make India a global manufacturing hub.

    Investment climate

    • Make in India (September 2014): FDI liberalised, with 100% automatic route in most sectors and 74% in defence (2020).
    • Corporate tax cut (2019): 22% for existing firms and 15% for new manufacturing units set up by March 2024.
    • Regulatory reform: National Single Window System (2021), Jan Vishwas Act 2023 (minor offences decriminalised), and the four Labour Codes in force from 21 November 2025.

    Incentives for scale and technology

    • PLI schemes in 14 sectors (₹1.97 lakh crore): mobiles, pharma, auto, batteries, solar modules, textiles, food.
    • India Semiconductor Mission (₹76,000 crore, 2021) and the Electronics Component Manufacturing Scheme (2025).
    • National Manufacturing Mission (Union Budget 2025-26) for small, medium and large industry.

    Infrastructure and logistics

    • PM Gati Shakti (2021), National Logistics Policy (2022) and Dedicated Freight Corridors.
    • 12 new industrial smart cities under the National Industrial Corridor Programme (August 2024).

    Andhra Pradesh angle: Two of these 12 nodes, Orvakal (Kurnool) and Kopparthy (YSR Kadapa), are in AP. The electronics cluster at Sri City, Kia's plant at Penukonda and the State's Industrial Development Policy 4.0 (2024-29) complement the national effort.

    The results are visible: smartphones are now among India's largest exports. Lifting manufacturing's share further needs better skills, more R&D, lower logistics costs and MSMEs linked to global value chains.

  3. 2020 · Paper IV · Q1(b) · 10 marks

    Highlight some of the major factors leading to low agricultural productivity in India.

    Model answer · free sample

    Agriculture employs about 46% of India's workers (PLFS 2023-24) but produces less than one-fifth of GVA. Yields of rice, wheat and pulses are well below those of China and world leaders.

    Structural factors

    • Small, fragmented holdings: the average holding is 1.08 ha, and 86% of holdings are small or marginal (Agriculture Census 2015-16). This limits mechanisation and investment.
    • Disguised unemployment and low capital formation in agriculture.
    • Insecure tenancy: oral leases leave tenants without credit, insurance or the incentive to invest.

    Technological factors

    • Dependence on the monsoon: nearly half of the net sown area is rain-fed.
    • Poor inputs: low seed replacement rates, and urea-biased fertiliser use (driven by the subsidy) that degrades soil health.
    • Weak research and extension: low R&D spending, and slow adoption of new varieties and machines.

    Institutional and market factors

    • Inadequate institutional credit, so farmers depend on moneylenders.
    • Post-harvest losses, poor storage and volatile prices discourage investment.
    • Climate change: heat waves, erratic rain and pests.

    Andhra Pradesh angle: The Godavari–Krishna deltas have some of India's highest paddy yields. Rain-fed Rayalaseema, especially drought-prone Anantapur, has low yields. Many delta farmers are tenants, so the AP Crop Cultivators Rights Act, 2019 gives them cards to obtain credit.

    Way forward: micro-irrigation (PMKSY, "more crop per drop"), Soil Health Cards, quality seeds, digital agriculture (AgriStack), FPOs and climate-resilient varieties can raise productivity sustainably.

Growth record, reforms and India's growth outlook · asked 1 time

  1. 2023 · Paper IV · Q1(a) · 10 marks

    Discuss the impact of 'New Age' reforms on the Indian Economy in the last eight years. Examine the scope of growth potential for India in this decade (2023 – 30).

    Model answer · free sample

    The 'new age' reforms since 2014 moved from one-time liberalisation to building public goods, formalisation and trust-based governance. The Economic Survey 2022-23 compared them with the 1998-2002 reforms that paid off in the 2003-08 boom.

    Impact of the reforms

    • Digital public infrastructure: JAM, DBT and UPI (2016) cut leakages; UPI crossed 20 billion transactions a month in 2025.
    • Tax reform: GST (2017) unified the national market; corporate tax was cut to 22% (15% for new manufacturing units) in 2019.
    • Balance-sheet repair: the IBC (2016) and the 4R strategy cut banks' gross NPAs from 11.2% (March 2018) to about 2.3% (March 2025).
    • Macro stability: inflation targeting (2016) and a fiscal glide path helped India absorb COVID-19 and the 2022 commodity shock.
    • Investment: a public capex push, PM Gati Shakti (2021), PLI schemes (2020) and liberal FDI.
    • Ease of business: the Jan Vishwas Act 2023 and four Labour Codes (in force from November 2025).

    Growth potential, 2023-30

    The Survey expected average real growth of about 6.5% in the medium term and said potential growth could rise to 7-8% as the reforms pay off. Outcomes so far beat the baseline: 7.4% in 2025-26 (NSO first advance estimate) and an RBI projection of 7.1% for 2026-27 (October 2026).

    • Drivers: a young workforce, infrastructure and logistics, China-plus-one supply chains, services exports and the digital economy.
    • Constraints: weak private investment, low female labour force participation (41.7%, PLFS 2023-24), skill gaps, global protectionism and climate risks.

    Andhra Pradesh's Swarna Andhra@2047 vision likewise counts on ports, industrial corridors and the Amaravati capital for high growth.

    Sustaining 7-8% growth needs second-generation reforms in land, labour, power and skills, and stronger state capacity: the path to Viksit Bharat@2047.

Trade gap, balance of payments and the falling rupee · asked 2 times

  1. 2025 · Paper IV · Q1(a) · 10 marks

    Analyse the factors contributing to India's Current Account Deficit (CAD) and unfavourable Balance of Payments (BoP) and discuss their implications for the Indian economy.

    Model answer · free sample

    The Current Account Deficit (CAD) arises when India's payments for imports of goods, services, income and transfers exceed its receipts. As per RBI (June 2026), the CAD was US$25.2 billion (0.6% of GDP) in 2025-26, against US$22.9 billion (0.6%) in 2024-25.

    Factors contributing to the CAD and BoP pressure

    • Large merchandise trade deficit: over 85% dependence on imported crude oil, plus gold, electronics, coal and fertilisers.
    • Narrow export base: weak manufacturing competitiveness and a trade deficit of about US$99 billion with China (2024-25).
    • External shocks: volatile crude prices (West Asia conflict, 2026) and US tariff actions in 2025.
    • Income outflows: interest, dividends and profits.
    • Capital account stress: volatile FPI flows and weak net FDI widen the BoP gap and weaken the rupee, which breached ₹90 per US dollar in December 2025.

    A large services surplus (IT, Global Capability Centres) and record remittances (about US$135 billion in 2024-25) keep the CAD low.

    Implications

    • Imported inflation: a weaker rupee and costlier crude raise input costs; rising inflation led the RBI to raise the repo rate to 5.50% in October 2026.
    • Reserve drawdown and costlier external borrowing.
    • Sustainability: a CAD below about 2.5% of GDP is considered manageable, unlike 4.8% in 2012-13 (which exposed India in the 2013 taper tantrum) or the 1991 crisis.

    AP angle: AP's merchandise exports were ₹1.59 lakh crore in 2025-26 (DGCI&S data), with marine products at 18%, so tariff shocks on shrimp hurt AP's aquaculture farmers.

    Export diversification through FTAs (India-UK CETA, 2025; India-EFTA TEPA), PLI-led manufacturing and the energy transition can make India's external sector resilient.

  2. 2023 · Paper IV · Q1(b) · 10 marks

    Is the exchange rate of the Indian Rupee fully determined by the market ? Explain the effects of Rupee Depreciation on the Indian Economy. Discuss the role of the RBI in defending the falling rupee.

    Model answer · free sample

    India follows a managed float. Since March 1993 (after the dual-rate LERMS of 1992) the rupee is set by demand and supply in the forex market. The current account is fully convertible (1994); the capital account only partially.

    Is it fully market-determined? No. The RBI intervenes in spot, forward and offshore (NDF) markets to curb excessive volatility, though it targets no level. Capital controls, ECB and FPI limits and large reserves (about $700 billion) also shape the rate. It is market-determined in principle, managed in practice.

    Effects of depreciation (from about ₹60 per dollar in 2014 to beyond ₹90 in December 2025)

    • Costlier imports: India imports over 85% of its crude oil, plus edible oils, fertilisers and electronics, so imported inflation and subsidy bills rise.
    • Debt and capital: unhedged firms' foreign borrowings (ECBs) cost more to service, and FPI outflows can become self-reinforcing.
    • Gains: IT services, textiles and marine exports become more competitive, and remittances are worth more in rupees.
    • Limits: the high import content of exports and the J-curve delay any trade-balance gain.

    Role of the RBI

    • Selling dollars from reserves; forward and swap operations.
    • Raising the repo rate by 250 bps in 2022-23, and again to 5.50% in October 2026.
    • Attracting inflows (July 2022): higher interest caps on FCNR(B) and NRE deposits, a larger ECB limit, eased FPI norms.
    • Rupee trade settlement through Special Rupee Vostro Accounts (2022) and a local-currency arrangement with the UAE (2023).

    AP angle: a weaker rupee helps Andhra Pradesh's shrimp and rice exporters but raises costs for Visakhapatnam's crude-importing refinery and pharma units.

    The RBI should smooth volatility, not defend a level; lasting stability needs a lower current account deficit.
