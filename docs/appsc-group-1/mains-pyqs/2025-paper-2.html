  1. 2025 · Paper II · Q1(a) · 10 marks
    Ancient India · Stone Age and the Indus Valley (Harappan) Civilization

    Discuss the origin and expansion of the Indus Valley Civilization.

    Model answer · free sample

    Harappan urbanismMehrgarhIndigenous originSpatial expansion

    The Indus Valley or Harappan Civilization was South Asia's first urban culture, with a mature phase of about 2600–1900 BCE. It came to light when Daya Ram Sahni excavated Harappa (1921) and R.D. Banerji excavated Mohenjo-daro (1922). Planned cities, standard weights, seals and an undeciphered script show a highly organised society. Its origin and spread are best understood through its Early, Mature and Late Harappan phases.

    1. Origin: two views

    • Diffusionist view: Mortimer Wheeler and Gordon Childe argued that the idea of city life came from Mesopotamia, pointing to trade contacts and some shared traits.
    • Indigenous evolution (now accepted): excavations at Mehrgarh in Baluchistan (J.-F. Jarrige) show farming villages from about 7000 BCE, with wheat, barley, cattle and mud-brick houses, growing step by step towards urban life.
    • Early Harappan phase (c. 3200–2600 BCE): sites such as Amri, Kot Diji, Kalibangan and Rehman Dheri already had fortifications, wheel-made pottery and planned layouts, the direct ancestors of the mature cities.
    • Distinctiveness: the script, seals, grid planning and covered drains have no Mesopotamian model, and the Rakhigarhi ancient-DNA study (2019) traced Harappan ancestry largely to local populations.

    2. Expansion and extent

    • Area: the civilization covered about 1.3 million sq km, larger than ancient Egypt or Mesopotamia.
    • Core along rivers: major cities followed the Indus and the Ghaggar-Hakra — Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, Ganweriwala, Rakhigarhi, Kalibangan and Banawali.
    • Coastal Gujarat: Lothal (with its dockyard), Dholavira (with its water reservoirs) and Surkotada linked the civilization to sea trade.
    • Frontiers: Manda in the north, Daimabad in the south, Sutkagen-dor in the west and Alamgirpur in the east; Shortughai in Afghanistan was a trading outpost.

    3. Drivers of expansion

    • Fertile floodplains: farming of wheat and barley on river floodplains produced a surplus to feed cities, craftsmen and traders.
    • Trade and raw materials: copper from Khetri, lapis lazuli from Badakhshan, and shell and carnelian from Gujarat; Mesopotamian texts call the region "Meluhha".
    • Standardisation: bricks in the ratio 1:2:4, uniform weights and seals sustained a common economic system across distant regions.
    • Later shift: after about 1900 BCE, as rivers changed course and trade declined (the causes are debated), Late Harappan settlements moved east into the Ganga–Yamuna doab and south into Gujarat and Maharashtra.

    AP angle: Andhra lay outside the Harappan zone; it was then home to Neolithic farming and cattle-herding communities of the southern Deccan, with remains at sites such as Nagarjunakonda.

    The Harappan Civilization was thus an indigenous growth that expanded along rivers and trade routes. Ongoing work at Rakhigarhi and Dholavira (a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2021) keeps refining this picture.

  2. 2025 · Paper II · Q1(b) · 10 marks
    Ancient India · Vedic age, Buddhism and Jainism

    Briefly analyze the teachings of Buddha and Mahavira.

    Model answer · free sample

    Shramana traditionFour Noble TruthsTriratnaAhimsa

    In the 6th–5th centuries BCE, the Gangetic valley saw the growth of cities, trade and a shramana (ascetic) reaction against costly Vedic sacrifices, ritualism and Brahmanical dominance. Gautama Buddha and Vardhamana Mahavira, the 24th Jain Tirthankara, gave this protest its most lasting form. Both offered liberation through ethical conduct rather than ritual, and both preached in the people's languages, Pali and Ardhamagadhi.

    1. Teachings of Buddha

    • Four Noble Truths: life is full of suffering (dukkha); desire (tanha) is its cause; suffering can end; and the way to end it is the Eightfold Path.
    • Ashtangika Marga (Middle Path): right view, intention, speech, action, livelihood, effort, mindfulness and concentration, avoiding both luxury and harsh austerity.
    • Philosophy: dependent origination (pratityasamutpada), impermanence (anicca) and no permanent soul (anatta); karma decides rebirth, and the goal is nirvana.
    • Social message: silent on God; rejected Vedic authority, animal sacrifice and caste; opened the sangha to all, including women.

    2. Teachings of Mahavira

    • Triratna: right faith, right knowledge and right conduct lead to liberation (kaivalya) from the cycle of birth.
    • Five vows: ahimsa, satya, asteya and aparigraha came from Parshvanatha, and Mahavira added brahmacharya; monks keep them strictly as mahavratas, lay followers as milder anuvratas.
    • Soul in everything: even stones, plants and water have a soul (jiva); hence extreme non-violence, fasting and austerity.
    • Anekantavada and syadvada: truth has many sides and can be stated only conditionally, a philosophy of tolerance.
    • Attitude to authority: rejected the Vedas and a creator God, but criticised varna less sharply than Buddhism.

    3. Analysis: common ground and differences

    • Common ground: both were Kshatriya teachers who rejected ritualism, Vedic authority and priestly monopoly, and stressed karma, rebirth and moral conduct.
    • Differences: Buddha's Middle Path was practical, while Mahavira demanded rigorous austerity; Buddhism denied a permanent soul, Jainism saw souls everywhere.
    • Spread: Buddhism's missionary sangha and royal patronage (Ashoka) made it a world religion; Jainism's rigour kept it smaller but enduring among merchants.
    • Social appeal: both attracted the new merchant class, and their ethics of non-violence suited trade and settled farming.

    AP angle: Buddhism flourished at Amaravati, Nagarjunakonda and Bhattiprolu, and Acharya Nagarjuna developed the Madhyamika philosophy; Jain remains survive at Ramatheertham (Vizianagaram) and Danavulapadu (YSR Kadapa).

    Their message of ahimsa, tolerance and self-discipline inspired Ashoka's dhamma and Gandhi's satyagraha, and it remains relevant in an age of conflict and excessive consumption.
