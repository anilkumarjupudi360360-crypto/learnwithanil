General Studies Paper IV: Ethics, Integrity and Aptitude · Public Service Values and Probity.

UPSC Civil Services (Main) 2021 · GS Paper 4 · Q1(a)

Question · Q1(a) · 10 marks · 150 words

Identify five ethical traits on which one can plot the performance of a civil servant. Justify their inclusion in the matrix.

Syllabus: Public Service Values and Probity · Accountability and ethical governance
Model answer · free sample

Ethical performance matrixIntegrityImpartialityAccountability

A civil servant's performance cannot be judged by targets alone: how results are achieved matters as much as what is achieved. An ethical performance matrix plots an officer on a few observable traits and complements the output-focused APAR, which already asks the reporting officer to comment on integrity. The values written into the All India Services (Conduct) Rules in 2014 and the Second ARC's report on Ethics in Governance (2007) point to five such traits.

1. Five traits and why each belongs in the matrix

  • Integrity: honesty in money, decisions and words, and consistency between private belief and public action. It is the foundation: without it, every other strength, even competence, can be turned to private gain.
  • Impartiality and non-partisanship: decisions on merit and the same rules for all, whatever a person's caste, religion, wealth or political links. It gives effect to equality before the law (Article 14) and keeps citizens' trust when governments change.
  • Accountability and transparency: owning decisions, recording reasons and being open to audit, RTI and grievance review. It turns discretion into answerable power and deters misuse.
  • Empathy and compassion: sensitivity to the poor, the disabled, women and other weaker sections; the 2014 conduct rules expressly ask for responsiveness to the public, particularly the weaker sections. It makes rules humane and keeps the last person from being excluded.
  • Dedication to public service: commitment, diligence and moral courage, seen in timely delivery and in crises such as floods, elections and epidemics. It separates service from mere compliance.

2. Why a matrix rather than a single score

  • Traits balance one another: integrity without empathy becomes rigid; empathy without impartiality slides into favouritism; dedication without accountability can turn arbitrary.
  • Diagnosis, not only grading: plotting each trait separately shows where an officer needs training or mentoring, which fits Mission Karmayogi's competency-based approach (the Framework of Roles, Activities and Competencies, FRAC).
  • Evidence over impression: most indicators can be drawn from records, so the assessment depends less on one superior's opinion.

3. Safeguards in using it

  • Many sources: combine the reporting officer's view with peer and citizen feedback, as in 360-degree appraisal.
  • Fairness to the officer: entries must be communicated so that the officer can represent against them, as the Supreme Court held in Dev Dutt v Union of India (2008).
  • Reward courage: the matrix must credit officers who refuse illegal orders or take honest risks, not penalise them for friction with superiors.

Plotting performance on these five traits makes ethics measurable and moves the civil service from mere rule-compliance to values-based governance, in which the way a result is achieved counts as much as the result itself.
