UPSC Civil Services (Main) 2019 · GS Paper 1 · Q2

Question · Q2 · 10 marks · 150 words

The 1857 Uprising was the culmination of the recurrent big and small local rebellions that had occurred in the preceding hundred years of British rule. Elucidate.

Syllabus: Modern Indian History · Freedom struggle and its leaders
Model answer · free sample

Revolt of 1857Civil rebellionsTribal uprisingsSepoy mutinies

In the century between Plassey (1757) and 1857, British expansion provoked a long chain of resistance: civil rebellions of dispossessed rulers, zamindars and poligars; peasant and religious movements; tribal uprisings; and sepoy mutinies. The Revolt of 1857, which began at Meerut on 10 May 1857, gathered these scattered grievances into one great upheaval. It was their culmination, though it also went beyond them in scale and aim.

1. A century of local rebellions

  • Civil rebellions: the Poligar wars of the Tamil country (1799–1801), Velu Thampi's revolt in Travancore (1808–09), the Paika rebellion of Odisha (1817) and Kittur Chennamma's resistance (1824) were led by chiefs who had lost land and power.
  • Peasant and religious movements: the Sanyasi–Fakir rebellion in Bengal, the Faraizi movement of Haji Shariatullah and Dudu Miyan, the Wahabi movement and the Moplah outbreaks in Malabar mixed religious revival with anger against revenue, zamindars and moneylenders.
  • Tribal uprisings: the Kol (1831–32), Bhil and Khond risings and the Santhal hul (1855–56) of Sidhu and Kanhu resisted land alienation and outsiders (dikus).
  • Sepoy mutinies: Vellore (1806), over new dress rules seen as a threat to caste and religion, and Barrackpore (1824), over service in Burma, showed early discontent in the Company's army.

2. Why 1857 was their culmination

  • The same grievances, accumulated: heavy land revenue and evictions, ruined artisans, the Doctrine of Lapse (Satara, Jhansi, Nagpur), the annexation of Awadh (1856) and fear of religious interference had built up for decades.
  • The same social groups: dispossessed rulers (Nana Saheb, Rani Lakshmibai, Begum Hazrat Mahal), zamindars (Kunwar Singh) and peasants joined the sepoys, who were largely peasants in uniform from Awadh.
  • The same form: like earlier risings, 1857 was led by traditional elites and sought to restore an older order, symbolised by the Mughal emperor Bahadur Shah Zafar.
  • Traditions of defiance: earlier risings had exposed British vulnerability, and the Santhal hul had been crushed only in 1856.

3. How 1857 went beyond them

  • Scale and unity: sepoys and civilians rose together across Delhi, Awadh, Rohilkhand, Bundelkhand, Bihar and central India, and Hindus and Muslims fought side by side.
  • A political aim: proclaiming Bahadur Shah emperor of Hindustan challenged Company rule itself, not just a local grievance.
  • Old limitations persisted: much of the south, Bengal and Punjab stayed quiet, many princes and the educated middle class stayed loyal, and there was no unified plan.

The Revolt of 1857 was thus both the climax of traditional resistance and a turning point: it ended Company rule (Government of India Act, 1858) and left a memory of united struggle on which modern nationalism later built.
