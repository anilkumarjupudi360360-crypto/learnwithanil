UPSC Civil Services (Main) 2020 · GS Paper 3 · Q1

Question · Q1 · 10 marks · 150 words

Explain intra-generational and inter-generational issues of equity from the perspective of inclusive growth and sustainable development.

Syllabus: Indian Economy · Inclusive growth
Model answer · free sample

Intra-generational equityInter-generational equityInclusive growthSustainable development

Equity has two time dimensions. Intra-generational equity is fairness among people alive today, across classes, castes, genders and regions; it is the core of inclusive growth, which the Eleventh Plan (2007-12) made its goal. Inter-generational equity is fairness between the present and future generations. The Brundtland Report (1987) defined sustainable development as development that "meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs".

1. Intra-generational issues: the inclusive-growth lens

  • Unequal access to jobs: growth led by capital- and skill-intensive sectors leaves about 90% of workers informal, and women's labour force participation was only 41.7% (PLFS 2023-24).
  • Social and regional gaps: SCs, STs and backward districts lag in assets, learning and health; the Aspirational Districts (2018) and Aspirational Blocks (2023) programmes target these gaps.
  • Basic services: government health spending was only 1.84% of GDP and out-of-pocket spending 39.4% of health expenditure (National Health Accounts 2021-22), so illness pushes families into poverty.
  • Environmental injustice today: the poor bear most of the pollution, displacement by mines and dams, and climate shocks; the District Mineral Foundations (2015) and the Forest Rights Act (2006) try to share benefits with affected communities.

2. Inter-generational issues: the sustainability lens

  • Depleting natural capital: about 11% of groundwater assessment units are over-exploited, and Punjab extracts 156% of its annual recharge (CGWB, 2025); soils, forests and fisheries are also being run down.
  • Climate debt: today's emissions impose costs on future generations, which is why India has pledged net zero by 2070 and 500 GW of non-fossil capacity by 2030.
  • Fiscal burden: high public debt and unfunded liabilities shift costs to future taxpayers; the FRBM Act, 2003 seeks to limit this.
  • Human capital: 35.5% of children under five were stunted (NFHS-5); undernourished children today mean a less productive workforce tomorrow.

3. How the two are linked

  • Trade-offs: free farm power and cheap coal help poor households now but deplete aquifers and the climate budget for the future.
  • Synergies: watershed works, rooftop solar, natural farming and green jobs raise incomes today while protecting resources.
  • Global equity: the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities (Rio, 1992) links equity among nations to equity across time.
  • Better measurement: GDP ignores resource depletion; natural-capital accounts and the inequality-adjusted HDI show who gains and what is lost.

Inclusive growth without sustainability borrows from the future, and sustainability without inclusion is unjust today. Following the precautionary principle (Vellore, 1996) and the spirit of Mission LiFE, India must grow in ways that are fair to both.
