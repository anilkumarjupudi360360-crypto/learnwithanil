UPSC Civil Services (Main) 2023 · GS Paper 4 · Q1(b)

Question · Q1(b) · 10 marks · 150 words

‘International aid’ is an accepted form of helping ‘resource-challenged’ nations. Comment on ‘ethics in contemporary international aid’. Support your answer with suitable examples.

Syllabus: Public Service Values and Probity · Ethical concerns and dilemmas in government
Model answer · free sample

Official development assistanceConditionalityDignity of recipientsAid effectiveness

International aid, or official development assistance (ODA), is concessional finance, goods and expertise that donor countries and agencies give to poorer nations for development and humanitarian relief. The UN target is 0.7% of donors' GNI (1970). Its ethics turn on motive, means and outcome. OECD preliminary data show that aid fell by 23.1% in real terms in 2025, the largest annual drop on record, which makes these questions sharper.

1. Ethical basis of aid

  • Humanitarian duty: shared humanity obliges the well-off to prevent suffering when they can do so at modest cost, as Peter Singer argued in "Famine, Affluence, and Morality" (1972).
  • Global justice: colonial extraction and unequal trade rules give rich nations a historical responsibility; in climate finance, COP29 (Baku, 2024) set a goal of at least US$300 billion a year for developing countries by 2035.
  • Enlightened self-interest: pandemics, terrorism and forced migration cross borders, so aid that builds stability serves donors too.

2. Ethical concerns in contemporary aid

  • Strategic motives and conditionality: aid is often used to win votes, bases or markets; the structural adjustment conditions of the 1980s and 1990s were criticised for forcing cuts in health and education spending that hurt the poor.
  • Tied aid and debt: recipients may be required to buy from donor firms, or burdened with loans for projects of doubtful viability; Sri Lanka's Hambantota port, leased for 99 years to a Chinese company in 2017, became a symbol of "debt-trap" fears, though the label is debated.
  • Dependency and paternalism: donor-driven priorities can weaken local institutions and treat recipients as passive beneficiaries, not partners.
  • Misconduct and diversion: the Oxfam Haiti scandal (revealed in 2018) exposed sexual exploitation by aid workers, and corruption diverts aid from the needy.
  • Selfishness and unpredictability: rich countries pre-booked COVID-19 vaccines while COVAX struggled; in 2025 the US dismantled USAID, its aid fell by more than half, and all five largest donors cut aid, abruptly halting health and food programmes.

3. India's approach: development partnership

  • Demand-driven and without political conditions: India's South–South cooperation offers grants, lines of credit and training through ITEC (1964) on partners' priorities.
  • Examples: Vaccine Maitri (2021) supplied COVID-19 vaccines abroad; Operation Dost (Türkiye and Syria earthquake, 2023); about US$4 billion of support to Sri Lanka in its 2022 crisis; wheat and medicines to Afghanistan; Operation Brahma after the Myanmar earthquake (2025).
  • Self-scrutiny: partnership aid also serves strategic interests, so transparency and timely completion of projects matter.

4. Way forward

  • Aid effectiveness: the Paris Declaration principles of ownership, alignment, harmonisation, managing for results and mutual accountability.
  • Localisation and safeguarding: more funds through local organisations, codes against exploitation, and fair debt restructuring under the G20 Common Framework.

Ethical aid is a partnership, not charity: it respects the dignity and agency of recipients, keeps its promises and measures success by their development, not the donor's leverage.
