UPSC Civil Services (Main) 2020 · GS Paper IV

UPSC Mains 2020 GS 4 · Q12: model answer

General Studies Paper IV: Ethics, Integrity and Aptitude · Case Studies.

Q1220 marks250 words2020 · GS Paper IV

Migrant workers have always remained at the socio-economic margins of our society, silently serving as the instrumental labour force of urban economics. The pandemic has brought them into national focus. On announcement of a countrywide lockdown, a very large number of migrant workers decided to move back from their places of employment to their native villages. The non-availability of transport created its own problems. Added to this was the fear of starvation and inconvenience to their families. This caused, the migrant workers to demand wages and transport facilities for returning to their villages. Their mental agony was accentuated by multiple factors such as a sudden loss of livelihood, possibility of lack of food and inability to assist in harvesting their rabi crop due to not being able to reach home in time. Reports of inadequate response of some districts in providing the essential boarding and lodging arrangements along the way multiplied their fears. You have learnt many lessons from this situation when you were tasked to oversee the functioning of the District Disaster Relief Force in your district. In your opinion what ethical issues arose in the current migrant crisis? What do you understand by an ethical care giving state? What assistance can the civil society render to mitigate the sufferings of migrants in similar situations?

Ethics of careDignity of migrantsCaring stateCivil society

When a nationwide lockdown was announced on 24 March 2020, millions of migrant workers, without wages, food or shelter, set out for their villages, many on foot. In May 2020, sixteen workers sleeping on a railway track near Aurangabad were killed by a goods train. The Supreme Court took suo motu cognisance and directed that stranded migrants be charged no fare and be given food and water. Overseeing my district's relief force, I saw that the crisis was as much ethical as logistical.

1. Ethical issues in the migrant crisis

  • Dignity and the right to life: hunger, long walks and humiliation, such as migrants being sprayed with disinfectant in Bareilly, violated the dignity protected by Article 21.
  • Justice and equity: a measure meant to protect everyone placed the heaviest burden on those least able to bear it.
  • Failure of anticipation and empathy: plans assumed a settled population; the mobile, invisible migrant was an afterthought.
  • Public health versus liberty: containment justified limits on movement, but those limits created a duty to provide food, shelter and safe transport.
  • Duties of employers and landlords: unpaid wages and evictions shifted private obligations onto the workers and the state.
  • Coordination and trust: gaps between source and destination States, and rumours, deepened panic and distrust.

2. An ethical care-giving state

  • Meaning: a state that does not wait for citizens to claim entitlements but notices vulnerability and responds with timely, respectful help. It draws on the ethics of care (Carol Gilligan, 1982), which values relationships and responsiveness alongside abstract rights; Joan Tronto (1993) breaks care into four linked elements, shown below.
  • Constitutional basis: the Preamble's promise of justice; the Directive Principles on livelihood, public assistance in unemployment, and just and humane conditions of work (Articles 38, 39, 41 and 42); and the right to live with dignity under Article 21 (Francis Coralie Mullin, 1981).
  • In practice: free foodgrains under the PM Garib Kalyan Anna Yojana (2020), Shramik Special trains from 1 May 2020, the Garib Kalyan Rojgar Abhiyaan for returnees (June 2020), and later the e-Shram portal (2021; over 30 crore registrations), One Nation One Ration Card and affordable rental housing complexes.

3. How civil society can help

  • Partnership, not substitution: civil society reaches where government cannot, but works best when the district coordinates it through one control room and shares data.
  • Rebuilding lives: skill mapping of returnees, links to local work, and help for a safe return when jobs resume.

4. Lessons from my district

  • Be prepared: a migrant help desk and database, transit camps with separate spaces for women and children, coordination with source and destination districts, and trained volunteers within the relief force.

The pandemic showed that the dignity of migrants is the true test of a humane state. A caring state, working hand in hand with an active civil society, turns rights on paper into relief on the road.
