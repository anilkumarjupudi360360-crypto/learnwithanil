UPSC Civil Services (Main) 2025 · GS Paper 2 · Q2

Question · Q2 · 10 marks · 150 words

Comment on the need of administrative tribunals as compared to the court system. Assess the impact of the recent tribunal reforms through rationalization of tribunals made in 2021.

Syllabus: Constitution and Polity · Judiciary
Model answer · free sample

Articles 323A and 323BTribunalisationTribunals Reforms Act 2021National Tribunals Commission

Tribunals are specialised quasi-judicial bodies that decide disputes in fields such as service matters, tax, company law and the environment. The 42nd Amendment (1976), following the Swaran Singh Committee, inserted Articles 323A and 323B to let legislatures create them. The Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021 rationalised them but met repeated judicial objections, leading to a new law in 2026.

1. Why tribunals are needed alongside courts

  • Expertise: benches of judicial and technical members suit technical disputes, as in the National Green Tribunal (2010) or the NCLT and NCLAT under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016.
  • Speed and flexibility: they are not bound by the Code of Civil Procedure or strict rules of evidence, follow natural justice and can work to statutory time limits.
  • Relief to courts: moving service disputes to the Central Administrative Tribunal (1985) and tax appeals to the ITAT eases the load on High Courts.
  • Access and cost: less formal procedures and benches in many cities help litigants.
  • Supplements, not substitutes: L. Chandra Kumar (1997) made judicial review under Articles 226, 227 and 32 part of the basic structure, so tribunal orders can be challenged before a High Court.

2. Problems that prompted reform

  • Executive control: parent ministries fund, staff and appoint tribunals while also litigating before them.
  • Vacancies, short tenures and overlapping bodies delayed justice.
  • Earlier attempt: the Finance Act, 2017 merged tribunals (26 to 19), but Rojer Mathew (2019) struck down the rules framed under it.

3. The 2021 rationalisation and its impact

  • Abolition: bodies such as the Film Certification Appellate Tribunal, the Intellectual Property Appellate Board and the Airport Appellate Tribunal were dissolved, and their work moved to High Courts and civil or commercial courts.
  • Uniform conditions: a four-year term, a minimum age of 50, and search-cum-selection committees chaired by the CJI or his nominee, with two Union Secretaries.
  • Burden shifted: High Courts received specialised cases without new judges; the Delhi High Court created an Intellectual Property Division (2021).
  • Clash over independence: the Act re-enacted provisions the Court had struck down in July 2021. In Madras Bar Association (19 November 2025) the Court struck down its appointment and tenure provisions and ordered a National Tribunals Commission within four months.
  • Response: the Tribunals Reforms Act, 2026 (assent 13 August, in force from 25 August 2026) set up a five-member National Tribunals Commission to oversee appointments, review performance, handle complaints against members and maintain a National Tribunals Data Grid for 16 tribunals; members get five-year terms (chairpersons up to 70, members up to 67).

Rationalisation has made the system leaner, but its impact depends on independence: an autonomous Commission, judicial primacy in selection and adequate staff would let tribunals deliver speedy, expert justice without diluting judicial review.
