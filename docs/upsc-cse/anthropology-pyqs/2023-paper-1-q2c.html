Q2(c)15 marks2023 · Paper I

Elucidate the different forms of malnutrition. Describe protein-calorie malnutrition with suitable examples.

Syllabus: Human Growth, Nutrition and Demography · Nutrition and health

Model answer · free

MalnutritionTriple burdenProtein-energy malnutritionKwashiorkor and marasmus

The World Health Organization defines malnutrition as deficiencies, excesses or imbalances in a person's intake of energy and nutrients. It covers both under- and over-nutrition, and India, like many developing countries, now faces a triple burden of undernutrition, micronutrient deficiency and rising obesity. Anthropologists treat it as a biocultural condition shaped by poverty, food beliefs, gender and disease.

1. Forms of malnutrition

  • Undernutrition: stunting (low height-for-age, a sign of chronic deprivation), wasting (low weight-for-height, acute) and underweight (low weight-for-age). NFHS-6 (2023–24) puts stunting among children under five at 29.3% (35.5% in NFHS-5) and underweight at 31.8% (32.1%); severe wasting fell from 7.7% to 5.2%, but overall wasting has barely changed.
  • Micronutrient deficiency ("hidden hunger"): iron (anaemia), iodine (goitre, cretinism), vitamin A (night blindness), vitamin D (rickets), zinc and folate.
  • Overnutrition: overweight, obesity and diet-related non-communicable diseases such as diabetes and hypertension, rising with processed foods and sedentary work.
  • Secondary malnutrition: caused by illnesses such as diarrhoea, tuberculosis or worm infestation that reduce absorption or raise needs.

2. Protein-energy (protein-calorie) malnutrition

  • Meaning: PEM results from too little energy, protein or both, and mostly affects children under five. Gómez (1956) graded it by weight-for-age; the Wellcome classification (1970) combined weight-for-age with oedema; WHO now identifies severe acute malnutrition by weight-for-height below –3 SD, mid-upper arm circumference below 115 mm, or oedema of both feet.
  • Kwashiorkor: described by Cicely Williams in the Gold Coast (Ghana) in the 1930s; the Ga word refers to the illness of a child displaced from the breast by a new baby. It typically appears at one to three years after abrupt weaning onto starchy food, with oedema, moon face, "flaky-paint" skin, thin pale hair, fatty liver and apathy. Its cause is now seen as more than protein lack, involving infection and gut microbes.
  • Marasmus: severe wasting from overall starvation, usually in the first year: "skin and bones", an old-man face, no oedema, and an alert, hungry child.
  • Marasmic kwashiorkor: wasting together with oedema.

3. Causes and Indian examples

  • Poverty and food insecurity, large families and short birth intervals.
  • Cultural practices: late start of breastfeeding, discarding of colostrum, food taboos in pregnancy and illness, and less food for girls.
  • Infection: repeated diarrhoea and worms cut appetite and absorption, while malnutrition weakens immunity.
  • Examples: child deaths from severe malnutrition have been repeatedly reported among the Korku of Melghat (Maharashtra) and the Sahariya of Sheopur (Madhya Pradesh), where seasonal migration, loss of forest foods and distant health services add to the problem.

4. Response

  • ICDS (1975), Mission Saksham Anganwadi and Poshan 2.0, PM POSHAN school meals, nutrition rehabilitation centres for severe cases, fortified foods and community-based management of acute malnutrition.

Malnutrition is not merely a shortage of food but a biocultural condition; reducing it needs diverse diets, maternal care, sanitation and culturally sensitive feeding advice, as the slow fall in underweight and wasting in NFHS-6 shows.
