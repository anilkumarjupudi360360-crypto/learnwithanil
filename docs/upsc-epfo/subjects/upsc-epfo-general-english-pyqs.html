Sentence Arrangement

  1. APFC 2023 · Q1 · General English · Sentence Arrangement

    Rearrange the parts P, Q, R and S to form a meaningful sentence. P: before the judge Q: given that he was clearly innocent R: was incomprehensible S: his decision to plead guilty

    1. SQRP
    2. QPSR
    3. QSPR
    4. SRQP
    Show answer

    Answer: (3) QSPR

    The sentence is "Given that he was clearly innocent, his decision to plead guilty before the judge was incomprehensible." Q is an introductory clause ("given that" = considering that) that sets the context, S supplies the subject "his decision to plead guilty", P must follow it directly because one pleads guilty before a judge, and R "was incomprehensible" is the predicate. QPSR wrongly attaches "before the judge" to "innocent" instead of to "plead guilty", while SQRP and SRQP leave "before the judge" stranded at the end, where it attaches to "incomprehensible" or "innocent" and distorts the meaning.

  2. APFC 2023 · Q2 · General English · Sentence Arrangement

    Rearrange the parts P, Q, R and S to form a meaningful sentence. P: when your stated principles Q: it is difficult R: are at odds with your ground realities S: to practise what you preach

    1. PRSQ
    2. SPRQ
    3. QPRS
    4. QSPR
    Show answer

    Answer: (4) QSPR

    The sentence is "It is difficult to practise what you preach when your stated principles are at odds with your ground realities." Q is an anticipatory "it" clause and needs the infinitive S ("to practise what you preach") as its real subject immediately after it; the "when" clause then follows, with P giving its subject and R its verb. PRSQ and SPRQ end with a stranded "it is difficult", and QPRS separates "it is difficult" from the infinitive it introduces. To practise what you preach means to act as you advise others to act.

  3. APFC 2023 · Q3 · General English · Sentence Arrangement

    Rearrange the parts P, Q, R and S to form a meaningful sentence. P: people in dire need Q: it is a truth universally acknowledged that R: for assistance S: reach out to their friends and family

    1. QPSR
    2. PSRQ
    3. QRPS
    4. RQPS
    Show answer

    Answer: (1) QPSR

    The sentence is "It is a truth universally acknowledged that people in dire need reach out to their friends and family for assistance." Q is the main clause and ends in "that", so it must open the sentence and be followed by a full noun clause: P is its subject, S its verb ("reach out to their friends and family") and R completes it, since one reaches out to someone for assistance. PSRQ leaves "that" hanging at the end, while QRPS and RQPS detach "for assistance" from "reach out". The opening echoes the first line of Jane Austen's Pride and Prejudice.

  4. APFC 2023 · Q4 · General English · Sentence Arrangement

    Rearrange the parts P, Q, R and S to form a meaningful sentence. P: his vengeful Q: by the expression on his face R: and diabolical nature S: were clearly revealed

    1. QSPR
    2. QPRS
    3. PSQR
    4. PRSQ
    Show answer

    Answer: (4) PRSQ

    The sentence is "His vengeful and diabolical nature were clearly revealed by the expression on his face." P and R together form the subject (two adjectives joined by "and" before the noun "nature"), S is the passive verb, and Q is the agent phrase with "by", which naturally comes after a passive verb. PSQR splits the adjectives from "nature", and the options beginning with Q put the "by" phrase before the verb it depends on, which is unnatural. Vengeful means seeking revenge; diabolical means wicked or devilish. (Strictly, the singular "nature" takes "was", but the parts are fixed as printed.)

  5. APFC 2023 · Q5 · General English · Sentence Arrangement

    Rearrange the parts P, Q, R and S to form a meaningful sentence. P: bitter fights Q: between the two rival teams R: on more than one occasion S: I have witnessed

    1. SRQP
    2. RSPQ
    3. QPRS
    4. SQRP
    Show answer

    Answer: (2) RSPQ

    The sentence is "On more than one occasion I have witnessed bitter fights between the two rival teams." R is a fronted adverbial of frequency, S gives the subject and verb, P is the object, and Q must follow P directly because it tells us which fights ("fights between the two rival teams"). SRQP and SQRP both put "between the two rival teams" before "bitter fights", which it should follow, and QPRS opens with a phrase that has nothing to attach to. SPQR would also be correct, but it is not among the options.

  6. EO/AO 2023 · Q1 · General English · Sentence Arrangement

    The first and sixth sentences of a passage are given as S1 and S6. The four sentences in between have been jumbled up and labelled P, Q, R and S. Choose the proper sequence of the four sentences. S1: Geological mapping, when properly done, demands skill and judgment. S6: Nothing is more naive than to believe that a field geologist should gather only 'facts', the interpretation of which is to be made at a later date. P: While the field work progresses he should conceive as many interpretations as are consistent with the known facts. Q: As the field work progresses and the larger geological picture begins to unfold, experience and judgment are essential if the geologist is to evaluate properly the vast number of facts gathered from thousands of outcrops. R: Such mapping requires keen observation and a knowledge of what data are significant. S: Above all, the field geologist must use the method of "working multiple hypotheses" to deduce the geological structure.

    1. SRQP
    2. RQSP
    3. PSQR
    4. SRPQ
    Show answer

    Answer: (2) RQSP

    The order is RQSP. R must come first because "Such mapping" refers back to the geological mapping of S1 and spells out the skill it demands: keen observation and knowing which data matter. Q moves on to the progress of the field work, when experience and judgment are needed to evaluate the facts. S ("Above all") gives the most important requirement, the method of multiple hypotheses, and P explains it: "he", the field geologist of S, should form many interpretations while the work goes on. That leads into S6, which rejects gathering facts now and interpreting them later. Only RQSP begins with R.

  7. EO/AO 2023 · Q2 · General English · Sentence Arrangement

    The first and sixth sentences of a passage are given as S1 and S6. The four sentences in between have been jumbled up and labelled P, Q, R and S. Choose the proper sequence of the four sentences. S1: Under the colonial regime, basic infrastructure such as railways, ports, water transport, posts and telegraphs did develop. S6: Naturally, therefore, these areas remained inaccessible to the people. P: There always remained an acute shortage of all-weather roads to reach out to the rural areas during the rainy season. Q: Roads constructed in India prior to the advent of British rule were not fit for modern transport. R: However, the real motive behind this development was not necessarily to provide basic amenities to the people but to subserve various colonial interests. S: The roads that were built primarily served the purposes of mobilizing the army within India and drawing out raw materials from the countryside to the nearest railway station.

    1. SPQR
    2. PQSR
    3. QPRS
    4. RQSP
    Show answer

    Answer: (4) RQSP

    The order is RQSP. R comes first: "However" contrasts with S1's statement that infrastructure "did develop", and "this development" refers back to it before revealing the real colonial motive. Q and S narrow this down to roads: roads built before British rule were unfit for modern transport, and the roads the British built mainly served army movement and the carrying of raw materials to railway stations. P gives the result, a shortage of all-weather roads to rural areas, and S6's "these areas" means those rural areas, so P must come last. Only RQSP starts with R and ends with P.

  8. EO/AO 2023 · Q3 · General English · Sentence Arrangement

    The first and sixth sentences of a passage are given as S1 and S6. The four sentences in between have been jumbled up and labelled P, Q, R and S. Choose the proper sequence of the four sentences. S1: Contemporary history is necessary, but it is at best a tentative record. S6: Some portentous events can be seen to be so from the moment of their occurrence, like the discovery of the New World; others of comparable momentousness are hidden in obscurity. P: The relevance of the issue of proportion is significant. Q: It possesses, indeed, a massive supply of public record, but it necessarily lacks the inner knowledge of politicians' minds, of planners' proposals, of scientists' discoveries, of technicians' break-throughs. R: It also lacks perspective since it is bereft of a sense of proportion. S: It is impossible, over just a few years of observation, to estimate accurately the significance of various events.

    1. QPSR
    2. SRPQ
    3. QSRP
    4. PRSQ
    Show answer

    Answer: (3) QSRP

    The order is QSRP. Q follows S1 directly: "It" is contemporary history, and its "massive supply of public record" picks up S1's "tentative record" before saying what it lacks. S gives a second limitation: a few years of observation cannot show the real significance of events. R adds, with "also lacks", the lack of perspective and of a sense of proportion, and P can only come after R, because "the issue of proportion" needs R to introduce it. S6 then illustrates proportion with portentous events, some obvious at once and others hidden. QPSR wrongly puts P before proportion has been mentioned.

  9. EO/AO 2023 · Q4 · General English · Sentence Arrangement

    The first and sixth sentences of a passage are given as S1 and S6. The four sentences in between have been jumbled up and labelled P, Q, R and S. Choose the proper sequence of the four sentences. S1: Income inequality in less-developed countries is worse than most estimates. S6: The tax regime in these countries needs to be progressive and futuristic. P: There is a staggering level of difference between the income metrics and the actual income of the prosperous in such countries. Q: The effective tax rates are not really progressive with regard to income. R: They need to be reengineered to bring into the tax net the enormous sums of 'missing' income. S: They are even less so with regard to wealth.

    1. PQSR
    2. QSPR
    3. QSRP
    4. SRPQ
    Show answer

    Answer: (2) QSPR

    The keyed order is QSPR. Q and S are a fixed pair: "even less so" in S needs "not really progressive" in Q (on income, then wealth). P then points to the gap between measured income and the actual income of the prosperous, and R gives the remedy: reengineer the tax rates to bring this "missing" income into the tax net, as S6 sums up. SRPQ fails because S cannot open, and QSRP mentions the "missing" income before P explains it. PQSR, with P explaining S1's "worse than most estimates" and R's "They" directly after S's "They", reads at least as naturally. (Official key; some sources differ.)

  10. EO/AO 2023 · Q5 · General English · Sentence Arrangement

    The first and sixth sentences of a passage are given as S1 and S6. The four sentences in between have been jumbled up and labelled P, Q, R and S. Choose the proper sequence of the four sentences. S1: The history of English literature is usually divided into different periods. S6: This is the real danger of periodization, which tends to give the impression of distinct categories where there are none. P: However there are important discontinuities to be kept in mind. Q: Each period has unique features that produce certain family resemblances between the writers of a particular period. R: Scholars often specialize in periods such as Medieval, Renaissance, Romantic, Victorian, and Modern literature. S: Failure to detect these discontinuities often produce a false sense of uniformity and consensus within a period.

    1. PQRS
    2. RQPS
    3. PSQR
    4. RSPQ
    Show answer

    Answer: (2) RQPS

    The order is RQPS. R follows S1 by naming the usual periods (Medieval, Renaissance, Romantic, Victorian, Modern), and Q explains what holds a period together: features that produce family resemblances among its writers. P, opening with "However", turns to the discontinuities this view can hide, and S must follow P because "these discontinuities" refers back to them. S6's "This is the real danger of periodization" sums up the false sense of uniformity in S, so S comes last. PSQR keeps P and S together but separates S from S6. (In S, printed "produce" should strictly be "produces", agreeing with "Failure".)

Vocabulary & Idioms

  1. APFC 2023 · Q6 · General English · Vocabulary & Idioms

    Choose the word or phrase closest in meaning to the underlined word "platitudes". The essay was badly researched and contained several "platitudes" and generalizations.

    1. Incorrect statements
    2. Imprecise comments
    3. Often used and unoriginal ideas
    4. Vague, subjective statements
    Show answer

    Answer: (3) Often used and unoriginal ideas

    A platitude is a remark, often with a moral tone, that has been used so often that it is stale and unoriginal, for example "honesty is the best policy" in an essay that offers nothing new. That fits an essay that was badly researched. Platitudes are usually true, so "incorrect statements" is wrong. "Imprecise comments" and "vague, subjective statements" describe generalizations, which the sentence mentions separately. The key idea is triteness, not inaccuracy or vagueness. Related words: cliche, banality, truism.

  2. APFC 2023 · Q7 · General English · Vocabulary & Idioms

    Choose the word or phrase closest in meaning to the underlined word "disinterested". The judgment of beauty is based on the human capacity for "disinterested" pleasure in the natural world.

    1. Indifference
    2. Free of all self-interest
    3. Not caring for something
    4. Extremely focused
    Show answer

    Answer: (2) Free of all self-interest

    Disinterested means impartial: not influenced by personal advantage or any wish to own or use the thing. The sentence echoes Kant's idea in aesthetics that pleasure in beauty is "disinterested", enjoyed for its own sake and not because we want or gain anything from the object. The trap is to confuse "disinterested" with "uninterested", which means indifferent or not caring (options 1 and 3). A disinterested judge is fair, not bored. "Extremely focused" has no connection with the word.

  3. APFC 2023 · Q8 · General English · Vocabulary & Idioms

    Choose the word or phrase closest in meaning to the underlined word "derelict". The hundred-year old college building was now in a "derelict" state.

    1. Preserved for historical value
    2. Unsafe for occupation
    3. Neglected and broken down
    4. Retaining its original beauty
    Show answer

    Answer: (3) Neglected and broken down

    Derelict (of a building or property) means in very poor condition because it has been abandoned and neglected, that is, dilapidated or run-down, as in "a derelict factory". "Neglected and broken down" gives exactly both parts of this sense. A derelict building may well be unsafe to live in, but that is a consequence, not the meaning of the word. "Preserved for historical value" and "retaining its original beauty" are nearly the opposite. As a noun, a derelict is a homeless, destitute person.

  4. APFC 2023 · Q9 · General English · Vocabulary & Idioms

    Choose the word or phrase closest in meaning to the underlined word "serendipitous". My "serendipitous" encounter with the man on the bus was to have a profound impact on my life.

    1. Fortunate
    2. Of historical significance
    3. Sudden and abrupt
    4. A chance event
    Show answer

    Answer: (4) A chance event

    Serendipitous means happening or found by chance, usually in a happy or beneficial way. The essential idea is chance: the meeting on the bus was unplanned. "Fortunate" captures only the happy side and misses the element of accident, while the good effect is already stated separately ("a profound impact on my life"). "Sudden and abrupt" refers to speed, not chance, and "of historical significance" is unrelated. The word was coined by Horace Walpole in 1754 from the tale The Three Princes of Serendip (Serendip being Sri Lanka).

  5. APFC 2023 · Q10 · General English · Vocabulary & Idioms

    Choose the word or phrase closest in meaning to the underlined word "complacency". Notwithstanding our victory today we must be careful to avoid a feeling of "complacency".

    1. Smug and self-satisfied
    2. A defensive attitude
    3. Pride
    4. Feeling of superiority
    Show answer

    Answer: (1) Smug and self-satisfied

    Complacency is a smug, uncritical satisfaction with oneself or one's achievements, which makes a person relax and ignore dangers or the need to improve. After a victory the risk is exactly that: feeling self-satisfied and letting one's guard down, which is why the speaker says "notwithstanding our victory". "Pride" and "feeling of superiority" are related but lack the sense of contented carelessness, and "a defensive attitude" is unrelated. Do not confuse it with complaisance, which means willingness to please others.

  1. EO/AO 2023 · Q6 · General English · Grammar & Usage

    Find the part of the sentence that contains an error. If there is no error, choose "No error". Ramesh liked his friend's music player to his own.

    1. Ramesh liked
    2. his friend's music player
    3. to his own.
    4. No error
    Show answer

    Answer: (1) Ramesh liked

    The error is in part (1). The verb "like" cannot take a "to" phrase of comparison; the verb that does is "prefer", since one prefers one thing to another. The correct sentence is "Ramesh preferred his friend's music player to his own." Parts (2) and (3) are correct once the verb is changed: "his friend's music player" is the object, and "to his own" (to his own music player) completes the comparison. "Liked ... more than his own" would also be acceptable, but with "to" the sentence needs "preferred".

  2. EO/AO 2023 · Q7 · General English · Grammar & Usage

    Find the part of the sentence that contains an error. If there is no error, choose "No error". Given the complex of the problem the committee is not expected to arrive at a decision any time soon.

    1. Given the complex of the problem
    2. the committee is not expected
    3. to arrive at a decision any time soon.
    4. No error
    Show answer

    Answer: (1) Given the complex of the problem

    The error is in part (1). As a noun, "complex" means a group of connected buildings or parts (a sports complex) or a psychological fixation (an inferiority complex); it does not mean the quality of being complicated. The noun needed is "complexity": "Given the complexity of the problem, the committee is not expected to arrive at a decision any time soon." Part (2) is a correct passive ("is not expected"), and part (3) uses the right collocation "arrive at a decision" and the idiom "any time soon", meaning in the near future.

  3. EO/AO 2023 · Q8 · General English · Grammar & Usage

    Find the part of the sentence that contains an error. If there is no error, choose "No error". The group of students on a visit to Srinagar circumnavigated around the Dal Lake.

    1. The group of students
    2. on a visit to Srinagar
    3. circumnavigated around the Dal Lake.
    4. No error
    Show answer

    Answer: (3) circumnavigated around the Dal Lake.

    The error is in part (3). To circumnavigate means to sail, fly or travel all the way around something (Latin circum, around, and navigare, to sail), so "circumnavigated around" is a redundancy, like "return back" or "revert back". The correct sentence is "The group of students on a visit to Srinagar circumnavigated the Dal Lake" (or "went around the Dal Lake"). Parts (1) and (2) are correct: "The group of students" is the subject, and "on a visit to Srinagar" describes it.

  4. EO/AO 2023 · Q9 · General English · Grammar & Usage

    Find the part of the sentence that contains an error. If there is no error, choose "No error". Jitender was the smartest of the two children in the room.

    1. Jitender was the smartest
    2. of the two
    3. children in the room.
    4. No error
    Show answer

    Answer: (1) Jitender was the smartest

    The error is in part (1). When two persons or things are compared, the comparative degree is used, not the superlative: "Jitender was the smarter of the two children in the room." The superlative "smartest" is used for three or more, as in "the smartest of all the children". Note that "the" stays before the comparative in the pattern "the + comparative + of the two" (the taller of the two, the better of the two). Parts (2) and (3) are correct as they stand.
