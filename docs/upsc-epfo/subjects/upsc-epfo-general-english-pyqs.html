  1. APFC 2023 · Q1 · General English · Sentence Arrangement

    Rearrange the parts P, Q, R and S to form a meaningful sentence. P: before the judge Q: given that he was clearly innocent R: was incomprehensible S: his decision to plead guilty

    1. SQRP
    2. QPSR
    3. QSPR
    4. SRQP
    Show answer

    Answer: (3) QSPR

    Option 3, QSPR, gives "Given that he was clearly innocent, his decision to plead guilty before the judge was incomprehensible." Q opens the sentence: "given that" means "considering that" and introduces the background fact. S then supplies the subject, "his decision to plead guilty", and P must follow it at once, because a person pleads guilty before a judge. R, "was incomprehensible", is the predicate. QPSR (option 2) wrongly ties "before the judge" to "innocent" and separates it from "plead guilty". SQRP (option 1) pushes "before the judge" to the very end, where it seems to say the decision was incomprehensible before the judge, and SRQP (option 4) makes him "innocent before the judge", which distorts the meaning. Tip: in sentence-rearrangement questions, first find the opener (an introductory clause such as "given that" or the main subject), then keep every modifying phrase next to the word it describes; a misplaced modifier is the usual trap.

  2. APFC 2023 · Q2 · General English · Sentence Arrangement

    Rearrange the parts P, Q, R and S to form a meaningful sentence. P: when your stated principles Q: it is difficult R: are at odds with your ground realities S: to practise what you preach

    1. PRSQ
    2. SPRQ
    3. QPRS
    4. QSPR
    Show answer

    Answer: (4) QSPR

    Option 4, QSPR, gives "It is difficult to practise what you preach when your stated principles are at odds with your ground realities." Q uses the anticipatory (dummy) "it": the real subject is the infinitive phrase S, "to practise what you preach", so S must come straight after Q. The "when" clause follows, with P supplying its subject and R its verb and complement. PRSQ (option 1) and SPRQ (option 2) end with a dangling "it is difficult" that has nothing to refer to, and QPRS (option 3) separates "it is difficult" from the infinitive it introduces, leaving "to practise what you preach" stranded at the end. Meanings: to practise what you preach is to act as you advise others to act; "at odds with" means in conflict with. Spelling tip: the question uses British spelling, in which "practise" is the verb and "practice" the noun, just as "advise" is the verb and "advice" the noun.

  3. APFC 2023 · Q3 · General English · Sentence Arrangement

    Rearrange the parts P, Q, R and S to form a meaningful sentence. P: people in dire need Q: it is a truth universally acknowledged that R: for assistance S: reach out to their friends and family

    1. QPSR
    2. PSRQ
    3. QRPS
    4. RQPS
    Show answer

    Answer: (1) QPSR

    Option 1, QPSR, gives "It is a truth universally acknowledged that people in dire need reach out to their friends and family for assistance." Q is the main clause and ends in "that", so it must open the sentence and be followed by a complete noun clause. P supplies that clause's subject ("people in dire need"), S its verb ("reach out to their friends and family"), and R completes the idea, since one reaches out to someone for assistance. PSRQ (option 2) leaves "that" hanging at the end with nothing to introduce. QRPS (option 3) and RQPS (option 4) detach "for assistance" from "reach out" and place it where it modifies nothing. Meanings: "in dire need" means in urgent or desperate need, and "reach out to" means to contact someone, often to seek or offer help. The opening echoes the famous first line of Jane Austen's Pride and Prejudice (1813). Tip: a part ending in "that", "which" or "who" can never come last.

  4. APFC 2023 · Q4 · General English · Sentence Arrangement

    Rearrange the parts P, Q, R and S to form a meaningful sentence. P: his vengeful Q: by the expression on his face R: and diabolical nature S: were clearly revealed

    1. QSPR
    2. QPRS
    3. PSQR
    4. PRSQ
    Show answer

    Answer: (4) PRSQ

    Option 4, PRSQ, gives "His vengeful and diabolical nature were clearly revealed by the expression on his face." P and R together make the subject: two adjectives joined by "and" ("vengeful and diabolical") before the noun "nature". S is the passive verb, and Q is the agent phrase with "by", which normally follows a passive verb (subject + be + past participle + by + agent). PSQR (option 3) splits the adjectives from their noun and makes no sense. QSPR (option 1) and QPRS (option 2) put the "by" phrase first, ahead of the subject and verb it depends on; QSPR is a clumsy inversion, and neither follows the natural passive order that the parts point to. Meanings: vengeful means seeking revenge; diabolical means wicked or devilish (from the Greek word for devil). Strictly, the singular "nature" takes "was", not "were", but the parts are fixed as printed, so judge the order, not the agreement.

  5. APFC 2023 · Q5 · General English · Sentence Arrangement

    Rearrange the parts P, Q, R and S to form a meaningful sentence. P: bitter fights Q: between the two rival teams R: on more than one occasion S: I have witnessed

    1. SRQP
    2. RSPQ
    3. QPRS
    4. SQRP
    Show answer

    Answer: (2) RSPQ

    Option 2, RSPQ, gives "On more than one occasion I have witnessed bitter fights between the two rival teams." R is an adverbial of frequency placed at the front for emphasis; S gives the subject and verb ("I have witnessed"); P is the object ("bitter fights"); and Q must follow P directly because it tells us which fights, namely fights between the two rival teams. SRQP (option 1) and SQRP (option 4) place "between the two rival teams" before "bitter fights", separating the phrase from the noun it describes, and QPRS (option 3) opens with a prepositional phrase that has nothing to attach to and ends awkwardly with the verb. SPQR ("I have witnessed bitter fights between the two rival teams on more than one occasion") would also be correct, but it is not offered, which is why the version with the fronted adverbial is the answer. Tip: adverbials of time and frequency can move to the start or the end of a sentence, but a phrase that qualifies a noun must stay beside that noun.

  6. EO/AO 2023 · Q1 · General English · Sentence Arrangement

    The first and sixth sentences of a passage are given as S1 and S6. The four sentences in between have been jumbled up and labelled P, Q, R and S. Choose the proper sequence of the four sentences. S1: Geological mapping, when properly done, demands skill and judgment. S6: Nothing is more naive than to believe that a field geologist should gather only 'facts', the interpretation of which is to be made at a later date. P: While the field work progresses he should conceive as many interpretations as are consistent with the known facts. Q: As the field work progresses and the larger geological picture begins to unfold, experience and judgment are essential if the geologist is to evaluate properly the vast number of facts gathered from thousands of outcrops. R: Such mapping requires keen observation and a knowledge of what data are significant. S: Above all, the field geologist must use the method of "working multiple hypotheses" to deduce the geological structure.

    1. SRQP
    2. RQSP
    3. PSQR
    4. SRPQ
    Show answer

    Answer: (2) RQSP

    Option 2, RQSP, is correct. S1 says that geological mapping "demands skill and judgment", and R must follow at once because "Such mapping" points back to it and names the first skill needed: keen observation and knowing which data are significant. Q then moves the passage forward in time: "as the field work progresses", experience and judgment, the second word of S1, are needed to evaluate facts gathered from thousands of outcrops. S opens with "Above all", which signals the most important requirement of all, the method of "working multiple hypotheses". P explains that method, and its pronoun "he" needs "the field geologist" of S just before it. P's idea of forming many interpretations while the work goes on leads straight into S6, which mocks gathering only facts now and interpreting them later. Options 1 and 4 open with S, but "Above all" cannot begin a list of requirements, and option 3 opens with P, whose "he" has no clear antecedent. Tip: first look for pronouns and demonstratives ("such", "these", "he") and for linkers such as "Above all", which tie a sentence to the one before it.

  7. EO/AO 2023 · Q2 · General English · Sentence Arrangement

    The first and sixth sentences of a passage are given as S1 and S6. The four sentences in between have been jumbled up and labelled P, Q, R and S. Choose the proper sequence of the four sentences. S1: Under the colonial regime, basic infrastructure such as railways, ports, water transport, posts and telegraphs did develop. S6: Naturally, therefore, these areas remained inaccessible to the people. P: There always remained an acute shortage of all-weather roads to reach out to the rural areas during the rainy season. Q: Roads constructed in India prior to the advent of British rule were not fit for modern transport. R: However, the real motive behind this development was not necessarily to provide basic amenities to the people but to subserve various colonial interests. S: The roads that were built primarily served the purposes of mobilizing the army within India and drawing out raw materials from the countryside to the nearest railway station.

    1. SPQR
    2. PQSR
    3. QPRS
    4. RQSP
    Show answer

    Answer: (4) RQSP

    Option 4, RQSP, is correct. R must follow S1: "However" contrasts with S1's admission that infrastructure "did develop", and "this development" refers back to it before revealing the real motive, to serve colonial interests rather than the people. Q and S then narrow the discussion to roads: roads built before British rule were unfit for modern transport, and the roads the British built mainly moved the army and carried raw materials from the countryside to the nearest railway station. P gives the result, an acute shortage of all-weather roads to the rural areas in the rainy season, and S6's "these areas" can only mean those rural areas, so P must come last. Option 1 opens with S, whose "The roads that were built" has no context, option 2 opens with P and leaves R without a link to S1, and option 3 puts R in the middle and ends with S, so S6's "these areas" loses its referent. The passage echoes the NCERT account of the Indian economy on the eve of independence: railways (first train Bombay to Thane, 1853), ports and telegraphs were built mainly to serve British trade and administration.

  8. EO/AO 2023 · Q3 · General English · Sentence Arrangement

    The first and sixth sentences of a passage are given as S1 and S6. The four sentences in between have been jumbled up and labelled P, Q, R and S. Choose the proper sequence of the four sentences. S1: Contemporary history is necessary, but it is at best a tentative record. S6: Some portentous events can be seen to be so from the moment of their occurrence, like the discovery of the New World; others of comparable momentousness are hidden in obscurity. P: The relevance of the issue of proportion is significant. Q: It possesses, indeed, a massive supply of public record, but it necessarily lacks the inner knowledge of politicians' minds, of planners' proposals, of scientists' discoveries, of technicians' break-throughs. R: It also lacks perspective since it is bereft of a sense of proportion. S: It is impossible, over just a few years of observation, to estimate accurately the significance of various events.

    1. QPSR
    2. SRPQ
    3. QSRP
    4. PRSQ
    Show answer

    Answer: (3) QSRP

    Option 3, QSRP, is correct. Q follows S1 directly: "It" is contemporary history, and its "massive supply of public record", introduced with the concession "indeed", picks up S1's "tentative record" before the turn "but it necessarily lacks" the inner knowledge of politicians, planners and scientists. S gives a second limitation: over just a few years of observation, the true significance of events cannot be estimated accurately. R adds a third with "also lacks": contemporary history has no perspective because it has no sense of proportion. P can only come after R, because "the issue of proportion" needs R to introduce the word, and P's remark that proportion matters leads into S6, which illustrates it: some momentous events, like the discovery of the New World, are obvious at once, while others of equal weight stay hidden. Option 1 (QPSR) and option 4 (PRSQ) mention proportion before R introduces it, and option 2 (SRPQ) pushes Q to the end, although its "massive supply of public record" must answer S1 at once. Tip: words such as "also" and "the issue of" show that a sentence depends on an earlier one.

  9. EO/AO 2023 · Q4 · General English · Sentence Arrangement

    The first and sixth sentences of a passage are given as S1 and S6. The four sentences in between have been jumbled up and labelled P, Q, R and S. Choose the proper sequence of the four sentences. S1: Income inequality in less-developed countries is worse than most estimates. S6: The tax regime in these countries needs to be progressive and futuristic. P: There is a staggering level of difference between the income metrics and the actual income of the prosperous in such countries. Q: The effective tax rates are not really progressive with regard to income. R: They need to be reengineered to bring into the tax net the enormous sums of 'missing' income. S: They are even less so with regard to wealth.

    1. PQSR
    2. QSPR
    3. QSRP
    4. SRPQ
    Show answer

    Answer: (2) QSPR

    Option 2, QSPR, is the keyed order. Q and S form a fixed pair: S's "They are even less so with regard to wealth" needs Q's "not really progressive with regard to income" immediately before it, so tax rates are judged first on income and then, even more harshly, on wealth. P then points to the staggering gap between measured income and the actual income of the prosperous, and R gives the remedy: the tax rates must be reengineered to bring this "missing" income into the tax net, which S6 sums up as the need for a progressive and futuristic tax regime. Option 4 (SRPQ) fails because S cannot open the passage, and option 3 (QSRP) speaks of the "missing" income before P explains it. However, option 1 (PQSR), with P directly explaining S1's claim that inequality is "worse than most estimates" and R's "They" following S's "They", reads at least as naturally, which is why the key is debated. Concept: a tax is progressive when its rate rises as income or wealth rises, as with India's income tax slabs. (Official key; some sources differ.)

  10. EO/AO 2023 · Q5 · General English · Sentence Arrangement

    The first and sixth sentences of a passage are given as S1 and S6. The four sentences in between have been jumbled up and labelled P, Q, R and S. Choose the proper sequence of the four sentences. S1: The history of English literature is usually divided into different periods. S6: This is the real danger of periodization, which tends to give the impression of distinct categories where there are none. P: However there are important discontinuities to be kept in mind. Q: Each period has unique features that produce certain family resemblances between the writers of a particular period. R: Scholars often specialize in periods such as Medieval, Renaissance, Romantic, Victorian, and Modern literature. S: Failure to detect these discontinuities often produce a false sense of uniformity and consensus within a period.

    1. PQRS
    2. RQPS
    3. PSQR
    4. RSPQ
    Show answer

    Answer: (2) RQPS

    Option 2, RQPS, is correct. S1 says that the history of English literature is usually divided into periods, and R follows by naming the periods scholars specialise in: Medieval, Renaissance, Romantic, Victorian and Modern. Q explains what holds a period together: unique features that produce family resemblances among its writers. P, opening with "However", turns against this neat picture by warning of important discontinuities, and S must follow P because "these discontinuities" refers back to them. S then states the effect, a false sense of uniformity and consensus within a period, and S6's "This is the real danger of periodization" sums up exactly that, so S comes last. Options 1 and 3 open with P, but "However" needs a view to contrast with, which R and Q supply, and option 3 also separates S from S6. Option 4 (RSPQ) uses "these discontinuities" before P has mentioned them. Tip: a sentence beginning with "However" needs the idea it contradicts before it. Note that in S, the printed "produce" should strictly be "produces", agreeing with the singular subject "Failure".

  8. PA 2024 · Q5 · General English · Vocabulary & Idioms

    Consider the following: The teacher has become so stern that his popularity is on the wane. It means:

    1. The teacher's increasing popularity has made him more strict.
    2. The teacher's increasing strictness has made him more popular.
    3. The teacher's decreasing popularity has made him more strict.
    4. The teacher's increasing strictness has made him less popular.
    Show answer

    Answer: (4) The teacher's increasing strictness has made him less popular.

    Option 4 is correct. The idiom "on the wane" means decreasing or declining, like the moon in its waning phase; its opposite is "on the rise" or "waxing". In a "so ... that" sentence the "so" part gives the cause and the "that" clause gives the result: the teacher has become so stern (cause) that his popularity is declining (result). Put simply, his increasing strictness has made him less popular. Options 1 and 3 reverse cause and effect, making popularity the reason for his strictness, and option 1 also wrongly says his popularity is increasing. Option 2 keeps the right cause but turns the result around, saying he has become more popular. Related words: the verb "wane" means to grow weaker, as in "His influence waned after the election"; "dwindle", "ebb" and "decline" are near synonyms. Tip: in paraphrase questions, first identify the cause and the effect, then check the direction (more or less) of each before choosing an option.

  9. PA 2024 · Q6 · General English · Vocabulary & Idioms

    Consider the following: He bears his troubles bravely, never begs for sympathy, never yields to sorrow and never shows pain. What is the most appropriate word that describes him?

    1. Vivacious
    2. Stoical
    3. Magnanimous
    4. Effusive
    Show answer

    Answer: (2) Stoical

    Option 2, stoical, is correct. A stoical person endures pain and hardship patiently, without complaining and without showing his feelings, which matches the description exactly: he bears his troubles bravely, never begs for sympathy, never yields to sorrow and never shows pain. The word comes from the Stoics, philosophers of ancient Greece (the school was founded by Zeno of Citium) who taught calm acceptance of what cannot be changed. Option 1, "vivacious", means lively and full of spirit. Option 3, "magnanimous", means generous and forgiving, especially towards a rival or a defeated enemy. Option 4, "effusive", means expressing feelings in an unrestrained, gushing way, almost the opposite of stoical. Related words: "stoicism" (noun); "imperturbable" (not easily upset) and "phlegmatic" (calm and unemotional) are near synonyms, while "emotional" and "demonstrative" are antonyms. Remember: the stoic hides his pain; the effusive person pours out his feelings.

  10. PA 2024 · Q7 · General English · Vocabulary & Idioms

    Consider the following: "This boy takes after his father." What does it mean?

    1. This boy follows his father's advice.
    2. This boy steals from his father's wallet.
    3. This boy manages his father's affairs.
    4. This boy resembles his father.
    Show answer

    Answer: (4) This boy resembles his father.

    Option 4 is correct. The phrasal verb "take after" means to resemble an older member of one's family, usually a parent, in looks, character or behaviour, as in "She takes after her mother; both are musical." So "This boy takes after his father" means the boy resembles his father. Option 1 is wrong because following advice is "listen to" or "follow" someone. Option 2 confuses the idiom with "take" in the sense of stealing. Option 3 describes "look after" or "manage", not "take after". Examiners often set phrasal verbs with "take", so learn them as a set: "take after" (resemble), "take to" (begin to like), "take up" (start a hobby or job), "take over" (assume control), "take in" (deceive, or give shelter) and "take off" (remove clothing, or of an aircraft, leave the ground). Trap: do not confuse "take after" with "look after" (care for) or "go after" (chase).

Anthropomorphism is the broader idea of attributing human form or behaviour to animals, gods or objects, usually literally, as with talking animals in fables and cartoons; it is not the usual name of this figure of speech. There is no irony (saying the opposite of what is meant, or an outcome opposite to what is expected) and no antithesis (contrasting ideas in balanced phrases, as in "Man proposes, God disposes"). Strictly, using "Africa" for "Africans" is also metonymy (naming a thing by something closely associated with it, as "the Crown" for the monarch), but that is not offered, so personification is the best choice. Other examples of personification: "Time flies", "The wind howled".

    4. beautiful, soul-quenching and red

    Provisional answer (Learn with Anil; UPSC key not yet out): (4) beautiful, soul-quenching and red

    Option 4. Adjectives follow a broad order: opinion, size, age, shape, colour, origin, material, purpose. Opinion adjectives ("beautiful", "soul-quenching") therefore come before a fact adjective such as colour ("red"), which goes last; this rules out options 1 and 2. Between two opinion adjectives, the general one that can describe almost anything ("beautiful") comes before the more specific one ("soul-quenching", which describes the effect on the viewer), as in "a lovely, comfortable chair". Remember the mnemonic OSASCOMP (Opinion, Size, Age, Shape, Colour, Origin, Material, Purpose), as in "a lovely small old round red Italian wooden dining table". Note also that when a list of adjectives follows a linking verb such as "looked", "seemed" or "was", the last two are joined by "and", as here.

  2. APFC & EO/AO 2025 · Q7 · General English · Grammar & Usage

    Select the most appropriate option for the blank space. Summers ______ be really hot at times, so make sure you stay hydrated.

    1. can
    2. could
    3. might
    4. may
    Show answer

    Provisional answer (Learn with Anil; UPSC key not yet out): (1) can

    Option 1, "can". "Can" expresses general or theoretical possibility, that is, what is sometimes true of something: "Summers can be really hot at times, so make sure you stay hydrated." The phrase "at times" signals this typical, recurring possibility, and the advice that follows rests on it. "May" and "might" express possibility about a particular situation ("It may rain tomorrow") and sound odd in a general statement about summers. "Could" suggests past ability or a more remote, conditional possibility ("It could get hotter if the monsoon is late"). Compare "Snakes can be dangerous" (a general truth) with "That snake may be dangerous" (a guess about one case). Trap: all four modals express possibility, so look for the clue that tells you whether the sentence states a general truth or guesses about one occasion; general truths take "can".

  3. APFC & EO/AO 2025 · Q8 · General English · Grammar & Usage

    Select the most appropriate option for the blank space. I was wondering which festival ______ we celebrate together this year?

    1. will
    2. might
    3. shall
    4. do
    Show answer

    Provisional answer (Learn with Anil; UPSC key not yet out): (3) shall

    Option 3, "shall". "Shall" is used with "I" and "we" in questions that ask for a suggestion, an offer or a decision: "Which festival shall we celebrate together this year?", like "Shall we go?" or "What shall I wear?" The speaker is wondering what the two of them should choose, not predicting a fact, so "shall" fits best. "Will we celebrate" asks about a future fact, "might" weakens it into a vague possibility, and "do we celebrate" is the simple present used for habits and routines, which does not fit a choice to be made "this year". Remember: in modern English "will" has largely replaced "shall" for plain future time, but "Shall I/we ...?" survives for offers and suggestions. In legal drafting, "shall" expresses an obligation ("The employer shall pay ..."), which is how statutes such as the Employees' Provident Funds and Miscellaneous Provisions Act, 1952 impose duties.

    1. He asked me if I would be
    2. interested in signing up
    3. as a member of the group.
    4. No error
    Show answer

    Answer: (4) No error

    Option 4, "No error", is correct, because every part of the sentence is grammatical. It reports a yes/no question whose direct form is "Would you be interested in signing up as a member of the group?" Such questions are reported with "if" or "whether", followed by normal statement word order ("if I would be", not "if would I be"), and without a question mark. The pronoun changes from "you" to "I", and "would" is correct after the past reporting verb "asked". In part 2, "interested in" takes the right preposition and is followed by the gerund "signing up", since a verb after a preposition takes the -ing form. In part 3, "sign up as a member of" is an idiomatic way of saying to enrol in or join a group, just as one signs up for a course. Rules to remember for reported questions: no inversion, no auxiliary "do" ("He asked where I lived", not "where did I live"), and a backshift of tense after a past reporting verb. Trap: do not mark a part as wrong just because "if" looks informal; "if" and "whether" are both acceptable here.

  45. EO/AO 2017 · Q7 · General English · Grammar & Usage

    Which one of the following parts of the sentence, "As you know that the ignorant are easily duped", has an error?

    1. As you know
    2. that the ignorant
    3. are easily duped
    4. No error
    Show answer

    Answer by Learn with Anil (no official key): (2) that the ignorant

    Option 2 is the answer adopted here: the error lies in the superfluous "that" of part (b). The sentence mixes two constructions. "As you know" is a comment clause; it should be followed by a comma and the main clause: "As you know, the ignorant are easily duped." Without "As", the correct form would be "You know that the ignorant are easily duped", where "that" introduces a noun clause as the object of "know". Using both "As" and "that" leaves the sentence without a main clause. Deleting "As" in part (a) would also correct the sentence, which makes the question debatable, but the usual correction removes "that". Part (c), "are easily duped", is correct: "the ignorant" (the + adjective) means ignorant people as a class and takes a plural verb, like "the rich", "the poor" and "the blind". "To dupe" means to deceive or trick. Option 4, no error, is wrong because the sentence as written is ungrammatical. (No official key; some sources differ.)

  46. EO/AO 2017 · Q8 · General English · Grammar & Usage

    Which one of the following parts of the sentence, "After a lot of argument I brought him forward to my point of view", has an error?

    1. After a lot of argument
    2. I brought him forward
    3. to my point of view
    4. No error
    Show answer

    Answer by Learn with Anil (no official key): (2) I brought him forward

    Option 2 is correct: the error is "I brought him forward". The phrasal verb for persuading someone to accept an opinion is "bring someone round (or around) to" something: "After a lot of argument I brought him round to my point of view." Its intransitive partner is "come round to": "He finally came round to my view." "Bring forward" means to move an event to an earlier date ("The meeting was brought forward to Monday") or to present a proposal, plan or evidence for discussion ("The minister brought forward a new bill"); it cannot mean persuade, so part (b) is wrong. Part (a), "After a lot of argument", is correct, since "argument" in the sense of discussion and debate can be uncountable after "a lot of". Part (c), "to my point of view", is the right preposition after "bring round". Option 4, no error, is therefore wrong. Other "bring" phrasal verbs often tested: bring about (cause), bring up (raise a child or mention a topic), bring out (publish or reveal) and bring down (reduce or topple).

  47. EO/AO 2017 · Q9 · General English · Grammar & Usage

    Which one of the following parts of the sentence, "Don't ask him how he is because if he starts talking about his health you'll never get off from him", has an error?

    1. Don't ask him how he is because
    2. if he starts talking about his health you'll never
    3. get off from him
    4. No error
    Show answer

    Answer by Learn with Anil (no official key): (3) get off from him

    Option 3 is correct: the error is "get off from him". The intended meaning is that once he starts talking about his health you will never be able to escape from him, and the phrasal verb for that is "get away from" someone: "you'll never get away from him." "Get away" means to escape or leave a person or place, as in "I couldn't get away from the office until eight." "Get off" has other senses: to leave a bus or train, to escape punishment ("He got off with a fine"), or to stop touching someone ("Get off me!"). "Get off from him" is not idiomatic in the sense of escaping a talkative person, so part (c) has the error. Part (a), "Don't ask him how he is because", correctly uses an indirect question with statement word order ("how he is", not "how is he"). Part (b), "if he starts talking about his health you'll never", correctly uses the present simple in the if-clause with "will" in the main clause (first conditional). So option 4, no error, is wrong.
