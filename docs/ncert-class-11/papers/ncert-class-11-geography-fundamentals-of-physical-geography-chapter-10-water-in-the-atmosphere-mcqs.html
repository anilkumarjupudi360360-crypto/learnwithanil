  1. Q1 · Geography · Fundamentals of Physical Geography

    Water vapour in the atmosphere varies from zero to about what share of its volume?

    1. 1 per cent
    2. 4 per cent
    3. 10 per cent
    4. 21 per cent
    Show answer

    Answer: (2) 4 per cent

    The chapter gives a range of zero to four per cent by volume. Even this small amount plays a big role in weather.

  2. Q2 · Geography · Fundamentals of Physical Geography

    The actual amount of water vapour present in the atmosphere, expressed as weight per unit volume of air (grams per cubic metre), is called:

    1. Relative humidity
    2. Absolute humidity
    3. Dew point
    4. Saturation
    Show answer

    Answer: (2) Absolute humidity

    Absolute humidity is the actual weight of water vapour in a given volume of air. Relative humidity compares this with what the air can hold at that temperature.

  3. Q3 · Geography · Fundamentals of Physical Geography

    Consider the following statements about relative humidity: 1. It is the percentage of moisture present in the air compared with its full capacity at a given temperature. 2. It is greater over the oceans and least over the continents. Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

    1. 1 only
    2. 2 only
    3. Both 1 and 2
    4. Neither 1 nor 2
    Show answer

    Answer: (3) Both 1 and 2

    Relative humidity changes with temperature because warmer air can hold more moisture. It is high over oceans and low over continents.

  4. Q4 · Geography · Fundamentals of Physical Geography

    The temperature at which saturation occurs in a given sample of air is called the:

    1. Boiling point
    2. Dew point
    3. Freezing point
    4. Lapse rate
    Show answer

    Answer: (2) Dew point

    When air cools to its dew point it becomes saturated and cannot hold more moisture, so condensation begins.

  5. Q5 · Geography · Fundamentals of Physical Geography

    Condensation of water vapour is caused mainly by:

    1. Loss of heat from the air
    2. Increase in the air temperature
    3. Decrease in relative humidity
    4. Increase in wind speed alone
    Show answer

    Answer: (1) Loss of heat from the air

    Cooling brings air to saturation. Condensation depends on how much and how fast the air cools, and on its relative humidity.

  6. Q6 · Geography · Fundamentals of Physical Geography

    Which set of conditions is ideal for the formation of dew?

    1. Cloudy sky, strong winds and low humidity
    2. Heavy rainfall and high temperature
    3. Hot afternoons with dust storms
    4. Clear sky, calm air, high relative humidity and cold, long nights
    Show answer

    Answer: (4) Clear sky, calm air, high relative humidity and cold, long nights

    Dew forms when moisture is deposited as droplets on cooler surfaces of solid objects such as stones, grass blades and leaves. For dew, the dew point must be above freezing point.

  7. Q7 · Geography · Fundamentals of Physical Geography

    Frost forms on cold surfaces when:

    1. Condensation takes place below the freezing point, with the dew point at or below 0°C
    2. Condensation takes place above the freezing point
    3. Rain falls on a hot surface
    4. Air is completely dry
    Show answer

    Answer: (1) Condensation takes place below the freezing point, with the dew point at or below 0°C

    When the dew point is at or below freezing, the excess moisture is deposited as minute ice crystals instead of water droplets.

  8. Q8 · Geography · Fundamentals of Physical Geography

    A fog mixed with smoke, common in urban and industrial centres, is called:

    1. Mist
    2. Frost
    3. Haze
    4. Smog
    Show answer

    Answer: (4) Smog

    Fog is a cloud with its base at or very near the ground. In cities and industrial areas smoke mixes with fog to form smog.

  9. Q9 · Geography · Fundamentals of Physical Geography

    How does mist differ from fog, according to the chapter?

    1. Mist contains more moisture; each nucleus has a thicker layer of moisture
    2. Mist forms only over deserts
    3. Mist is drier than fog
    4. Mist forms only above 5,000 m
    Show answer

    Answer: (1) Mist contains more moisture; each nucleus has a thicker layer of moisture

    In mist each nucleus carries a thicker layer of moisture. Mists are frequent over mountains, where rising warm air meets cold surfaces; fogs are drier.

  10. Q10 · Geography · Fundamentals of Physical Geography

    Which clouds form at high altitudes of 8,000–12,000 m, are thin and detached with a feathery appearance, and are always white?

    1. Cumulus
    2. Stratus
    3. Cirrus
    4. Nimbus
    Show answer

    Answer: (3) Cirrus

    Cirrus clouds are the high, wispy clouds. Cumulus clouds look like cotton wool and generally form at 4,000–7,000 m.
